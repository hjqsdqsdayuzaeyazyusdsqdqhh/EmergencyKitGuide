Emergency Kit New York: Urban and Coastal Disaster Preparedness

New York emergency kit guide covering hurricanes, nor'easters, urban emergencies, flooding, and extreme cold. NYC and upstate preparedness strategies.

Last updated: July 25, 2025By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

New York's Complex Disaster Threat Landscape

New York State faces a remarkably diverse range of natural and human-caused disasters that vary significantly between its downstate urban centers and its rural upstate regions. The state stretches from the Atlantic coast and Long Island Sound to the Great Lakes and the Canadian border, creating vastly different risk profiles for its nearly 20 million residents. New York City and the surrounding metropolitan area face coastal flooding from hurricanes and nor'easters, extreme cold, urban infrastructure failures, and the unique challenges of managing emergencies in one of the world's most densely populated urban environments with high-rise buildings, underground transit systems, and millions of commuters.

Upstate New York contends with heavy lake-effect snow from Lake Ontario and Lake Erie, severe ice storms that can leave communities without power for weeks, river flooding along the Susquehanna and Mohawk valleys, and tornadoes during severe weather events. Hurricane Sandy in 2012 was a defining moment for New York preparedness, demonstrating that even a state far from the tropics can suffer catastrophic damage from tropical cyclones. Sandy's storm surge flooded subway tunnels, knocked out power to lower Manhattan for days, and destroyed entire neighborhoods in Staten Island, the Rockaways, and Long Island. The lessons learned from Sandy continue to shape New York's emergency preparedness framework today.

Nor'easters and Blizzard Preparedness

Nor'easters are the most frequent and disruptive weather events in New York, occurring multiple times each winter season from November through March. These powerful extratropical storms draw moisture from the Atlantic Ocean and collide with cold Arctic air to produce heavy snow, freezing rain, sleet, and damaging winds along the coast. Major nor'easters can dump two feet or more of snow across the state, with drifts reaching several feet in exposed areas. The blizzard of 2016 brought over 27 inches to parts of New York City and shut down the entire transit system. These storms cause widespread power outages, road closures, and roof collapses under heavy snow loads, making thorough preparation essential.

Stock at least seven days of food and water for every household member before winter storm season begins. Ensure you have an adequate supply of batteries, flashlights, and a battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio. Keep rock salt, sand, or ice melt for walkways and driveways, and maintain snow removal equipment in good working order. If you rely on electric heat, have a backup heating source such as a vented kerosene heater or a properly installed wood-burning stove, but never use a gas oven, charcoal grill, or camp stove indoors due to carbon monoxide poisoning risk. Insulate exposed water pipes and know how to shut off your water main to prevent burst pipe damage. For a comprehensive winter survival kit checklist, review our 72-hour survival kit guide.

Hurricane and Coastal Storm Preparedness

While New York does not experience hurricane landfalls as frequently as southeastern states, the threat from tropical cyclones is real and well-documented. Hurricane Sandy in 2012 proved that even a Category 1 hurricane that makes landfall in New Jersey can produce devastating storm surge in New York City and Long Island. Storm surge of nine feet flooded subway tunnels, destroyed electrical infrastructure, and left thousands of residents homeless. New York City has since designated six evacuation zones (Zone 1 through Zone 6) ranked by storm surge vulnerability, with Zone 1 being the most at risk. Residents in these zones, as well as those in coastal areas of Long Island, Westchester, and the Hudson Valley, should have evacuation plans and go-bags ready during Atlantic hurricane season from June through November.

New York's coastal flooding risk also extends to nor'easters, which can produce storm surge comparable to a hurricane when they coincide with high astronomical tides. The January 2016 nor'easter produced record storm surge in parts of Long Island and coastal Connecticut. Sign up for your county's emergency notification system and the NY-Alert mass notification system to receive immediate evacuation orders. Keep waterproof dry bags for important documents and electronics, and maintain at least seven days of emergency supplies including water, food, medications, and batteries. Urban Emergency Preparedness for New York City

Emergency preparedness in New York City presents unique challenges that suburban and rural residents do not face. High-rise residents must plan for extended stairwell evacuations if elevators are disabled, with some buildings requiring residents to walk down 30 or more flights to reach street level. Store a go-bag near your apartment door with comfortable walking shoes, a flashlight, portable phone charger, medications, and essential documents. Know how many flights of stairs separate your floor from the street and plan for family members who may have mobility limitations. Many NYC buildings have designated stairwells and evacuation assembly areas that you should identify in advance.

NYC apartment residents face additional constraints including limited storage space, restrictions on portable generators and grills in buildings, and dependence on an aging infrastructure of steam heat, electrical grids, and subway systems that can fail during severe weather. The subway system is particularly vulnerable to flooding, as demonstrated during Sandy when salt water inundated tunnels and destroyed signaling equipment. During major storms, expect subway and commuter rail service suspensions that can leave you stranded far from home. Keep an emergency kit both at home and at your workplace, and carry a small survival kit in your backpack or briefcase daily. The NYC Emergency Management website provides borough-specific hazard information and evacuation zone maps. For apartment-specific emergency planning, consult our home emergency kit guide.

Extreme Cold, Ice Storms, and Winter Power Outages

New York experiences bitterly cold winters with temperatures regularly dropping below zero in upstate regions and single digits in New York City. Ice storms are a particularly dangerous hazard, as freezing rain coats trees, power lines, and roads in a layer of ice that can topple trees and down power lines across wide areas. The 2008 ice storm in the greater Albany area left over 500,000 customers without power for up to two weeks in sub-freezing temperatures. Extended winter power outages in New York can be life-threatening, as homes without heat can reach dangerously cold indoor temperatures within hours.

Build a winter power outage kit that includes sleeping bags or blankets rated to at least zero degrees Fahrenheit, extra layers of warm clothing including thermal underwear, wool socks, and insulated boots, and a safe indoor heating source. Never use a gas stove, oven, or charcoal grill for indoor heating as carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. Install battery-operated carbon monoxide detectors on every level of your home and test them monthly. Stock a supply of heating fuel such as kerosene, propane, or seasoned firewood if you have a fireplace. Fill your bathtub with water before an ice storm to ensure you can flush toilets if water pressure drops. ConEdison and NYSEG provide outage maps and storm preparation guides for downstate and upstate customers respectively. Lake-Effect Snow in Upstate New York

Upstate New York, particularly the Tug Hill Plateau, the ski regions near Syracuse, and the Buffalo metropolitan area, experience some of the heaviest lake-effect snow in the United States. Lake-effect snow bands originating from Lake Ontario and Lake Erie can dump two to four feet of snow in narrow bands within a 24-hour period, burying communities and isolating residents for days. The city of Buffalo experienced devastating lake-effect storms in November 2014 that dumped over seven feet of snow in some areas, killing dozens and trapping thousands in their homes and vehicles. Lake-effect snow can develop rapidly and with little advance warning, making year-round preparedness essential for residents of these regions.

Maintain an extended supply of food and water for at least 14 days during lake-effect season from November through March, as road access may be cut off for several days after heavy events. Ensure your heating fuel supply is fully stocked before the season begins and identify neighbors who may need assistance during prolonged snow events. Keep snow removal equipment maintained and store sand or salt for walkways. If you commute, keep a winter survival kit in your vehicle including blankets, food, water, a shovel, and jumper cables. Never leave your vehicle during a lake-effect snow event if you become stranded, as whiteout conditions make it nearly impossible to see and navigate safely. Flooding, Heat Waves, and Seasonal Hazards

New York experiences river and flash flooding during spring snowmelt, heavy summer thunderstorms, and tropical system remnants. The Susquehanna River Valley and Mohawk Valley are particularly flood-prone, with major flood events occurring regularly. Urban flooding in New York City is an increasing concern as aging storm drains and combined sewer systems are overwhelmed by intense rainfall events that are becoming more frequent with changing climate patterns. Basements, subway stations, and underground roadways can flood within minutes during heavy downpours, trapping commuters and residents.

New York summers also bring extreme heat events that can be particularly dangerous in urban areas where the heat island effect elevates temperatures several degrees above surrounding rural areas. Elderly residents, people without air conditioning, and those living on upper floors of walk-up buildings are at the highest risk during heat waves. Keep electrolyte packets, cooling towels, and a battery-powered fan in your emergency kit for summer events. Identify the nearest cooling center through your city or county's emergency management office. Sign up for NY-Alert at alerts.ny.gov to receive notifications about severe weather, flooding, and other emergency situations affecting your specific location. Evacuation Planning and Emergency Resources

Effective evacuation planning in New York requires addressing both the urban complexity of the downstate region and the rural challenges of upstate areas. In New York City, evacuation zones are designated by neighborhood and borough, with specific evacuation routes and public shelter locations identified by NYC Emergency Management. Use the Know Your Zone tool at nyc.gov to find your zone and nearest evacuation routes. Public transit is often used for evacuation in NYC, with buses deployed along designated routes when subway service is suspended. For suburban Long Island and Westchester residents, evacuation typically involves traveling inland on major highways that become severely congested during storms.

Upstate residents face different evacuation challenges, including limited road access in rural areas that may be cut off by fallen trees, ice, or flooding. Identify at least two evacuation routes from your home and community, and know the location of the nearest emergency shelter. Register with your county's emergency notification system to receive immediate alerts about evacuation orders. Keep your vehicle fueled to at least half full during winter storm season and store a go-bag in the trunk with emergency supplies. Coordinate with neighbors, particularly elderly or disabled residents who may need transportation assistance during emergencies. Frequently Asked Questions

What disasters does New York face most often?
New York faces nor'easters and blizzards that can dump several feet of snow, coastal hurricanes and tropical storms that cause storm surge flooding, extreme cold and ice storms in winter, severe thunderstorms and tornadoes in warmer months, flooding along rivers and in urban areas, and heat waves during summer. New York City faces unique urban emergency risks including subway flooding, high-rise evacuations, and widespread power outages.
How do I prepare for a nor'easter in New York?
Stock at least seven days of food and water, extra blankets and warm clothing, batteries and flashlights, a NOAA weather radio, and rock salt or sand for icy walkways. Fill your car with gas and charge all devices before the storm. If you rely on electric heat, have a backup heating source. Trim tree branches near power lines before winter storm season to reduce outage risk.
What should New York City apartment residents include in their emergency kits?
NYC apartment residents should focus on compact, portable supplies that fit limited storage space. Include a go-bag with one day of supplies, portable water purification, battery-powered lights, a power bank, important documents, and comfortable walking shoes. Know the stairwell routes to street level from your floor and identify the nearest public shelter. Store supplies on an upper shelf in case of basement flooding.
How do I prepare for power outages during a New York winter?
Stock extra blankets, sleeping bags, warm layered clothing, and a safe backup heating source that can be used with proper ventilation. Never use a gas stove or oven for indoor heating. Keep a NOAA weather radio with batteries, charge power banks fully before a storm, and fill your bathtub with water for flushing toilets if you lose water pressure. ConEdison and NYSEG provide outage maps and alerts you should register for.
Do I need to prepare for hurricanes in New York?
Yes. Hurricane Sandy in 2012 demonstrated that New York is vulnerable to hurricane storm surge, particularly in low-lying coastal areas of New York City, Long Island, and the Hudson Valley. While direct hurricane hits are less frequent than in the Southeast, tropical storms and their remnants can produce dangerous flooding and extended power outages. Residents within evacuation zones should have go-bags ready and know their evacuation routes.
What is NY-Alert and how do I sign up?
NY-Alert is New York State free emergency notification system that sends alerts via text, email, phone calls, and social media about severe weather, missing persons, and other emergency situations. Sign up at alerts.ny.gov and select your county to receive location-specific notifications. NY-Alert is coordinated by the Division of Homeland Security and Emergency Services and works alongside local emergency notification systems.
How do I prepare for lake-effect snow in upstate New York?
Lake-effect snow bands from Lake Ontario and Lake Erie can dump multiple feet of snow in narrow bands across western and central New York within hours. Stock extra food and water for up to two weeks, ensure you have adequate heating fuel, keep snow removal equipment maintained, and avoid travel during lake-effect snow warnings. Rural residents may lose road access for days, so maintain extended supply levels during the lake-effect season from November through March.
