Best Family Emergency Kits: Complete Household Survival Kits

Find the best family emergency kits for complete household preparedness. Compare kits for families with children, seniors, and pets.

Last updated: July 25, 2025By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Families Need Dedicated Emergency Kits

Families face unique emergency preparedness challenges that individual kits cannot address. A household with two adults, three children, a senior grandparent, and a family dog has vastly different supply requirements than a single adult preparing for emergencies alone. The quantity of food, water, and medical supplies must scale to cover every member. The variety of items must accommodate different ages, health conditions, dietary needs, and comfort requirements. And the communication and evacuation plans must account for the logistical reality of moving multiple people, potentially including those with limited mobility, to safety quickly.

Despite these complexities, many families either have no emergency supplies at all or rely on a single inadequate kit that was designed for individual use. This guide addresses the specific needs of family emergency preparedness, from calculating supply quantities for your household size to selecting age-appropriate items for children, seniors, and pets. We explain how to structure your family's emergency supplies using a shared-plus-individual approach, provide guidance on creating and practicing family communication plans, and compare the features that distinguish effective family emergency kits from those that fall short. Calculating Supply Quantities for Your Household

The first step in selecting a family emergency kit is calculating exactly how many supplies your household requires. Water is the easiest to calculate: one gallon per person per day, multiplied by the number of family members, multiplied by the number of days you want to prepare for. A family of five planning for three days needs a minimum of 15 gallons of water. For two weeks of preparedness, that figure rises to 70 gallons. These quantities are typically stored in bulk containers at home rather than packed into portable bags, but they must be factored into your overall preparedness budget and storage planning.

Food calculations follow a similar approach but require adjustments for age and activity level. Adults generally need 2,000 to 2,500 calories per day, while children need between 1,200 and 2,000 calories depending on their age and size. Infants require formula or baby food rather than standard emergency rations. Seniors may need fewer calories but require specific dietary considerations such as softer textures or low-sodium options. Create a household supply spreadsheet that lists each family member's daily requirements for food, water, medications, and any specialty items, then multiply by your target preparation period. This data-driven approach ensures you do not under-prepare for your family's actual needs. For detailed calorie planning, see our 72-hour emergency food list.

Baby and Infant Considerations

Infants and babies require supplies that are entirely different from what adults need, and these items must be planned for separately. Formula-fed babies need a minimum three-day supply of formula, clean bottles, nipples, and sterile water for mixing. Breastfeeding mothers should store a manual breast pump and a small supply of stored breast milk in an insulated bag with ice packs. Diapers, wipes, diaper cream, and a portable changing pad are essentials that are easy to overlook when focusing on survival-oriented supplies. Pack infant-safe over-the-counter medications like acetaminophen drops for fever management.

Beyond basic needs, infant comfort items reduce anxiety for both baby and parents during stressful emergencies. A favorite pacifier, a small blanket, and a familiar stuffed animal provide comfort in unfamiliar environments. A portable travel crib or pack-and-play ensures safe sleep in evacuation shelters or temporary housing. A baby carrier keeps your hands free while holding your infant, which is critical during evacuation on foot. Pack extra clothing in multiple sizes since babies grow quickly and you may not have time to gather clothes before leaving. Our emergency kit for babies guide provides complete infant preparedness recommendations.

School-Age Children and Teenagers

Children of school age represent a significant portion of family emergency supply needs and require age-appropriate planning. Pack familiar foods that your children actually eat rather than assuming they will accept unfamiliar emergency rations. Comfort items such as a favorite book, a deck of cards, coloring supplies, or a small game reduce stress and anxiety during what can be a frightening experience. Include a written card with the family emergency contact information, meeting point, and out-of-area contact in each child's individual bag. Teach young children their full name, your phone number, and the family meeting point as early as possible.

Teenagers warrant special attention because they may be driving independently or may be away from home when an emergency strikes. Ensure your teen has their own vehicle emergency kit if they drive, and establish communication protocols that account for cell network congestion during large-scale events. Teenagers can carry heavier individual packs than younger children, so their go-bags can include more comprehensive supplies. Include a small amount of cash in each teenager's bag for emergency purchases. Our teen driver emergency kit guide addresses the specific needs of teen motorists.

Senior and Elderly Family Members

Elderly family members often have the most complex supply requirements in a household. Prescription medications are the highest priority: maintain at least a seven-day supply of every medication your senior family member takes, along with a written list of drug names, dosages, prescribing physicians, and pharmacy contact information. Include backup eyeglasses, hearing aid batteries, and any assistive devices they depend on daily such as a cane, walker, or wheelchair. Seniors are also more vulnerable to temperature extremes, so pack additional thermal blankets and hand warmers for cold weather, or extra water and sun protection for warm climates.

Dietary needs for seniors may differ significantly from the rest of the family. Include soft foods, nutritional supplement drinks, and low-sodium options for family members with difficulty chewing or dietary restrictions. Medical alert information should be prominently displayed on a card attached to the senior's go-bag or worn as a medical alert bracelet. Practice your evacuation plan with elderly family members to identify mobility challenges and ensure your plan accounts for their pace and limitations. If a senior requires medical equipment like a CPAP machine, include a portable battery backup or power station capable of running the device. Our emergency kit for seniors guide provides comprehensive elderly preparedness recommendations.

Pet Supplies and Animal Preparedness

Pets are family members who cannot prepare for themselves, making their inclusion in your family emergency kit both a moral responsibility and a practical necessity. Pack at least a three-day supply of your pet's regular food in an airtight, waterproof container. Include collapsible water bowls, a manual can opener if you feed canned food, a leash with identification tags, a harness, and a sturdy carrier large enough for your pet to stand and turn around in. Copies of vaccination records, a recent photo of your pet, and a list of pet-friendly evacuation shelters in your area should be stored in a waterproof bag.

Many emergency shelters do not accept animals, so identifying pet-friendly alternatives in advance is essential. Research hotels along your evacuation route that accept pets, locate friends or family members outside your area who could house your animals, and consider boarding facilities that have emergency protocols. Include any medications your pet requires, waste bags for cleanup, a familiar blanket or toy to reduce anxiety, and a waterproof container for food storage. For households with multiple pets, calculate supply quantities for each animal individually. Our family emergency plan guide covers pet evacuation planning as part of comprehensive household preparedness.

Shared vs. Individual Family Bags

The most effective family emergency kit strategy uses a layered approach combining shared bulk supplies with individual grab-and-go bags for each family member. The shared kit, stored in an accessible location like a hall closet or garage shelf, contains heavy bulk items that serve everyone: large water containers, bulk food supplies, cooking equipment, tools, comprehensive first aid supplies, and backup power equipment. These items are too heavy and bulky for individual packs but form the backbone of your family's preparedness infrastructure.

Individual bags contain personal items that vary by family member: prescription medications, a change of clothes, comfortable shoes, personal hygiene products, a small flashlight, identification documents, and personal comfort items. Each bag should be lightweight enough for the intended person to carry comfortably. Color-code bags by family member using colored tape or bag tags for quick identification in a crisis. Position individual bags near the primary exit for rapid grab-and-go evacuation. This dual approach ensures that if your family must evacuate quickly, everyone has personal essentials in hand while shared supplies are loaded into the vehicle if time permits. For more detailed packing strategies, see our 72-hour kit guide.

Storage, Rotation, and Maintenance

Family emergency supplies require more frequent maintenance than individual kits because the variety of items and the rate at which family needs change are both higher. Children outgrow clothing and shoes, medications expire, food needs rotation, and family composition changes with new births, aging, and other life events. Establish a systematic review schedule, ideally every six months, that covers every category of your family's emergency supplies. Assign specific review tasks to different family members so the process is shared and everyone stays familiar with what is stored and where.

Storage location matters for families with young children. Keep emergency supplies accessible but out of reach of curious toddlers who might tamper with medications, fire-starting equipment, or sharp tools. A high shelf in a locked closet or a latched storage container balances accessibility with safety. Store water and food in cool, dry locations away from chemicals and direct sunlight. Keep a waterproof document bag with family identification papers, insurance documents, birth certificates, and emergency contact lists in an easily grabbable location. Update this document bag whenever any family information changes. Our emergency preparedness checklist provides a comprehensive maintenance schedule.

Communication Plans and Family Meeting Points

A family emergency kit is only effective if your family can coordinate during a crisis. Establish a communication plan that includes an out-of-area contact person, a primary and secondary family meeting point, and procedures for different emergency scenarios. The out-of-area contact serves as a relay point since local phone lines may be jammed during a large disaster. If local calls fail, family members can call the out-of-area contact to report their status and location. Program this number into every family member's phone and write it on a card in each person's go-bag.

Designate two family meeting points: one near your home for situations where you must evacuate but can return later, and one outside your neighborhood for situations where your home area is inaccessible. Practice your communication plan and evacuation routes at least twice per year, including nighttime drills. Teach all family members how to turn off utilities like gas and water at the main shutoff points. Post emergency instructions and meeting point information visibly near the phone or on the refrigerator. Include backup communication methods like two-way radios for situations where cell networks are congested or down. Our emergency communication plan guide provides templates and practice schedules.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I choose the right size family emergency kit?
Calculate your family's needs by multiplying per-person supply requirements by the number of household members. For water, plan one gallon per person per day for at least three days. For food, plan 2,000 calories per adult and 1,200 to 2,000 per child daily. Factor in the ages, health conditions, and special needs of every family member. A family of four typically needs a kit rated for four to eight people depending on the supplier's sizing scale.
What special items do families with babies need?
Families with infants should include formula, bottles, disposable diapers, wipes, baby food, infant-safe water, a change of clothes, a portable crib or travel bassinet, a baby carrier, over-the-counter infant medications like fever reducers, and comfort items like a favorite toy or blanket. Calculate additional water needs for formula preparation and pack a manual bottle warmer or thermos for hot water.
Should I include pet supplies in my family emergency kit?
Yes. Pack at least a three-day supply of pet food, collapsible water bowls, a leash with identification tags, any needed medications, copies of vaccination records, a pet carrier, waste bags, and a comfort item like a favorite toy. Research pet-friendly evacuation shelters in advance since many emergency shelters do not accept animals. Include a recent photo of your pet in case of separation.
How do I create an emergency communication plan for my family?
Establish an out-of-area contact person that all family members can reach to relay their status. Designate a primary and secondary family meeting point. Program emergency contacts into all phones and write them on paper as backup. Teach young children how to call 911 and recite their full name, address, and parent phone numbers. Practice your communication plan at least twice per year.
How should I organize supplies for multiple family members?
Use a shared-plus-individual approach. Keep bulk supplies like water, food, and tools in a central shared kit. Pack individual grab-and-go bags for each family member containing personal items like medications, a change of clothes, comfortable shoes, personal hygiene products, a flashlight, and identification documents. Color-code bags by family member for quick identification during emergencies.
How often should I update my family emergency kit?
Review your family emergency kit every six months and after any major life event. Replace expired food and water, update children's clothing sizes, rotate medications, adjust supplies for seasonal changes, and refresh emergency contact information. Involve all family members in the review process so everyone knows what is available and where items are stored.
