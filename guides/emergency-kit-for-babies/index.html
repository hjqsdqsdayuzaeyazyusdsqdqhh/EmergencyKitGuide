Emergency Kit for Babies: Infant and Toddler Emergency Supplies

Assemble a complete emergency kit for babies and toddlers. Covers formula, diapers, safety equipment, and comfort items for infants during disasters.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Babies Require Dedicated Emergency Supplies

Infants and toddlers have needs that are fundamentally different from older children and adults. They cannot communicate hunger, discomfort, or illness beyond crying, making it essential that caregivers have every possible supply on hand to diagnose and address problems quickly. During an emergency, access to stores, pharmacies, and pediatricians may be cut off for days. A baby who runs out of formula, diapers, or essential medication faces immediate health risks that can escalate rapidly. Building a dedicated baby emergency kit ensures that your youngest family member is protected even when normal resources are unavailable.

A baby emergency kit is not simply a few extra diapers tucked into the family disaster bag. It is a comprehensive system that covers feeding, hygiene, medical care, safety, sleep, and emotional comfort. Each category requires careful planning, appropriate quantities, and regular rotation to ensure supplies remain fresh and usable. This guide walks you through every component, explains how to calculate the right quantities for your baby's age and needs, and provides practical tips for storage and maintenance. Pair this baby-specific kit with the family family 72-hour emergency kit for a complete household preparedness system.

Formula, Bottles, and Feeding Supplies

For formula-fed babies, your emergency kit must contain enough formula to last at least seven days. A typical infant consumes 24 to 32 ounces of formula per day, which means you need approximately 168 to 224 ounces for a full week. Store both powder formula for efficiency and at least 48 hours worth of ready-to-feed formula for situations where clean water is unavailable or boiling is not possible. Pack at least eight to ten bottles with nipples, a bottle brush for cleaning, and enough clean water to mix powder formula. Sterilization tablets or a small pot for boiling water and bottles are essential backups when dishwashing is not an option.

For breastfed babies, the mother's ability to produce milk may be affected by stress, dehydration, or poor nutrition during an emergency. Pump and store breast milk in the freezer if possible, and include a manual breast pump in the emergency kit as a backup. Pack milk storage bags, a small cooler with ice packs for transporting expressed milk, and a nursing cover for privacy during evacuation or shelter situations. Include a supply of nipple cream and breast pads for the mother's comfort and hygiene. Our 72-hour emergency food list covers shelf-stable food options that can supplement a nursing mother's nutrition.

Diaper Supply and Changing Essentials

Newborns may go through 10 to 12 diapers per day, while toddlers typically use 6 to 8. For a three-day emergency, that translates to 30 to 36 diapers for a newborn and 18 to 24 for a toddler. For a full week of preparedness, stock 70 to 84 diapers for a newborn and 42 to 56 for a toddler. Pack a mix of sizes since babies grow quickly and a size that fits today may be too small next month. Include a supply of overnight diapers which are more absorbent and can stretch supply during emergencies when changing frequency may be reduced due to limited water for cleaning.

Beyond diapers, your changing supplies should include at least 200 unscented baby wipes, a portable changing pad that can be wiped clean, diaper rash cream or ointment, disposable bags for soiled diapers and clothing, and hand sanitizer for the caregiver. Cloth diaper users should pack a supply of disposable diapers for emergencies since cloth requires washing with clean water that may not be available. Store all changing supplies in a waterproof bag to keep them clean and dry. Include a small trash bag for each day's diaper disposal to maintain hygiene in a shelter environment. Baby-Safe Water and Sanitation

Clean water is even more critical for babies than for adults because their immune systems are still developing and they are more susceptible to waterborne illness. Store at least one gallon of water per day specifically for baby use, covering formula preparation, bottle cleaning, and basic hygiene. Use commercially sealed bottled water whenever possible rather than tap water that has been stored at home, as commercial bottling provides an additional safety margin. Label baby water containers clearly and store them separately from general household water supplies to prevent contamination.

Include water purification tablets or a small portable water filter as a backup for situations where your stored supply runs low. Never use untreated natural water sources to prepare formula for infants. Pack sterilization tablets for bottles and nipples, a small pan for boiling water if you have access to a heat source, and disposable bottle liners for quick cleanup. Maintain strict hand hygiene by packing disposable gloves and hand sanitizer for every diaper change or feeding. In shelter environments where running water may be limited, these sanitation supplies become critical for preventing illness. Our 72-hour water storage guide covers purification methods in detail.

Infant First Aid and Medical Supplies

A baby-specific first aid kit should include infant-safe pain relievers such as acetaminophen drops (with a dosing syringe), a digital rectal thermometer for accurate temperature readings, saline nasal drops to relieve congestion, a rubber bulb syringe for clearing nasal passages, and teething gel approved for infants. Include a supply of adhesive bandages designed for small fingers and skin-safe medical tape. Pack petroleum jelly to protect the diaper area from rash, infant-safe sunscreen for babies over six months, and insect repellent safe for young children if your area is prone to mosquitoes.

Write the pediatrician's name, phone number, and the Poison Control Center number (1-800-222-1222) on a laminated card and include it in the first aid kit. If your baby has any known allergies, chronic conditions, or takes daily medications, document these on the same card and include a supply of required medications. Pack a copy of the baby's immunization record and health insurance information in a waterproof pouch. For families with newborns, include umbilical cord care supplies if the cord stump has not yet healed. Our emergency preparedness checklist provides a complete framework for organizing medical supplies.

Portable Sleep Solutions: Cribs, Bassinets, and Carriers

Babies need a firm, flat sleep surface to reduce the risk of suffocation and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). During an evacuation, a portable travel crib or bassinet provides this essential surface in a compact, lightweight package. Modern travel cribs fold into carry bags weighing as little as 10 to 12 pounds and can be set up in under a minute. If space is extremely limited, a travel bassinet designed for newborns offers an even smaller footprint. Never improvise a sleep surface by using pillows, blankets, or bunched clothing, as these materials significantly increase suffocation risk for infants.

A baby carrier or wrap is essential for hands-free evacuation. When you need to carry supplies, open doors, or navigate stairs, a structured baby carrier keeps the infant secure against your body while freeing both hands. Practice using the carrier before an emergency so you can put it on quickly and correctly in a stressful situation. Pack a lightweight blanket or sleep sack for the baby's warmth during sleep, along with a fitted sheet for the travel crib. Include a small nightlight or glow stick to check on the baby without turning on bright lights during nighttime feedings. Comfort Items and Emotional Security

Disruptions to routine cause significant stress for infants and toddlers, even though they cannot understand why their environment has changed. Familiar comfort items help reduce anxiety and promote sleep during chaotic circumstances. Pack the baby's favorite pacifier (bring at least three, as they get lost easily), a beloved stuffed animal or lovey, a familiar blanket that smells like home, and a few small, safe toys for distraction during long waits at shelters or evacuation centers. If the baby uses a specific sound machine or lullaby to fall asleep, include a small battery-powered sound machine or a phone loaded with familiar sounds.

Maintain the baby's feeding and sleep schedule as closely as possible during an emergency. Predictability provides comfort even when the surroundings are unfamiliar. Pack a few favorite board books or soft activity toys that can occupy a toddler during extended periods in a car seat or carrier. Include a small supply of the baby's favorite snacks or treats for toddlers who have started solid foods. If the baby uses a specific type of diaper cream, laundry detergent, or soap, pack those exact brands to avoid skin reactions from unfamiliar products. Consistency in small details makes an outsized difference in keeping a baby calm and healthy during a crisis.

Baby Clothing and Seasonal Layering

Pack at least three full changes of baby clothing in the emergency kit, including onesies, pants, socks, and a hat. Choose layers that can be added or removed as temperature changes. For cold weather, include a fleece sleep sack, warm socks, a knit hat, and a lightweight snowsuit if evacuation occurs during winter. For warm weather, pack lightweight cotton onesies, a sun hat, and breathable socks. Include a waterproof outer layer such as a rain suit or poncho designed for infants, since babies are particularly vulnerable to getting wet and cold during evacuations.

Store clothing in a sealed waterproof bag to keep it clean and dry. As the baby grows, update clothing sizes in the kit every three months. Include a swaddling blanket for newborns and a larger receiving blanket for older infants. Pack burp cloths and bibs to manage feeding messes when laundry facilities are unavailable. If the baby has sensitive skin, use fragrance-free detergent to wash emergency clothing and pack a small supply of the detergent in the kit. For comprehensive supply planning, consult our disaster supply checklist to ensure your baby's needs are integrated into your overall household preparedness.

Baby-Proofing Your Emergency Shelter Space

When you arrive at a shelter, hotel, or relative's home during an evacuation, the space will not be baby-proofed. Electrical outlets, cleaning supplies, sharp objects, and small choking hazards may be accessible. Pack a set of portable outlet covers, a roll of painter's tape for securing loose cords or covering sharp corners, and a portable baby gate if you expect to stay in a location with stairs. Bring a small toolkit with child-safety latches that can be temporarily installed on cabinets containing hazardous materials.

Inspect any emergency shelter space thoroughly before allowing the baby to explore. Check for small items on the floor that could be choking hazards, secure windows and balcony doors, and identify the location of fire extinguishers and emergency exits. Place the baby's travel crib away from windows, heaters, and heavy furniture that could topple. If the baby is crawling or walking, use a portable playpen to create a safe enclosed area during times when you need to focus on other tasks. Keep the baby's kit organized and accessible so you can perform quick diaper changes and feedings without searching through disorganized supplies in a stressful environment. Frequently Asked Questions

How much formula should I store in an emergency kit?
Store at least seven days worth of formula, including both powder and ready-to-feed options. A breastfed baby whose mother may be stressed and producing less milk should have approximately 24 to 32 ounces per day. For formula-fed infants, calculate based on the baby's current daily intake and add a 25 percent buffer. Ready-to-feed formula is more convenient during emergencies because it requires no water, but it is heavier and more expensive than powder. Always include at least a small supply of ready-to-feed for situations where clean water is unavailable.
How many diapers should I keep in the kit?
Stock at least 72 hours worth of diapers, which typically means 8 to 12 diapers per day for newborns and 6 to 8 for toddlers. This translates to roughly 24 to 36 diapers for a three-day supply. For a full week, prepare 56 to 84 diapers. Pack a mix of sizes if your baby is near a size transition, since growth during stress can happen quickly. Include a portable changing pad, diaper rash cream, disposable bags for soiled diapers, and at least 200 unscented baby wipes.
What baby food alternatives work if I run out of pureed food?
For babies over six months who have started solid foods, pack a variety of shelf-stable options including single-ingredient cereal packets, teething crackers, mashed banana in squeeze pouches, and soft canned fruits in juice. If pureed baby food runs out, softened adult foods such as canned vegetables drained and mashed, oatmeal made with safe water, and mashed avocado from a sealed container can provide nutrition. Avoid honey for babies under one year old and avoid common allergens if the baby has not been tested for them.
Are portable cribs practical for emergency kits?
A lightweight travel crib or portable bassinet is highly practical and should be included if space allows. Models that fold into a compact carry bag weigh as little as 10 to 12 pounds and set up in under a minute. If a full portable crib is too large, a travel bassinet or a wearable baby carrier that supports the infant in a reclined position provides a safe sleep alternative. Never let a baby sleep on the floor without a firm, flat surface as this increases the risk of suffocation.
What first aid items are essential for babies?
Include infant-safe acetaminophen or ibuprofen (age-appropriate), a digital thermometer, saline nasal drops for congestion, a bulb syringe, teething gel approved for infants, petroleum jelly for diaper area protection, and infant-safe sunscreen. Pack bandages designed for small fingers and adhesive tape that is gentle on delicate skin. Include the pediatrician's phone number and the Poison Control hotline number (1-800-222-1222) written on a card inside the kit.
