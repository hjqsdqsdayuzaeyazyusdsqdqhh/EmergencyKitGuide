Emergency Kit California: State-Specific Disaster Preparedness Guide

Build a California emergency kit tailored to earthquakes, wildfires, and landslides. State-specific supplies, evacuation routes, and seasonal prep tips.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

California's Unique Disaster Risk Profile

California is one of the most disaster-prone states in the nation, facing a convergence of hazards that few other regions experience simultaneously. The state sits along the Pacific Ring of Fire, placing it squarely in one of the world's most active seismic zones. The San Andreas Fault runs roughly 800 miles through the state, and the Hayward Fault beneath the San Francisco Bay Area is considered one of the most dangerous fault lines in the country. Seismic events in California range from minor tremors that residents learn to live with to catastrophic earthquakes capable of toppling buildings, severing utilities, and isolating entire communities for days or weeks.

Beyond seismic risk, California endures an annual wildfire season that has grown longer and more destructive due to prolonged drought, high winds, and expanding development into the wildland-urban interface. Coastal communities face tsunami threats from undersea earthquakes, while inland valleys endure extreme heat waves that regularly push temperatures above 110 degrees Fahrenheit. Add the recurring reality of planned power shutoffs, landslides during atmospheric river events, and water supply disruptions, and it becomes clear that a generic emergency kit is simply not enough for California residents. Your kit must address overlapping hazards that can strike at different times of the year, sometimes simultaneously.

Earthquake Preparedness: Essentials for Seismic Safety

Earthquake preparedness in California starts with securing your home and building a kit designed for post-quake survival. Bolt heavy furniture and appliances to walls, install latches on kitchen cabinets, and anchor your water heater to the wall studs with a proper strapping kit. Place sturdy, closed-toe shoes and a small flashlight beside every bed so you can protect your feet from broken glass and navigate in the dark if power is lost. Keep a heavy-duty wrench in an accessible location so you can manually shut off your gas supply if you smell a leak after an earthquake.

Your earthquake-specific supplies should include a loud whistle to signal rescuers if you are trapped under debris, heavy work gloves for handling broken glass and rubble, and thick plastic sheeting with duct tape to seal broken windows or create temporary shelter. Store at least seven days of water per person, as water mains frequently break during significant seismic events. A battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio keeps you informed about aftershock warnings and emergency instructions. Wildfire Season Preparation and Evacuation Readiness

California's wildfire season typically peaks between October and December when hot, dry Santa Ana winds sweep through Southern California and Diablo winds blow through the northern part of the state. These wind events can push wildfire flames at astonishing speeds, consuming thousands of acres in hours and giving residents very little time to evacuate. Defensible space around your home is your first line of defense. Clear vegetation, dead leaves, and combustible materials within at least 100 feet of structures, trim tree branches that overhang your roof, and move firewood stacks away from the house.

Pack a dedicated wildfire go-bag that stays in your car or near your front door at all times during fire season. Include N95 respirator masks to protect against smoke inhalation, safety goggles with sealed edges, a change of clothes, sturdy shoes, copies of essential documents in a waterproof pouch, and at least three days of food and water. Identify at least two evacuation routes from your neighborhood and practice driving them. Monitor CalFire incident reports and your county's emergency alert system. Sign up for Nixie or your county's reverse-911 notification service so you receive immediate evacuation orders. For more detailed wildfire planning, see our wildfire preparedness guide.

Coastal and Tsunami Preparedness

California's 1,100 miles of coastline expose millions of residents and visitors to tsunami risk, particularly in communities from Crescent City down through San Francisco, Los Angeles, and San Diego. A major undersea earthquake along the Cascadia Subduction Zone or the Pacific Rim can generate a tsunami that reaches California shores in as little as 15 minutes. Know whether your home or workplace falls within a designated tsunami evacuation zone by checking the California Tsunami Program maps maintained by CalOES.

The most important tsunami safety rule is simple: if an earthquake lasts more than 20 seconds and you are near the coast, do not wait for an official warning. Move immediately to high ground at least 100 feet above sea level or at least two miles inland. Keep a go-bag in your car and another at your workplace with at least one day of supplies. If you are visiting the coast, study the evacuation route signs posted in beach parking areas and low-lying coastal streets. Tsunami evacuation routes are marked with blue and white signs throughout California's coastal communities. Extreme Heat and Drought Preparedness

California's Central Valley regularly experiences summer temperatures exceeding 110 degrees Fahrenheit, and extended heat waves are becoming more frequent and intense. Extreme heat is the deadliest weather-related hazard in California, responsible for more fatalities annually than wildfires, earthquakes, and floods combined. During heat waves, your emergency kit must include extra water, electrolyte packets, lightweight and breathable clothing, wide-brimmed sun hats, and a battery-powered fan. Identify cooling centers in your neighborhood through your county's emergency management website.

California's recurring droughts create additional challenges for emergency water storage. During mandatory water rationing periods, residents may face restrictions that limit how much water they can store. Pre-position your emergency water supply before rationing begins. Fill bathtubs and large containers at the first sign of a supply disruption. A portable water filtration system such as a Sawyer or LifeStraw allows you to purify water from natural sources if municipal supply is compromised. Consider installing a rainwater collection system, which is legal in California for non-potable uses. Our emergency water storage guide covers long-term water preservation methods ideal for California's dry climate.

Public Safety Power Shutoffs (PSPS) and Extended Outages

California's investor-owned utilities, including Pacific Gas and Electric, Southern California Edison, and San Diego Gas and Electric, implement Public Safety Power Shutoffs during high wind events when downed power lines pose an extreme wildfire ignition risk. These shutoffs can affect millions of customers simultaneously and last anywhere from 24 hours to five days or longer depending on weather conditions and equipment inspection timelines. Living without power in California means no air conditioning during extreme heat, no refrigeration for medications and food, and no electric water pumps in many rural areas.

Build a power outage readiness kit that includes fully charged portable power banks with at least 20,000 mAh capacity, a portable solar panel charger, battery-powered LED lanterns, and a battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio. Keep a car charger for your phone and consider a small portable inverter that plugs into your vehicle's 12-volt outlet for charging devices. If you rely on medical equipment that requires electricity, register with your utility's medical baseline program to receive advance notice of PSPS events. Stock a cooler with ice packs to preserve temperature-sensitive medications during outages. For more comprehensive power outage preparation, see our home emergency kit guide.

Evacuation Planning for California Residents

Effective evacuation planning in California requires understanding the specific threats facing your community and identifying multiple routes to safety. Wildfire evacuations often involve narrow, winding roads through canyon and hillside neighborhoods where traffic congestion can quickly become life-threatening. Keep your car fueled at least half full at all times during fire season and store a go-bag in the trunk. Map at least two evacuation routes from your neighborhood and practice driving them so you are familiar with alternate paths if your primary route is blocked by flames, fallen trees, or other hazards.

Register for your county's emergency notification system and download the California Emergency app or the FEMA app for real-time alerts. Identify evacuation shelters and pet-friendly shelters in advance by checking with your local Red Cross chapter or county Office of Emergency Services. If you live in a seismic zone, know the locations of earthquake-resistant public buildings that may serve as temporary shelters. Coordinate with neighbors who may need assistance during evacuations, such as elderly residents, people with disabilities, or families without reliable transportation. County-Specific Considerations Across California

California's 58 counties face dramatically different risk profiles. Los Angeles County must prepare for earthquakes, wildfires, and coastal flooding simultaneously. San Francisco and the Bay Area face severe earthquake risk from the Hayward and San Andreas faults, along with tsunami exposure along the waterfront. San Diego County contends with wildfire risk in its eastern foothills and coastal flooding during king tide events. Rural counties like Humboldt and Mendocino face isolation during major earthquakes because limited road access can be cut off by landslides and bridge failures.

Inland counties in the Central Valley, including Sacramento, San Joaquin, and Fresno, face extreme heat, agricultural chemical exposure during wildfires, and flash flooding from atmospheric rivers. Mountain communities in the Sierra Nevada must prepare for heavy winter snowfall that can isolate homes for days, along with wildfire risk during the dry season. Regardless of your county, register with local emergency services, know your specific hazard zones, and tailor your kit to the threats most likely to affect your area. Visit the CalOES website for county-specific hazard maps and emergency contact information to ensure your preparations match your actual risk.

Seasonal Preparation Calendar for California

California's disaster risks follow a predictable seasonal pattern that allows residents to prepare strategically. During January through March, focus on winter storm readiness, including clearing gutters, trimming trees near power lines, and checking your home for leaks. Spring is the ideal time to review and restock your earthquake kit, test all electronics, replace expired food and water, and update your evacuation plans. Take advantage of calmer weather to conduct family emergency drills and inspect your home's seismic retrofitting.

Summer brings extreme heat preparation to the forefront. Stock extra water, check that your home's cooling systems are functional, and prepare for possible rolling blackouts on high-demand days. September through December is peak wildfire season in California. Clear defensible space around your home, keep your car fueled, and monitor fire weather conditions daily. Keep your go-bag packed and accessible from October through December. By following this seasonal cycle, you maintain readiness year-round without feeling overwhelmed. Frequently Asked Questions

What makes a California emergency kit different from a standard kit?
California faces a unique combination of earthquakes, wildfires, tsunamis, extreme heat, and planned power shutoffs. A California-specific kit includes earthquake safety tools like sturdy shoes and a wrench for gas shutoff, N95 masks and goggles for wildfire smoke, dust masks for post-earthquake debris, and extended water storage due to recurring drought conditions. You also need to plan for extended power outages from PSPS events that can last multiple days.
How much water should I store for California wildfires and droughts?
Store a minimum of two gallons per person per day for at least seven days. California water supplies can be disrupted by earthquakes damaging infrastructure, wildfires contaminating reservoirs, or drought-related rationing. Aim for 14 gallons per person for a one-week supply. Consider investing in a 55-gallon water barrel with a siphon pump and keep water purification tablets as a backup.
Should I prepare for power shutoffs in California?
Yes. Public Safety Power Shutoffs (PSPS) occur when utilities like PG&E, SCE, or SDG&E shut off power during high wind events to prevent equipment from starting wildfires. PSPS events can last two to five days or longer. Keep fully charged power banks, a portable solar charger, battery-powered lighting, and a car charger for your phone. A small portable generator used outdoors only can provide backup power for critical devices.
Do I need earthquake-specific supplies in my California kit?
Absolutely. Include sturdy closed-toe shoes by your bed to protect against broken glass, a gas shutoff wrench, a whistle for signaling if trapped, work gloves, and heavy-duty plastic sheeting to seal broken windows. After an earthquake, water mains and gas lines can break, so your extended water supply and a manual can opener become critical survival items.
What should I include for wildfire smoke protection?
Pack at least five N95 or P100 respirator masks per person, safety goggles with sealed edges, and a portable air purifier if possible. During California wildfire season, smoke can travel hundreds of miles and degrade air quality for weeks. Keep a supply of HEPA filters for your home HVAC system and seal gaps around windows and doors with weatherstripping or plastic sheeting.
How do I prepare for a tsunami in coastal California?
If you live or work near the California coast, know your tsunami evacuation zone. After an earthquake lasting more than 20 seconds, move immediately to high ground at least 100 feet above sea level. Keep a go-bag in your car and at work. The California Tsunami Program maps evacuation routes for coastal cities from Crescent City to San Diego. Practice your route at least twice a year.
What resources does CalOES provide for California emergency preparedness?
The California Governor's Office of Emergency Services (CalOES) offers free preparedness checklists, maps of evacuation zones, and the ReadyGov California program with multilingual resources. CalOES also coordinates mutual aid across counties during major disasters and manages the AlertReady mass notification system. Visit caloes.ca.gov for county-specific hazard maps and preparedness guides.
