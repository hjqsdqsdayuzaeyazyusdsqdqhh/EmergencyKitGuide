Emergency Kit Florida: Hurricane, Flooding, and Tropical Storm Preparedness

Florida emergency kit guide for hurricane season, storm surge, flooding, extreme heat, and lightning. State-specific supplies and evacuation planning.

Last updated: July 25, 2025By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Florida's Hurricane-Driven Disaster Profile

Florida is the most hurricane-prone state in the United States, with a peninsula that juts into the warm waters of the Atlantic Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico, exposing nearly the entire state to tropical cyclone threats. The Atlantic hurricane season officially runs from June 1 through November 30, with the most active period occurring between August and October when sea surface temperatures reach their annual peak. Florida has been struck by more major hurricanes than any other state in the nation, and the memories of Andrew in 1992, the record-breaking 2004 season with four landfalls, Irma in 2017, and Ian in 2022 serve as constant reminders that preparation is not optional.

Beyond hurricanes, Florida faces a year-round threat of extreme heat and humidity, daily afternoon thunderstorms that produce more lightning strikes than any other state, widespread flooding from storm surge and heavy rainfall, and sinkholes that can open suddenly in the state's limestone bedrock. The Florida Division of Emergency Management coordinates preparedness efforts across 67 counties, each with its own emergency management office and evacuation zone maps. Understanding Florida's unique geography, including its low elevation, flat terrain, and high water table, is essential for building an emergency kit that addresses the specific risks you will actually face.

Hurricane Season Preparation Timeline

Effective hurricane preparation in Florida requires starting well before the storm season begins. By April and May, you should have completed all major home preparations, including installing or testing hurricane shutters, trimming trees and palm fronds near your roof, clearing gutters and storm drains, reinforcing garage doors, and inspecting your roof for damaged or missing shingles. Fill any prescriptions you take regularly and arrange for at least a 30-day supply, as pharmacies may be closed for extended periods after a storm. Service your generator and test it under load, verify that your sump pump is working, and confirm that your flood insurance policy is active, as new policies require a 30-day waiting period before taking effect.

When a tropical system enters the Gulf of Mexico or approaches the Florida coast, shift into immediate preparation mode. Fill your vehicle's gas tank to full, withdraw cash from ATMs, stock at least seven days of food and water for every household member, and charge all portable power banks to capacity. If you are in an evacuation zone, begin loading your go-bag into your vehicle and plan to leave as soon as a hurricane watch is issued for your county. Monitor the National Hurricane Center forecasts closely and follow instructions from your county emergency management office without delay. Understanding Florida's Evacuation Zone System

Florida uses a lettered evacuation zone system that ranks coastal and low-lying areas by their vulnerability to storm surge, the deadliest threat from hurricanes. Zone A, which includes barrier islands, coastal areas, and mobile home parks, is the first zone evacuated and faces the highest storm surge risk. Zone B areas are slightly further inland but still vulnerable to significant flooding. Zone C through Zone E areas are progressively less vulnerable, though they can still experience dangerous conditions during major hurricanes. Know your specific zone letter by entering your home address at FloridaDisaster.org or checking with your county emergency management office.

Evacuation route planning is critical in Florida because the state's peninsular geography creates severe traffic bottlenecks. Most coastal residents must travel north or inland on a limited number of highways, including I-75, I-95, I-4, and the Florida Turnpike. Contraflow lanes are activated on some highways during large-scale evacuations, but this process takes time to implement. Leave early when a hurricane watch is issued rather than waiting for the mandatory evacuation order, as roads become gridlocked within hours. Identify at least two evacuation destinations outside your county, including pet-friendly hotels and designated public shelters. Storm Surge, Flooding, and Water Preparedness

Storm surge is the leading cause of hurricane fatalities in Florida, and the state's flat topography means that ocean water can penetrate miles inland during a major storm surge event. Hurricane Ian in 2022 produced storm surge of over 15 feet in parts of Southwest Florida, destroying entire neighborhoods and causing catastrophic damage well beyond the coast. Even Category 1 and 2 hurricanes can produce deadly storm surge in shallow coastal areas. Flood insurance is essential for every Florida homeowner and renter, as standard homeowners policies do not cover flood damage. The National Flood Insurance Program provides up to $250,000 in building coverage and $100,000 in contents coverage, but you must purchase a policy at least 30 days before it takes effect.

Store at least seven days of drinking water in your emergency kit, as Florida's water treatment plants and distribution systems may lose power or sustain damage during and after hurricanes. The heat and humidity that persist after a storm make dehydration a serious risk, so aim for two gallons per person per day. Pack waterproof dry bags for important documents, electronics, and medications. Consider purchasing a portable water filtration device such as a Sawyer or LifeStraw as a backup purification method if your stored supply runs low. Our emergency water storage guide provides detailed guidance on water storage containers and purification methods for Florida's climate conditions.

Extreme Heat, Humidity, and Lightning Safety

Florida's subtropical climate produces extreme heat and humidity throughout the summer months, with heat index values regularly exceeding 105 degrees Fahrenheit. Heat-related illness is a year-round concern in Florida, and when combined with power outages after hurricanes, the risk of heat exhaustion and heat stroke increases dramatically. Elderly residents, young children, outdoor workers, and people without air conditioning are particularly vulnerable. During any emergency that disrupts power, identify the nearest cooling center operated by your county, as Florida municipalities routinely open air-conditioned public buildings during extended outages.

Florida is the lightning capital of the United States, experiencing more lightning strikes per square mile than any other state. During the summer months, nearly every afternoon brings thunderstorms with dangerous lightning that can strike up to 10 miles from the center of a storm. Lightning kills or injures people in Florida every year, and outdoor activities during thunderstorms are extremely risky. Your emergency kit should include a NOAA weather radio with alert function so you receive severe thunderstorm warnings even when you are asleep. Pack electrolyte replacement packets, cooling towels, and a battery-powered fan for heat emergencies. Sinkholes, Flooding, and Ground-Level Hazards

Florida's geological foundation of porous limestone bedrock creates a unique hazard that few other states face: sinkholes. These sudden ground collapses can swallow vehicles, destroy homes, and open without warning, particularly after heavy rainfall saturates the soil or when groundwater levels fluctuate during drought and recovery cycles. Sinkhole activity is most common in Central Florida, particularly in Pasco, Hillsborough, and Marion counties, but sinkholes can occur anywhere in the state where limestone sits beneath the surface. While there is no way to prevent sinkholes, understanding this risk informs where you store your emergency kit and how you plan for rapid evacuation.

Florida's flat terrain and low elevation also create chronic flooding problems that extend far beyond hurricane events. Summer thunderstorms regularly dump several inches of rain in under an hour, overwhelming storm drains and flooding streets and homes in low-lying areas. Inland communities along rivers such as the St. Johns, the Peace, and the Withlacoochee can experience flooding that persists for days or weeks after heavy rain events or tropical systems. Keep your emergency kit stored at a level above potential flood lines, and consider keeping a secondary go-bag on an upper floor if you live in a flood-prone area. For flood-specific supplies and preparation steps, consult our flood emergency kit guide.

Florida-Specific Emergency Kit Additions

A Florida emergency kit requires several state-specific additions that go beyond the standard 72-hour supply list. Pack insect repellent with DEET as the top ingredient, since standing water from flooding and storm surge creates massive mosquito breeding grounds that increase the risk of West Nile virus, Zika, and other mosquito-borne illnesses. Include waterproof dry bags or heavy-duty garbage bags for protecting electronics, documents, and clothing from water damage. Sunscreen with SPF 50 or higher and a wide-brimmed sun hat are essential for outdoor activities during power outages and evacuations when shade structures may be damaged or unavailable.

Florida's hurricane season overlaps with the peak of mosquito and pest activity, so packing sealed containers for food storage becomes even more important to prevent attracting insects and rodents to your living space. Consider adding a portable battery-powered or hand-crank fan to maintain airflow in your home if air conditioning is lost. Store at least a 30-day supply of any prescription medications, as Florida pharmacies may be unable to restock for extended periods after major hurricanes. If you have a pet, pack a pet carrier, leash, vaccination records, and at least seven days of pet food, as not all shelters accept animals. Shelter Options and Post-Storm Recovery

Florida offers several sheltering options for residents who cannot evacuate to private destinations. Public hurricane shelters are operated by county emergency management offices in coordination with the American Red Cross and are typically located in hardened public buildings such as schools, community centers, and hospitals. Shelters in evacuation zones do not open during mandatory evacuations, so you must travel to a shelter outside your zone. Florida also operates special needs shelters for residents who require electricity for medical equipment, oxygen, dialysis, or other life-sustaining needs. Register with your county's special needs shelter program well before hurricane season begins, as registration is required for access.

After a hurricane passes, the recovery phase begins immediately and presents its own set of hazards. Downed power lines, flooded roadways, contaminated water supplies, structural damage to buildings, and displaced wildlife including snakes and alligators create a dangerous environment. Do not return to your home until authorities issue an official all-clear. When you do return, inspect your home for structural damage before entering, avoid standing water that may be electrically charged from downed lines, and photograph all damage for insurance claims before cleaning up. Keep your emergency kit accessible during the recovery period as power, water, and supply chain disruptions can persist for weeks after a major hurricane. Frequently Asked Questions

When is hurricane season in Florida and when should I prepare?
Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 through November 30, with peak activity from August through October. Florida residents should complete all hurricane preparations by June 1 each year. This includes installing storm shutters, stocking at least seven days of supplies, filling prescriptions, and reviewing evacuation routes. Early preparation avoids the last-minute supply rushes that leave store shelves empty.
How do I know if I live in a Florida evacuation zone?
Florida uses a lettered evacuation zone system (Zone A through Zone E) based on storm surge vulnerability. Zone A is the most vulnerable and evacuates first. Check your zone by visiting your county emergency management website or entering your address at FloridaDisaster.org. Know your zone letter and the specific evacuation route for your area before hurricane season begins.
Do I need flood insurance in Florida?
Yes. Standard homeowners and renters insurance policies do not cover flood damage, which is the most common and costly damage from hurricanes in Florida. The National Flood Insurance Program provides coverage up to $250,000 for building property and $100,000 for contents. Florida has the highest flood risk in the nation due to its flat terrain, low elevation, and high water table. Purchase flood insurance at least 30 days before hurricane season for it to take effect.
What supplies do I need specifically for Florida hurricanes?
Beyond standard emergency supplies, Florida hurricane kits need extra water due to heat and humidity, battery-powered fans, waterproof dry bags for documents, plywood or hurricane shutters for windows, a portable generator with fuel stored safely, and insect repellent for post-storm mosquitoes. Pack at least seven days of supplies as Florida evacuations can be lengthy due to the peninsula limited northbound routes.
What makes Florida flooding different from other states?
Florida extremely flat terrain, low elevation, high water table, and peninsular geography make it uniquely vulnerable to flooding. Storm surge can penetrate far inland because there is little elevation change to stop it. Even without hurricanes, Florida experiences daily afternoon thunderstorms that can cause localized flooding. The state limestone bedrock also creates sinkholes that can open suddenly after heavy rain events.
Should I evacuate or shelter in place during a Florida hurricane?
Always follow mandatory evacuation orders from your county emergency management office. If you are in an evacuation zone, leave when told. If you are not in an evacuation zone, shelter in place in an interior room away from windows. Never leave during the storm itself as conditions can deteriorate rapidly. Before hurricane season, decide whether you will stay with family inland, book a hotel outside the zone, or use a public shelter.
