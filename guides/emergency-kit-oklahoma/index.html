Emergency Kit Oklahoma: Tornado Alley, Severe Storms, and Wildfire Preparedness

Oklahoma emergency kit guide for Tornado Alley, severe thunderstorms, ice storms, extreme heat, flooding, and wildfire. State-specific disaster preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Oklahoma: Ground Zero for Tornado Alley

Oklahoma occupies the heart of Tornado Alley and has earned its reputation as the most tornado-battered state in the nation. The Oklahoma City metropolitan area has been struck by more tornadoes than any other major metropolitan area in the United States, and the state averages approximately 56 tornadoes per year with some years exceeding 100. The 2013 Moore EF5 tornado killed 24 people, leveled an elementary school, and caused over $2 billion in damage. The 1999 Bridge Creek-Moore tornado produced the highest wind speed ever recorded on Earth at 301 mph. These are not historical curiosities, they are annual threats that every Oklahoma family must prepare for with seriousness and urgency.

But tornadoes are only one chapter in Oklahoma's disaster story. Severe thunderstorms produce large hail that can injure people and destroy vehicles. Flash flooding kills more people in Oklahoma than tornadoes in many years. Ice storms can paralyze the entire state for weeks. Western Oklahoma faces significant wildfire risk during dry periods, and extreme summer heat pushes temperatures above 105 degrees with dangerous humidity. Building an emergency kit for Oklahoma means preparing for violent, fast-developing weather events that can strike with only minutes of warning. Start with a solid foundation using our family 72-hour emergency kit and then expand to address Oklahoma's specific hazards.

Storm Cellars, Safe Rooms, and Tornado Shelter Planning

In Oklahoma, the single most important investment you can make for tornado preparedness is a FEMA-compliant storm shelter or safe room. Underground storm cellars provide the highest level of protection and can withstand direct hits from EF5 tornadoes. Above-ground safe rooms engineered to FEMA P-361 standards offer life-saving protection for homeowners who cannot install underground shelters due to high water tables or other site limitations. The Oklahoma Department of Emergency Management offers cost-share programs that help homeowners install FEMA-compliant shelters, and the Oklahoma Storm Shelter Tax Credit provides additional financial assistance for installation.

Every Oklahoma household must have a tornado shelter plan that every family member knows and has practiced. If you do not have a storm cellar or safe room, identify the lowest interior room in your home away from all windows, such as a basement, interior bathroom, or closet. Practice getting your entire family from wherever they are in the house to the shelter location in under two minutes. Mobile home residents face extreme danger during tornadoes since manufactured homes offer zero tornado protection regardless of tie-down systems. Every mobile home community in Oklahoma should have access to a community storm shelter, and mobile home residents must know the location and have practiced reaching it before tornado season begins. NOAA Weather Radio and Tornado Warning Systems

In Oklahoma, the seconds between a tornado warning and tornado impact can mean the difference between life and death. A NOAA All-Hazards weather radio with Specific Area Message Encoding capability is the most reliable way to receive tornado warnings even when cell towers are overloaded and internet service is disrupted. These radios sound an audible alarm when a tornado warning is issued for your programmed county, waking you from sleep during nighttime storms. Oklahoma experiences a significant number of nighttime tornadoes that strike while families are sleeping, making an audible alarm radio essential for life safety. Keep batteries in a separate waterproof bag and test your radio monthly during tornado season.

Supplement your weather radio with multiple alert systems. Download the FEMA app and your local news station's weather app for smartphone alerts. Sign up for your county's emergency notification system for text and email alerts. Oklahoma City and Tulsa both operate outdoor warning sirens that sound during tornado warnings, but these sirens are designed for people who are outdoors and may not be audible inside buildings with windows closed. Never rely on a single warning source. Keep your phone charged during severe weather watches and maintain a portable power bank in your 72-hour kit so you can continue receiving alerts if power fails.

Post-Tornado Rescue and Recovery Supplies

After a tornado passes, the immediate environment becomes extremely hazardous. Broken glass, exposed nails, downed power lines, leaking gas lines, and unstable structures create a secondary wave of danger. Your post-tornado kit should include heavy-duty leather work gloves, sturdy closed-toe boots with puncture-resistant soles, hard hats or helmets to protect against falling debris, N95 respirator masks for dust and insulation particles, and safety goggles. Keep a whistle in your shelter location to signal rescuers if you become trapped under debris. A battery-powered or hand-crank flashlight is essential since power will likely be out across the affected area.

Store a basic tool kit in your shelter including a hammer, pry bar, adjustable wrench, duct tape, and a utility knife. These tools allow you to clear debris, shut off damaged utilities, and create exits if trapped. Keep a first aid kit with trauma supplies including tourniquets, pressure bandages, and clotting gauze since tornado injuries frequently involve severe lacerations and crush injuries. Have your home insurance policy number and agent phone number written down in your shelter kit. After a tornado, photograph all damage before moving anything for insurance claims. Ice Storms and Extended Power Outages

Oklahoma ice storms are among the most destructive and long-lasting weather events the state experiences. The December 2007 ice storm coated central and eastern Oklahoma with up to two inches of ice, toppling thousands of trees and power poles, and leaving over 600,000 customers without power for up to two weeks. Ice storms are particularly dangerous because the ice accumulation makes roads impassable, emergency vehicles cannot reach victims, and power restoration is complicated by continued hazardous conditions. The weight of ice on trees causes limbs to fall on homes and vehicles, and prolonged cold without heat creates life-threatening indoor conditions.

Prepare for ice storms by stocking non-perishable food that requires no cooking, since you may not have power for an electric stove or microwave. Fill your bathtub and every available container with water before the storm arrives for drinking and toilet flushing. Keep vehicles fuel tanks above half capacity throughout winter in case you need to evacuate to a warming center. Stock extra blankets, sleeping bags, and warm clothing. If you have a fireplace or wood stove, maintain a cord of seasoned firewood. Never use gas generators, charcoal grills, or propane heaters indoors as they produce deadly carbon monoxide. Invest in a backup power system sized to run essential heating and communication equipment.

Flash Flooding and Severe Thunderstorm Hazards

Flash flooding is consistently one of Oklahoma's deadliest weather hazards. The state's flat terrain, clay-heavy soils, and numerous creek and river systems create ideal conditions for rapid runoff during heavy rainfall. Oklahoma City experienced catastrophic flash flooding in May 2013 when over six inches of rain fell in under two hours, flooding highways and trapping drivers in their vehicles. The May 2015 Memorial Day weekend floods killed several people across central Oklahoma as creek waters rose with terrifying speed. Flash floods are particularly dangerous for drivers since as little as six inches of moving water can knock a person down, and two feet of water can float most vehicles.

Never drive through flooded roadways, turn around, don't drown. Know the flood-prone areas in your community including low-water crossings that are routinely impassable during heavy rain. If you live near a creek, stream, or drainage channel, monitor National Weather Service flash flood warnings and be prepared to move to higher ground immediately. Keep sandbags and a sump pump accessible if your home is in a flood-prone area. Store important documents and electronics above potential flood levels. Include water purification supplies in your emergency kit since flooding can contaminate municipal water supplies. Wildfire Risk in Western Oklahoma

While Oklahoma is best known for tornadoes, wildfire poses a significant and often underestimated threat, especially across the western half of the state where grasslands and agricultural land create fire-prone conditions. Spring and fall are the most dangerous wildfire seasons in Oklahoma, when dry vegetation, low humidity, and strong winds combine to create explosive fire behavior. The 2017 Starbution Fire burned over 30,000 acres in Logan and Payne counties, destroying dozens of structures and forcing mass evacuations. Drought conditions can escalate wildfire risk rapidly, and western Oklahoma communities in counties like Woodward, Ellis, and Roger Mills face particularly elevated danger during dry periods.

Create defensible space around your home by clearing dead vegetation, mowing grass short, and removing combustible materials within 30 feet of structures. Keep a dedicated wildfire evacuation go-bag packed with important documents, medications, N95 masks for smoke protection, protective goggles, a water bottle, and a phone charger. Know at least two evacuation routes from your neighborhood and monitor Oklahoma Forestry Services fire danger ratings. For homeowners in the wildland-urban interface, consider fire-resistant landscaping and building materials. Extreme Heat and Summer Preparedness

Oklahoma summers bring extreme heat that regularly pushes temperatures above 100 degrees Fahrenheit with high humidity that creates dangerous heat index values. The combination of heat and humidity in July and August can produce heat index readings above 115 degrees, which poses serious risk of heat exhaustion and heatstroke for anyone working or spending time outdoors. Oklahoma's 2011 heat wave set numerous temperature records and contributed to multiple heat-related fatalities across the state. Children, elderly residents, outdoor workers, and people without air conditioning face the highest risk during prolonged heat events.

Summer heat preparedness in Oklahoma requires adequate water storage beyond the standard recommendation, electrolyte replacement packets to replenish minerals lost through heavy sweating, wide-brim sun hats, high-SPF sunscreen, and breathable lightweight clothing. If your air conditioning fails during a heat wave, identify cooling centers in your community such as public libraries, shopping centers, and community centers. Keep a battery-powered fan in your home emergency kit. Never leave children or pets in parked vehicles, as interior temperatures can exceed 140 degrees in under 30 minutes in Oklahoma summer sun. Stay hydrated even if you do not feel thirsty, and limit strenuous outdoor activity to early morning or evening hours during extreme heat.

Oklahoma Emergency Resources and Tribal Communities

The Oklahoma Department of Emergency Management coordinates disaster response across all 77 counties and works closely with the Oklahoma National Guard, which activates during major tornado, flood, and ice storm events. The Oklahoma Mesonet, one of the most advanced weather observation networks in the world, provides real-time weather data that is critical during severe storm season. Sign up for your county and city emergency notification system to receive timely tornado and flood warnings. The American Red Cross of Oklahoma operates shelters and provides disaster relief services, and Oklahoma Voluntary Organizations Active in Disaster coordinates volunteer efforts during major events.

Oklahoma is home to 39 federally recognized tribal nations, and many tribal communities have their own emergency management departments and resources. Tribal members should register with both their tribal nation and county emergency management offices for alerts. Some tribal nations operate their own disaster relief programs and can provide assistance faster than federal agencies, particularly in remote areas. Remote tribal communities may face longer emergency response times and should maintain larger emergency supplies than standard recommendations. Take a CPR and first aid course through the Red Cross or a local fire department, and consider joining your community's Community Emergency Response Team program to receive training in disaster response. Frequently Asked Questions

How many tornadoes does Oklahoma experience per year?
Oklahoma averages approximately 56 tornadoes per year, making it one of the most tornado-prone states in the nation. The peak tornado season runs from April through June, but tornadoes can occur in any month. Oklahoma City and the surrounding metro area have been struck by more tornadoes than any other metropolitan area in the United States. The 2013 Moore tornado was an EF5 that killed 24 people and destroyed an elementary school.
What is the best type of tornado shelter for Oklahoma homes?
A reinforced underground storm cellar or safe room rated to EF5 tornado standards is the safest option for Oklahoma homes. FEMA and the Oklahoma Department of Emergency Management recommend installing a FEMA-compliant safe room in new construction. Above-ground safe rooms can also provide life-saving protection. If you have no shelter, go to the lowest interior room away from windows, such as a bathroom or closet. Mobile home residents must have an evacuation plan since mobile homes offer zero tornado protection.
What should be in a tornado emergency kit for Oklahoma?
An Oklahoma tornado kit should include a NOAA weather radio with battery backup and alarm function, helmets or thick blankets to protect against flying debris, sturdy shoes, a whistle to signal rescuers, heavy-duty work gloves, a flashlight with extra batteries, a basic first aid kit, sturdy boots, and copies of important documents. Since tornadoes often strike with minimal warning, keep this kit in your shelter location. Pack water, snacks, and any necessary medications in case you are trapped.
How do I prepare for Oklahoma ice storms?
Oklahoma ice storms can knock out power for days to weeks across large areas. The 2007 ice storm left over 600,000 Oklahomans without power for up to two weeks. Stock non-perishable food that requires no cooking, fill your bathtub with water before the storm for flushing toilets, keep vehicles fuel tanks above half, have blankets and sleeping bags rated to low temperatures, and charge all devices. Never use generators, gas grills, or charcoal indoors due to carbon monoxide poisoning risk.
Is wildfire a real threat in Oklahoma?
Yes, Oklahoma experiences significant wildfire risk, particularly in the western half of the state where grasslands and agricultural land create fire-prone conditions. Spring and fall are the most dangerous periods. The 2017 Starbution fire burned over 30,000 acres in Logan and Payne counties. Western Oklahoma communities face the highest risk, and drought conditions can escalate fire danger rapidly. Maintain defensible space around your home and keep a wildfire go-bag ready.
What Oklahoma emergency agencies should I know about?
The Oklahoma Department of Emergency Management coordinates disaster response statewide. The Oklahoma Mesonet weather network provides real-time weather data critical during severe storm season. Sign up for Oklahoma City and your county's emergency notification system. The Oklahoma National Guard activates during major disasters. Local fire departments and EMS are your first responders. The American Red Cross of Oklahoma operates shelters and disaster relief services. Save the FEMA app for real-time disaster alerts.
How should Indian Nation communities in Oklahoma prepare differently?
Oklahoma's 39 tribal nations have unique emergency management structures and resources. Many tribal communities have their own emergency management departments and mutual aid agreements with neighboring counties. Tribal members should register with both their tribal nation and county emergency management offices for alerts. Some tribal nations operate their own disaster relief programs and can provide assistance faster than federal agencies. Remote tribal communities may face longer emergency response times and should maintain larger emergency supplies.
