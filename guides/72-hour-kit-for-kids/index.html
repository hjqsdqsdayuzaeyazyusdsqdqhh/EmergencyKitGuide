72-Hour Kit for Kids: What Every Parent Needs to Know

Create a 72-hour emergency kit designed specifically for children. Age-appropriate supplies, comfort items, and safety essentials parents should pack.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Kids Need Their Own Emergency Kit

Children are among the most vulnerable members of any household during an emergency. Their smaller bodies require more water per pound of body weight, their immune systems are less developed, and they experience fear and anxiety more intensely than adults. A generic emergency kit designed for adults may not meet the specific nutritional, medical, and emotional needs of children at different developmental stages.

Providing your child with their own emergency kit does more than ensure they have the right supplies. It empowers them with a sense of control during a frightening situation. When a child knows exactly where their flashlight is, that their favorite stuffed animal is in their bag, and that they have their own water and snacks, it dramatically reduces panic and helps them cooperate with adults during evacuations or shelter-in-place scenarios. This guide walks you through every item a child's 72-hour kit should contain, organized by age group and emergency type.

Choosing the Right Backpack

The container you choose for your child's emergency kit matters more than you might think. For toddlers and preschoolers aged two to five, a small, brightly colored backpack or even a drawstring bag works well. Look for one with a wide zipper opening that little hands can manage independently. Avoid overly complex buckle systems or bags that require fine motor skills your child may not yet have.

For school-age children aged six to twelve, a standard child-sized backpack with padded straps is ideal. Make sure the bag is proportionate to your child's body so it does not cause strain when fully loaded. For teenagers, a compact daypack or hiking-style backpack works well and can hold more substantial supplies. Whatever bag you choose, add reflective tape or patches so your child is visible in low-light conditions. Attach a luggage tag with your child's name, your phone number, and an out-of-state emergency contact.

Water and Food Essentials for Children

Children dehydrate faster than adults and have higher caloric needs relative to their body weight during periods of stress. Pack at least half a gallon of water per child per day for drinking, which totals 1.5 gallons for a 72-hour period. If space permits, add an extra half gallon for mixing food or basic hygiene. Use lightweight collapsible water bottles that take up minimal space when empty.

For food, focus on familiar, non-perishable items your child already enjoys. Granola bars, peanut butter crackers, dried fruit, applesauce pouches, and cereal bars are excellent choices. Avoid foods that require cooking or large amounts of water. Include a supply of any formula, baby food, or specialized dietary items for younger children. Pack a small supply of comfort snacks like chocolate chips or gummy bears, as familiar treats can provide emotional reassurance during stressful times. Rotate the food supply every six months and check expiration dates regularly. Our 72-hour emergency food list has detailed recommendations for all age groups.

Clothing and Weather Protection

Pack one complete change of weather-appropriate clothing in a sealed waterproof bag. Include a pair of sturdy closed-toe shoes or sneakers, two sets of socks, underwear, a long-sleeve shirt, pants, and a light jacket or hoodie. In colder climates, add a warm hat, gloves, and an extra layer. For infants, pack additional onesies and a sleep sack.

Children grow quickly, so update the clothing in their kit at least twice a year. What fits in January will likely be too small by July. Include a lightweight emergency Mylar blanket sized for children, which provides warmth without adding significant weight. Rain ponchos designed for kids are inexpensive and pack flat. If your family lives in a disaster-prone area, consider storing seasonal clothing alternatives so your child is prepared for the specific weather hazards most likely to affect your region. First Aid Supplies for Kids

Children's first aid needs differ from adults in several important ways. Pediatric-sized bandages fit better on small fingers and arms. Include children's doses of pain relievers such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen, along with a dosing chart based on your child's weight. Add antihistamine medicine for allergic reactions, anti-diarrheal medication, and any prescription medications your child takes regularly.

Include adhesive bandages in fun colors or character designs, which can help distract and comfort a child after a minor injury. Pack medical tape, gauze pads, antibiotic ointment, burn cream, and a digital thermometer. Include a small bottle of children's sunscreen and insect repellent. Do not forget any EpiPens if your child has known allergies. Keep a laminated card with your child's medical information, blood type, allergies, and your pediatrician's contact number inside the kit. First aid knowledge is essential for the whole family, so also review our car first aid kit guide.

Comfort Items and Emotional Support

The emotional wellbeing of children during emergencies is just as important as their physical safety. Pack a small comfort item for each child. For toddlers and young children, this might be a favorite stuffed animal, a small blanket, or a pacifier. For older children, a family photo, a small journal, or a handwritten note from a parent can provide enormous comfort.

Include screen-free entertainment items such as a deck of cards, a small coloring book with crayons, a puzzle book, or a compact board game. A small MP3 player loaded with stories or music can keep children calm during long waits. Avoid packing items that require batteries or charging unless you also provide a power source. Let your child help choose their comfort items so they feel a sense of ownership over their kit. This participation builds confidence and makes them more likely to keep the kit accessible and ready.

Identification and Important Documents

In the chaos of an emergency, children can become separated from their parents. Ensure your child's kit contains a sealed waterproof pouch with photocopies of their birth certificate, medical insurance card, immunization records, a recent photo of your child, and a recent family photo showing your child with their parents. Write your full names, phone numbers, and an out-of-state emergency contact on a card and place it inside your child's pocket.

For older children and teenagers, include a laminated emergency contact card they can keep in their pocket or around their neck. Teach your child to memorize your phone number and full name by the age of five. Practice reciting this information regularly. Some families also place a identification bracelet on young children during evacuations that includes the child's name and parent contact information. This is especially useful during large-scale disasters when rescue workers may encounter many unaccompanied children.

Hygiene and Sanitation for Children

Good hygiene prevents illness during emergencies when medical care may be limited. Pack wet wipes, hand sanitizer, a small bar of soap or body wash, a child-sized toothbrush, toothpaste, and a comb. Include a sufficient supply of diapers and wipes for babies and toddlers. For children who are potty-training or recently toilet-trained, pack extra underwear and a portable potty seat.

Include plastic bags for soiled clothing or waste disposal. In situations where running water is unavailable, no-rinse body wash and waterless shampoo can help maintain cleanliness. Pack a small towel or microfiber cloth for each child. Feminine hygiene products should be included for pre-teen and teenage girls. These items may seem mundane, but maintaining a sense of cleanliness has a measurable positive effect on morale and emotional resilience during extended emergency situations.

Teaching Kids to Use Their Kit

A kit your child does not understand is a kit that will not help them when it matters most. Sit down with your child and go through every item in their backpack. Explain what each item is for and when they might need to use it. Let them practice turning on the flashlight, opening a snack, using the whistle, and wrapping a bandage on a stuffed animal.

Run family evacuation drills at least twice a year. Assign each child the responsibility of grabbing their own kit. Practice meeting at your designated family meeting point outside the home. Role-play common scenarios such as what to do if the house is filling with smoke, if an earthquake strikes, or if you become separated in a crowd. Keep the tone calm and empowering, never frightening. Children who practice emergency procedures feel more confident and are far more likely to respond effectively during a real event. Frequently Asked Questions

What age should a child start having their own emergency kit?
Children as young as three or four can begin learning about emergency kits and helping pack simple items. By age five to seven, most children can manage a small personal backpack with essentials like a flashlight, water bottle, snacks, and a comfort item. Teenagers should have a more complete kit that mirrors adult preparedness supplies, including basic first aid knowledge and communication tools.
How much should a child's emergency kit weigh?
A child's emergency kit should weigh no more than 10 to 15 percent of their body weight. For a 50-pound child, that means the entire kit should be five to seven pounds maximum. Choose lightweight items, use a properly fitted backpack, and avoid overpacking. The goal is a kit your child can comfortably carry during an evacuation without adult assistance.
What comfort items are most important for kids in emergencies?
Every child should have a small comfort item such as a stuffed animal, a favorite blanket, or a family photo. These items provide emotional security during stressful situations. For older children, include a small activity like a deck of cards, a puzzle book, or coloring supplies. Familiar items help reduce anxiety and provide a sense of normalcy when everything else feels chaotic.
Should I include electronics in my child's emergency kit?
A small, hand-crank or battery-powered radio can help children stay informed and entertained. Avoid including devices that require frequent charging unless you also pack a solar charger. A simple MP3 player loaded with audiobooks or music can be comforting. Screen-free options like coloring books, puzzle books, and card games are often more practical and reliable during extended power outages.
How do I teach my child to use their emergency kit?
Practice with your child by going through the kit together and explaining each item. Run evacuation drills so they know where the kit is stored and how to grab it quickly. Role-play scenarios where they use the flashlight, open a snack, or find their whistle. Make it a positive learning experience rather than something frightening. Children who understand their emergency kit feel more confident and less anxious during real emergencies.
