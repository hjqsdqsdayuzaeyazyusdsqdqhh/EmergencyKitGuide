Hurricane Evacuation Plan: Routes, Timing, and Go-Bag Strategy

Create a reliable hurricane evacuation plan with route planning, go-bag preparation, timing strategies, and shelter options for your family.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why a Hurricane Evacuation Plan Saves Lives

When a hurricane is bearing down on your coastline, every minute counts. Families who wait until the last moment to prepare often find themselves stuck in gridlocked traffic, running on empty fuel tanks, and scrambling for unavailable hotel rooms. A well-structured hurricane evacuation plan removes the guesswork from one of the most stressful decisions you will ever face. By mapping routes, pre-packing supplies, and establishing clear roles for every family member ahead of time, you dramatically reduce the chaos that accompanies a last-minute departure.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency recommends that every household in hurricane-prone regions develop a written evacuation plan well before June 1, the official start of Atlantic hurricane season. This plan should account for every family member, every pet, and every vehicle. It should identify multiple inland destinations, list critical phone numbers on paper, and specify where emergency supplies are stored so that anyone in the household can grab them and go. This guide walks you through each element of that plan in practical detail.

Understanding Your Evacuation Zone

The first step in any hurricane evacuation plan is knowing your designated evacuation zone. Most coastal counties divide their jurisdictions into lettered or numbered zones based on vulnerability to storm surge, flooding, and wind damage. Your zone does not depend on your home address alone; it depends on elevation, proximity to the coast, and the path of nearby waterways. Contact your county emergency management office or visit their website to determine your exact zone. Write it down, post it near your front door, and make sure every family member knows it by heart.

Mandatory evacuation orders are issued zone by zone, starting with the areas closest to the water. Even if your zone has not yet been called, conditions may change rapidly. If a storm intensifies or its track shifts, zones further inland may receive evacuation orders with little notice. Understanding your zone also helps you gauge how much time you have. Zone A residents, for example, are typically ordered to leave first and may have only 24 to 36 hours before conditions become dangerous. Residents in Zone C or D may have an additional 12 to 24 hours, but should not assume safety. Review our disaster preparedness plan for a broader framework that includes evacuation planning.

Planning Multiple Evacuation Routes

Relying on a single evacuation route is a common and dangerous mistake. When millions of people evacuate simultaneously, highways become parking lots. A single accident, bridge closure, or flooded overpass can strand your family for hours. Plan at least three distinct routes out of your area: a primary route using major highways heading inland, a secondary route using a different highway corridor, and a tertiary route that may use state roads or local streets to reach a completely different evacuation corridor. Each route should lead to a different predetermined destination.

Print detailed maps of each route and keep them in your vehicle and emergency kit. Do not rely solely on GPS or smartphone navigation, as cell towers may be overloaded or destroyed. Mark fuel stops along each route and note which towns have stations that may remain open during an evacuation. Also identify bridges and low-lying areas that could flood, so you can avoid them if conditions deteriorate. Drive each route at least once before hurricane season begins so that you are familiar with the turns, landmarks, and potential trouble spots. This kind of preparation is a natural extension of the strategies covered in our car emergency kit guide.

Timing Your Evacuation: When to Leave

Timing is the most critical variable in a hurricane evacuation. Leave too early and you may endure an unnecessary evacuation. Leave too late and you risk being caught on the road in dangerous conditions. The general consensus among emergency management professionals is to evacuate as soon as a mandatory evacuation order is issued for your zone, or ideally 48 to 72 hours before projected landfall if you anticipate heavy traffic. Monitor the National Hurricane Center advisories closely beginning when a tropical system enters the Gulf of Mexico or approaches the Atlantic seaboard.

Several indicators can help you decide to leave early even before an official order. If forecast models show your area within the cone of uncertainty, if a storm surge warning has been issued for your coast, or if local officials begin discussing voluntary evacuations, those are strong signals to begin your departure. Fuel availability is another factor. Gas stations along evacuation routes often run out of fuel within hours of a mandatory order. Filling your tank at the start of a hurricane watch ensures you will not be stranded. For additional preparedness strategies, consult our emergency preparedness checklist.

Building Your Hurricane Go-Bag

A hurricane go-bag is a portable kit containing everything your family needs for at least 72 hours away from home. Unlike a standard emergency kit designed for sheltering in place, a hurricane go-bag emphasizes portability, waterproofing, and documents. Pack each bag in a sturdy, water-resistant backpack or duffel bag. Include at least one gallon of water per person per day, non-perishable food for three days, a battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio, flashlights with extra batteries, a whistle, a first aid kit, and prescription medications for at least seven days.

Beyond survival essentials, your go-bag must contain critical documents. Place photocopies of photo IDs, insurance policies, birth certificates, social security cards, vehicle registrations, and bank information in a waterproof document bag. Include a USB drive with digital copies of all these documents as well. Pack cash in small bills, as ATMs and credit card machines will likely be offline. Do not forget comfort items for children, a change of weather-appropriate clothing for each family member, sturdy closed-toe shoes, and personal hygiene supplies. Our 72-hour kit guide provides a comprehensive supply list that adapts well to a portable go-bag format.

Vehicle Preparation and Fuel Strategy

Your vehicle is your lifeline during a hurricane evacuation. Begin preparing it as soon as a tropical system enters your region. Check tire pressure and tread depth, verify that your spare tire is properly inflated, top off windshield washer fluid, and ensure your brakes are in good condition. Pack a basic vehicle emergency kit including jumper cables, a flashlight, reflective triangles, a tire repair kit, and a portable air compressor. If you own an SUV or truck, you have an advantage in carrying additional supplies, but even a sedan can be loaded efficiently with careful planning.

Fuel strategy is equally important. Fill your tank completely the moment a hurricane watch is issued for your area. If possible, keep a five-gallon jerry can of fuel stored safely in your garage, as recommended by local fire codes. Many gas stations lose power during storms and cannot pump fuel even if their tanks are full. During major evacuations, fuel shortages develop quickly along evacuation corridors. Plan your fuel stops and carry enough fuel to reach your destination without depending on stations along the way. Take photographs of your vehicle before departure for insurance documentation. Review our road trip emergency kit guide for additional vehicle preparedness tips.

Pet Evacuation: Keeping Your Animals Safe

Pets are part of the family, and leaving them behind during a hurricane is both dangerous and illegal in some jurisdictions. Start preparing your pet evacuation plan at the beginning of hurricane season. Identify pet-friendly hotels along each of your evacuation routes and call ahead to confirm their pet policies. Many hotels fill up quickly during evacuations, so having a list of at least five options per route gives you flexibility. Keep a printed list of these hotels in your go-bag along with their phone numbers and addresses.

Pack a dedicated pet emergency kit containing at least three days of pet food, water, bowls, a leash and collar with updated identification tags, copies of vaccination records, any medications your pet requires, a pet carrier or crate large enough for your animal to stand and turn around, and a comfort item such as a favorite toy or blanket. If you have a larger animal such as a horse or livestock, coordinate with local agricultural extension offices or fairgrounds that may serve as temporary evacuation shelters for animals. Never attempt to evacuate with a pet in a crate that is not secured inside the vehicle, and never leave a pet unrestrained in a moving car. Our home emergency kit guide covers pet supplies in additional detail.

Shelter Options: Hotels, Friends, and Emergency Shelters

Your evacuation destination should be identified long before a storm threatens. The safest option is staying with friends or family members who live at least 100 miles inland in a area not prone to flooding. This provides free lodging and emotional support during a stressful event. Contact these contacts annually to confirm their willingness to host your family and to update their address and phone information. If friends and family are not available, pre-book hotel rooms in inland cities along your evacuation routes, ideally within driving distance of four to six hours.

Public emergency shelters are a last resort, but they are an essential safety net. The American Red Cross, in coordination with local emergency management agencies, opens shelters in schools, community centers, and churches when mandatory evacuations are ordered. These shelters provide basic accommodations including cots, food, water, and first aid, but they are crowded and offer limited privacy. Pets are typically not allowed in general population shelters, though some communities now operate separate pet shelters. Know the locations of shelters in your county and in the counties along your evacuation routes. Visit our home evacuation checklist for a complete rundown of what to do before you leave your house.

Family Reunification: What If You Get Separated

Even with the best planning, families can become separated during a hurricane evacuation. One family member may be at work, children may be at school, and traffic conditions may force different routes. Establish a family reunification plan that includes a primary meeting point outside your home and a secondary meeting point at your evacuation destination. Every family member, including older children, should know both meeting points by heart and have them written down in their personal go-bag.

Designate an out-of-state contact person whom all family members can call or text to report their status and location. Out-of-state lines often remain operational when local networks are overwhelmed. Program this contact into every family member's phone and write the number on a card kept in each go-bag. Practice your reunification plan at least twice a year, including a scenario where cell service is unavailable. Teach children to approach emergency personnel, such as police officers or firefighters, for help if they become separated from the family. Two-way radios provide reliable communication within a one-to-three-mile range when cell towers are down. For more on building a comprehensive family plan, see our family 72-hour emergency kit guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I evacuate during a hurricane?
You should evacuate as soon as a mandatory evacuation order is issued for your zone, or earlier if you feel unsafe. Ideally, begin evacuating 48 to 72 hours before the projected landfall to avoid traffic congestion and fuel shortages. Waiting until the last moment puts you at risk of being caught on congested highways with limited fuel and shelter options.
What is the difference between a mandatory and voluntary evacuation?
A mandatory evacuation means local authorities have ordered all residents in a specific zone to leave immediately. Emergency services will not be available once conditions deteriorate. A voluntary evacuation is a strong recommendation to leave but is not legally enforced. Regardless of the classification, leaving early is always the safest choice when a hurricane threatens your area.
Can I bring my pets when I evacuate?
Yes, you should always evacuate with your pets. Never leave animals behind. Pack a pet emergency kit with food, water, medications, vaccination records, a carrier or crate, leash, and comfort items. Identify pet-friendly hotels, boarding facilities, and emergency shelters along your evacuation route well before a storm approaches. Many communities now operate pet-inclusive shelters during hurricane events.
How many evacuation routes should I plan?
Plan at least three separate evacuation routes: a primary route heading inland, a secondary route using different highways, and a tertiary route that may use local roads to reach a completely different corridor. Each route should lead to a different destination at least 100 miles inland. Map these routes using both GPS and printed maps in case cell towers fail.
What do I do if roads are blocked during evacuation?
If your primary route is blocked, immediately switch to your predetermined alternate route. Stay calm and follow directions from law enforcement or traffic control personnel. Do not attempt to drive through flooded roads. If you are trapped in traffic, remain in your vehicle with seatbelts fastened and monitor your battery-powered weather radio for updates and alternative instructions from emergency management.
Should I board up my windows before evacuating?
Yes, if you have time and materials, install plywood storm shutters or commercial hurricane shutters on all windows before you leave. This protects your home from wind-borne debris and reduces the chance of catastrophic interior damage if the roof is compromised. If you do not have shutters, use 5/8-inch plywood panels cut to fit each window and secure them with concrete screws into the window frame.
What should I do with my vehicle during a hurricane?
Fill your gas tank completely at the first sign of a hurricane watch. Park your vehicle in a garage if available, or move it to higher ground away from flood-prone areas and trees that could fall on it. Take photographs of your vehicle's condition before you leave for insurance purposes. If you must leave your vehicle behind, park it away from power lines and low-lying areas.
Where should I go during a hurricane evacuation?
Evacuate inland to a location at least 100 miles from the coast or to a designated shelter in a safe zone. Pre-identify friends or family members inland who can host you, book hotel rooms in advance, and know the location of public emergency shelters. The American Red Cross and local emergency management agencies publish shelter locations when evacuations are ordered. Never stay in a mobile home during any hurricane.
