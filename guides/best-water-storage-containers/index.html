Best Water Storage Containers: Emergency Water Supply Compared

Compare the best water storage containers for emergency preparedness. From large barrels to portable bottles, find the right water storage solution.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Emergency Water Storage Is a Non-Negotiable Priority

Water is the most critical survival resource, and most households have shockingly little stored when an emergency strikes. FEMA recommends storing at least one gallon of water per person per day for a minimum of three days, but water supply disruptions from earthquakes, hurricanes, pipe breaks, or contamination events can last far longer. The average American household uses 300 gallons of water per day, but during an emergency when only drinking and basic sanitation needs must be met, one to two gallons per person per day is the standard guideline. Without adequate water storage, families face dehydration within days and serious health risks from contaminated water sources.

Choosing the right water storage containers is just as important as deciding how much to store. The wrong container can leach chemicals into your water, harbor bacterial growth, or prove too heavy to move when you need to evacuate. Containers also vary dramatically in capacity, portability, durability, and cost. A 55-gallon drum provides excellent long-term storage for families sheltering in place, while collapsible water containers and portable bottles are essential for evacuation scenarios. This guide compares every major category of water storage container, explains the materials and certifications that matter, and helps you build a water storage strategy that covers both sheltering and evacuation situations.

55-Gallon Water Drums: Maximum Capacity for Home Storage

Large 55-gallon water drums are the foundation of serious home water storage. A single drum provides enough water for one person for 55 days or a family of four for nearly two weeks. Made from high-density polyethylene (HDPE) plastic, these drums are designed specifically for long-term water storage and are manufactured from food-grade materials that will not impart taste or chemicals to the water. Blue is the standard color for water drums, and you should avoid repurposing drums that previously held chemicals or non-food substances.

The main advantages of 55-gallon drums are their capacity and cost efficiency. Per gallon of stored water, drums are among the cheapest container options available. They stack efficiently on pallets and can be stored in garages, basements, or utility rooms. The primary disadvantages are weight and immobility. A full 55-gallon drum weighs over 460 pounds, making it impossible to move without specialized equipment. This means drums are strictly for sheltering-in-place scenarios and cannot be part of an evacuation plan. Most drums require a drum pump to access the water, and some models include a spigot near the bottom for gravity-fed dispensing. Water Bricks and Stackable Containers: Versatile Mid-Range Options

Water Bricks and similar stackable containers fill the gap between small portable bottles and massive 55-gallon drums. These containers typically hold 3.5 gallons each and are designed to interlock for stable stacking. Made from food-grade polyethylene, they are BPA-free and designed for long-term water storage. A stack of ten Water Bricks stores 35 gallons of water in a compact footprint that fits in a closet, under a bed, or on garage shelving. Their modular design means you can start with a few and add more over time as your budget allows.

The versatility of Water Brick-style containers makes them popular with families who want flexible water storage. Unlike 55-gallon drums, individual containers can be carried to different locations as needed. A 3.5-gallon container weighs about 29 pounds when full, which is manageable for most adults. They can be stacked up to four high without additional support, making efficient use of vertical space. The main drawback compared to drums is the higher per-gallon cost and the need to treat and fill each container individually. Some families use Water Bricks for their primary storage and supplement with larger drums for bulk reserves. This hybrid approach provides both flexibility and capacity.

Collapsible Water Containers: Space-Saving Evacuation Solutions

Collapsible water containers are made from flexible, food-grade plastics that fold flat when empty, making them extremely space-efficient for storage. Most collapse to less than an inch thick, allowing you to store several in a drawer, closet, or emergency bag. When needed, they expand to hold five to seven gallons of water and include handles for carrying. Popular models use FDA-approved, BPA-free materials and feature leak-proof spigots or screw-top lids for easy dispensing.

The primary advantage of collapsible containers is their portability and storage efficiency. They are ideal for evacuation scenarios where you need to carry water but cannot spare the space for rigid containers. Many models fit flat in a suitcase, backpack, or car trunk alongside other emergency supplies. The trade-off is durability. Collapsible containers are not as robust as rigid alternatives and can puncture or leak if handled roughly or placed on rough surfaces. They are also not designed for long-term static storage, as the flexible material can develop weak points over time. The best approach is to use collapsible containers for evacuation and portable water needs while relying on rigid containers for stationary home storage.

Material Comparison: Food-Grade Plastic, Stainless Steel, and Glass

Food-grade polyethylene plastic is the most common material for emergency water storage containers. Look for containers marked with recycling code 2 (HDPE) or 5 (PP), as these plastics are specifically approved for food and water contact. HDPE is durable, lightweight, affordable, and resistant to impact and UV radiation. It does not impart taste or odor to water and can last for decades when properly maintained. The key requirement is BPA-free certification, as bisphenol A and similar chemicals can leach from lower-quality plastics into stored water over time.

Stainless steel containers offer excellent durability and complete chemical inertness, meaning they will never leach anything into your water regardless of storage duration. They are also naturally resistant to bacterial growth and can be thoroughly sanitized with boiling water or bleach solutions. The downsides are weight, cost, and opacity. Stainless steel containers weigh more than plastic alternatives, cost two to five times as much, and cannot be visually inspected for water level or clarity without opening. Glass containers are the most chemically inert option but are impractical for emergency storage due to their fragility, weight, and tendency to break during earthquakes or rough handling. For most families, food-grade HDPE plastic represents the best balance of safety, cost, and practicality.

Capacity Planning: How Much Water Storage Do You Actually Need

The baseline calculation for emergency water storage is one gallon per person per day. This covers drinking and minimal sanitation needs. For a family of four preparing for a two-week emergency, you would need 56 gallons of stored water. For a 30-day supply, that number doubles to 120 gallons. However, these are minimum figures. Hot climates, physical activity, illness, pregnancy, and nursing all increase water requirements. Most preparedness experts recommend planning for two gallons per person per day to account for cooking, basic hygiene, and the unpredictable nature of emergencies.

Practical capacity planning also considers your available storage space and what you can realistically fill and maintain. A full 55-gallon drum takes up about 23 inches of floor space and weighs over 460 pounds. Ten Water Bricks store 35 gallons in roughly the same floor area but at a fraction of the weight. Five-gallon stackable containers from brands like Scepter or Reliance offer a middle ground with easy handling and efficient stacking. Consider dividing your water storage between multiple locations: a primary supply at home, secondary supplies at a workplace or family member's house, and portable supplies in each vehicle. BPA-Free Considerations and Water Safety Certifications

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a chemical compound used in some plastics that has been linked to potential health effects including hormonal disruption. While the FDA has approved BPA for use in food containers, many consumers prefer to avoid it, especially for long-term water storage where the water sits in contact with the plastic for months or years. BPA-free containers use alternative materials like Tritan copolyester or specialized HDPE formulations that eliminate BPA while maintaining durability and food safety. Most reputable water storage manufacturers now offer BPA-free product lines as standard.

Beyond BPA, look for containers that carry NSF/ANSI 61 certification, which confirms the container meets standards for materials that come into contact with drinking water. FDA food-grade designation indicates the manufacturer uses materials approved for food contact. Some containers also carry state-level certifications from agencies like the California Department of Public Health. While these certifications are not strictly required for home water storage, they provide additional assurance that the container will not contaminate your water supply. Avoid repurposing containers that were not designed for water storage, such as milk jugs, juice containers, or chemical storage drums, as these may harbor residues that are difficult or impossible to remove completely.

Cleaning, Treatment, and Maintenance Protocols

Proper cleaning and treatment are essential for safe long-term water storage. New containers should be washed with a solution of one tablespoon of unscented liquid household bleach per gallon of water, rinsed thoroughly, and air-dried before filling. Fill containers with tap water that has been treated with one-eighth teaspoon (about 8 drops) of unscented liquid household bleach per gallon. Municipal tap water already contains chlorine, but adding a small amount provides extra protection against bacterial growth during long-term storage. Seal containers tightly and label them with the date filled.

Maintenance is equally important. Inspect water containers every six months for cracks, leaks, discoloration, or unusual odors. Replace any water that shows signs of contamination or off-putting taste or smell. Re-treat stored water annually by adding a fresh dose of bleach. Keep water storage containers away from direct sunlight, which can promote algae growth and degrade plastic over time. Store them off concrete floors using pallets or shelving, as concrete can absorb moisture and transfer it to the container, potentially promoting mold growth. If you use a water storage drum with a spigot, run water through the spigot occasionally to ensure it remains functional and clean.

Portability vs Capacity: Building a Multi-Layer Water Strategy

The most effective emergency water storage strategy uses multiple container types at different scales. Your home base should have the largest possible reserve using 55-gallon drums or large stackable containers. This provides the bulk of your water supply for sheltering-in-place scenarios where pipes are broken or water treatment plants are offline. Supplement this with mid-range containers like Water Bricks or five-gallon jugs that can be moved relatively easily if you need to relocate supplies within your home or to a neighbor's house.

For evacuation scenarios, include portable water containers in each family member's grab-and-go bag. Collapsible containers, personal water bottles, and hydration packs provide water during transport. Keep at least two gallons per person in your vehicle at all times in durable, leak-proof containers. Consider adding a water purification method to your emergency kit as a backup. Portable filters like the Sawyer Mini or Lifestraw, purification tablets, and unscented household bleach can extend your water supply by making suspect water sources safe to drink. Price Comparison and Budget-Friendly Options

Water storage costs vary significantly by container type and material. 55-gallon food-grade drums cost $50 to $100 each, making them the most affordable option per gallon stored. Water Brick-style containers run $8 to $15 each for 3.5 gallons, or about $2.50 to $4.30 per gallon of capacity. Collapsible containers cost $10 to $20 for five to seven gallons. Five-gallon stackable jugs range from $5 to $15 each. Stainless steel containers are the most expensive, often $30 to $80 for sizes comparable to plastic options. When budgeting, consider both the upfront cost and the long-term maintenance requirements.

A budget-friendly approach starts with whatever containers you can acquire affordably and grows over time. Many families begin with a few five-gallon jugs purchased at a hardware store for under $10 each and gradually upgrade to larger containers as their budget allows. Food-grade buckets with gamma seal lids offer an inexpensive alternative to purpose-built water containers, costing just a few dollars each at restaurant supply stores. The key is to start storing water now rather than waiting for the perfect container. Even a few extra gallons of stored water can make a significant difference during a short-term emergency. Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should I store for my family?
Store a minimum of one gallon per person per day for at least three days, though two weeks to one month is recommended for comprehensive preparedness. A family of four should store at least 12 gallons for three days, 28 gallons for two weeks, or 56 gallons for one month. For drinking comfort and basic hygiene, plan for two gallons per person per day, doubling these estimates. Pregnant women, nursing mothers, and people in hot climates may need even more.
What type of container is best for long-term water storage?
Food-grade HDPE (recycling code 2) plastic containers are the best choice for most families. They are durable, affordable, lightweight, and designed specifically for long-term water contact. Blue 55-gallon drums are ideal for large-scale home storage. Water Brick-style containers offer versatility with easier handling. For smaller portable needs, stainless steel bottles are extremely durable but more expensive. Avoid containers not designed for water storage like milk jugs, which can harbor bacteria.
How do I keep stored water safe to drink?
Fill containers with treated tap water and add one-eighth teaspoon of unscented liquid household bleach per gallon. Seal tightly, label with the date, and store in a cool, dark location away from sunlight and chemicals. Inspect every six months, replace water annually, and re-treat with fresh bleach. Keep containers off concrete floors and away from areas where pesticides or gasoline fumes could contaminate the supply. If water develops an off taste, re-treat or replace it.
Can I use my bathtub to store water in an emergency?
Yes, filling your bathtub with water immediately before a predicted emergency is a practical backup strategy. The average bathtub holds 80 to 100 gallons. However, bathtub water should be used within 24 to 48 hours since it is not sealed and can collect bacteria, dust, and other contaminants. Do not add bleach to bathtub water if you have young children or pets who might drink from it. Bathtub water is best for sanitation and cleaning rather than drinking.
Are collapsible water containers reliable enough for emergencies?
Collapsible containers are useful for evacuation and short-term portable water needs but are not ideal for long-term storage. They are lightweight and space-efficient when empty, making them perfect for go-bags and car kits. However, their flexible walls are more susceptible to punctures and leaks than rigid containers. Use them as part of a multi-layer water strategy alongside more durable rigid containers for primary storage.
How often should I replace or refresh my stored water?
Replace commercially bottled water according to the manufacturer's expiration date, which is typically one to two years from the date of packaging. For water you have stored yourself in containers, replace it every six to twelve months and re-treat with fresh bleach. Municipal tap water contains residual chlorine that provides some protection, but it is not enough for indefinite storage. Always re-treat and replace water that has developed an off color, taste, or odor.
What is the difference between water purification and water filtration?
Water filtration physically removes contaminants like sediment, bacteria, and protozoa by passing water through a porous barrier. Filters like Sawyer Mini or LifeStraw remove parasites and bacteria but may not eliminate viruses or chemical contaminants. Water purification uses chemicals like chlorine dioxide tablets or UV light to kill or inactivate microorganisms, including viruses. For comprehensive emergency preparedness, having both a filter and purification tablets provides the most versatile protection against a wide range of waterborne threats.
How much space do different water storage containers take up?
A 55-gallon drum takes up about 3.3 cubic feet of floor space. Ten Water Bricks storing 35 gallons occupy roughly 2.4 cubic feet. Five-gallon stackable jugs storing 30 gallons need about 2 cubic feet when stacked. Collapsible containers storing 25 gallons flat-pack into about 0.5 cubic feet but require rigid containers or a bathtub to hold the water when expanded. Consider your available storage space and choose a combination of container sizes that maximizes your water supply within the area you have available.
