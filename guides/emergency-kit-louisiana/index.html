Emergency Kit Louisiana: Hurricane, Flooding, and Gulf Coast Disaster Preparedness

Louisiana emergency kit guide covering hurricanes, catastrophic flooding, storm surge, extreme heat, and river flooding. Gulf Coast state disaster preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Understanding Louisiana's Unique Disaster Landscape

Louisiana sits at the intersection of some of the most destructive natural forces in North America. Bordered by the Gulf of Mexico to the south, carved by the Mississippi River, and battered annually by Atlantic hurricane season, the Pelican State faces a disaster profile unlike any other. Much of the state, including the New Orleans metropolitan area, lies at or below sea level, making it uniquely vulnerable to catastrophic flooding from multiple sources simultaneously. The lessons learned from Hurricane Katrina in 2005 and Hurricane Ida in 2021 have reshaped emergency preparedness across the region, but the fundamental risks remain.

Building an effective emergency kit for Louisiana requires understanding these layered threats. You are not just preparing for one type of disaster but for the possibility that a hurricane brings storm surge, river flooding, wind damage, prolonged power outages, and extreme heat all at once. The Louisiana Governor's Office of Homeland Security and Emergency Preparedness (GOHSEP) coordinates state-level response, but individuals and families must be prepared to sustain themselves for extended periods. This guide walks you through every supply category you need for Gulf Coast survival, from waterproof document storage to heat illness prevention.

Hurricane Preparedness: The Foundation of Louisiana Readiness

Hurricane season runs from June 1 through November 30, with the peak threat period from August through October. Louisiana has been struck by more major hurricanes than almost any other state, and the devastation caused by recent storms has demonstrated that even well-prepared communities can face weeks without normal services. A standard 72-hour emergency kit is the minimum starting point, but Louisiana residents should plan for at least seven to fourteen days of self-sufficiency. Power restoration after a major hurricane can take two weeks or longer, particularly in rural parishes south of Interstate 10.

Your hurricane kit should include a battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio to receive emergency broadcasts when cell towers and internet service fail. Pack at least two fully charged portable power banks, one for each adult family member, along with charging cables for all devices. Heavy-duty tarps, rope, and a basic tool kit allow you to make temporary roof repairs if your home sustains wind damage. Consider including a portable generator fuel plan if you own a generator, and never operate generators indoors or in enclosed spaces due to deadly carbon monoxide accumulation. Water Storage and Purification for Flood-Prone Areas

Water is the most critical supply in any emergency kit, but Louisiana faces a cruel irony during floods: there may be water everywhere, but none of it is safe to drink. Hurricane-driven storm surge contaminates municipal water systems with saltwater and sewage. River flooding carries agricultural runoff, industrial chemicals, and pathogens. After Hurricane Katrina, New Orleans residents went weeks without safe tap water. Store at least one gallon of water per person per day for drinking, and an additional half gallon for sanitation and cooking. A family of four should store a minimum of 28 gallons for a seven-day supply.

In addition to stored water, include multiple purification methods in your kit. Water purification tablets provide a lightweight backup, and a portable filter like a Sawyer Squeeze or LifeStraw can make questionable water sources drinkable. Avoid using flood water for any purpose including cleaning wounds, as it may contain harmful bacteria, viruses, and chemical contaminants. If you rely on a well water system, understand that flooding can contaminate your well for months. Evacuation Planning and the Contraflow System

Louisiana has one of the most complex evacuation systems in the nation. When a major hurricane threatens the New Orleans metropolitan area, the state activates contraflow on Interstate 10, reversing all lanes to carry traffic outbound. This system was first used during Hurricane Georges in 1998 and has been refined since. However, contraflow creates massive traffic congestion, and residents in outlying parishes may not have access to this system. Early evacuation is critical. The difference between leaving 48 hours before landfall versus 24 hours can mean the difference between a manageable drive and hours of gridlock on a highway with limited fuel and services.

Your evacuation kit should be a separate, portable version of your main emergency supply. Pack each family member a grab-and-go bag containing a three-day supply of water, non-perishable food, medications, a change of clothing, important documents in a waterproof pouch, cash, a flashlight, and a portable phone charger. Keep vehicle emergency supplies current including jumper cables, a spare tire, basic tools, and extra fuel stored safely. Identify at least two evacuation destinations, one inland and one out of state, and confirm each with the people you plan to stay with. Pre-book pet-friendly hotels along your route if you have animals, as shelters in Louisiana accept pets in designated areas but capacity is limited.

Protecting Against Extreme Heat During Power Outages

Louisiana's summers bring average high temperatures in the low to mid-90s with humidity levels that routinely exceed 80 percent. When a hurricane knocks out power for days or weeks, the combination of heat and humidity creates life-threatening conditions, particularly for the elderly, young children, and those with chronic health conditions. Heat-related illness killed more people after Hurricane Katrina than the storm itself. Your emergency kit must address cooling as a primary survival need, not an afterthought.

Include battery-powered or USB-powered fans, cooling towels that activate with water, electrolyte replacement packets, and a supply of lightweight, loose-fitting clothing. Plan to relocate to the lowest interior room of your home during the hottest hours if air conditioning is unavailable, as heat rises within a structure. If your home has a basement, that may be the coolest refuge. Identify cooling centers in your community that may open during extended power outages, though access may be limited. Never leave children or pets in parked vehicles, and check on elderly neighbors who may be unable to cool themselves safely. Flood-Proofing Your Documents and Valuables

Louisiana's position as the most flood-prone state in the nation means that document protection is not optional, it is essential. Many residents learned this the hard way during Hurricane Katrina when irreplaceable documents were destroyed by weeks of submersion in contaminated floodwater. Your emergency document kit should be stored in a waterproof, submersible container rated for complete immersion. Place original documents inside sealed waterproof bags within the container for redundant protection. Essential documents include photo identification for all family members, homeowner and flood insurance policies, property deeds, vehicle titles, birth certificates, Social Security cards, and medical records including prescription information.

Maintain digital copies of all documents on an encrypted USB drive stored in a separate location from the waterproof container, and upload copies to secure cloud storage. Photograph your home's interior and all valuable possessions annually to support insurance claims. Store these photographs in the cloud rather than solely on your phone. Keep at least $300 in cash in small denominations in your emergency kit, as ATMs and credit card machines will be nonfunctional during extended power outages. Louisiana's frequent flooding events make this level of document preparedness a year-round necessity, not just a hurricane season precaution.

Food Storage for Extended Louisiana Emergencies

Louisiana's food supply chain is vulnerable to disruption during hurricane season. Ports along the Mississippi River may close, trucking routes through flood-prone areas become impassable, and local grocery stores are emptied within hours of an evacuation notice. Plan for a minimum of seven days of non-perishable food per person, with two thousand calories per adult daily. Focus on foods that require no refrigeration, minimal preparation, and can be consumed without heating, since natural gas service may be interrupted and outdoor cooking equipment should never be used indoors.

Canned meats, peanut butter, crackers, granola bars, dried fruit, canned fruits and vegetables, powdered milk, and ready-to-eat meals are all excellent choices for Louisiana conditions. Pack a manual can opener, disposable plates, utensils, and wet wipes for hygiene. Consider the cultural reality that Louisiana residents may prefer certain regional foods during stressful times, so include comfort foods your family enjoys. Store food in a cool, dry location and rotate the supply every six months. In the extreme Louisiana heat, check stored food more frequently for signs of spoilage or melting. Medical Supplies and Coastal Health Hazards

Louisiana's warm, humid climate creates unique medical challenges during emergencies. Floodwater exposure causes skin infections, gastrointestinal illness, and wound contamination. Standing water after storms breeds mosquitoes that carry West Nile virus and other diseases. Mold growth in flooded homes creates respiratory hazards that persist for months. Your first aid kit should go beyond basic bandages and pain relievers to address these coastal-specific health threats. Include waterproof wound closure strips, antibiotic ointment, antifungal cream, hydrocortisone cream for insect bites and skin irritation, and a supply of over-the-counter anti-diarrheal medication.

Maintain a seven-day supply of all prescription medications, as pharmacies may be closed or inaccessible for extended periods. Louisiana residents near the petrochemical corridor between Baton Rouge and New Orleans should include N95 respirator masks, protective eyewear, and plastic sheeting with duct tape in their kits for potential chemical release scenarios during storms. Include insect repellent with DEET or picaridin for mosquito protection after flooding. Keep a basic first aid manual in your kit and consider taking a wilderness first aid course, as professional medical help may be delayed during major disasters. Subsidence, Coastal Erosion, and Long-Term Threats

Louisiana faces a slow-motion disaster that compounds every storm season: the land itself is sinking. Subsidence, caused by sediment compaction, oil and gas extraction, and the absence of natural river delta replenishment, means that areas which were once above sea level are now vulnerable to flooding during normal high tides. Coastal erosion has removed hundreds of square miles of protective wetlands that once buffered communities from storm surge. The National Weather Service has coined the term "sunny day flooding" to describe tidal flooding events that occur even without storms. These long-term changes mean that your emergency preparedness must account for worsening conditions over time.

Evaluate your property's flood risk annually using updated FEMA flood maps, which are periodically revised to reflect changing conditions. If you live in an area that has been reclassified to a higher flood risk zone, adjust your emergency kit and evacuation plan accordingly. Consider flood insurance through the National Flood Insurance Program if you do not already have coverage, as standard homeowner policies do not cover flood damage. Elevate critical utilities and consider flood-resistant building materials for home improvements. The Cajun Navy and other volunteer organizations have demonstrated the power of community resilience in Louisiana, but individual preparedness remains the foundation of survival.

Special Considerations: Pets, Elderly Residents, and Community Resources

Louisiana's pet ownership rates are among the highest in the nation, and many residents learned during Hurricane Katrina that pet evacuation planning is not optional. Designated pet-friendly shelters exist but have limited capacity, and many residents refused to evacuate because they could not bring their animals. Pack a pet emergency kit including at least seven days of pet food, water, medications, vaccination records, a sturdy leash and carrier, waste bags, and a comfort item like a favorite blanket. Microchip your pets and keep identification tags current with your cell phone number, not your home number.

Elderly residents face disproportionate risks during Louisiana emergencies due to heat sensitivity, medication dependence, limited mobility, and potential isolation. Check on elderly neighbors and family members before every storm. Ensure they have functional medical alert devices that work during power outages, backup power for any medical equipment they depend on, and a written emergency plan that includes their specific needs. Rural communities in Acadiana and the Florida Parishes may experience extended isolation during flooding events, making self-sufficiency even more critical. Register with your local Office of Emergency Preparedness for special needs assistance if you or a family member requires evacuation help. Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I be prepared to shelter in place during a Louisiana hurricane?
Louisiana residents should prepare for at least seven days of self-sufficiency during hurricane season. While a standard 72-hour kit is essential, the reality of Gulf Coast hurricanes is that power restoration, road clearing, and supply chain recovery often take a week or longer. After Hurricane Ida, many residents in the New Orleans metro area were without power for over two weeks during extreme summer heat. Stock additional water, non-perishable food, and battery-powered fans beyond the standard three-day supply.
What makes Louisiana flooding different from other states?
Louisiana faces a unique combination of flooding threats that most states do not experience simultaneously. The state sits largely at or below sea level, particularly the New Orleans metropolitan area, which depends on an extensive levee and pump system to stay dry. Residents face storm surge from the Gulf of Mexico, river flooding from the Mississippi River and its tributaries, flash flooding from heavy rainfall, and subsidence-related flooding as the land literally sinks. This multi-layered flood risk means Louisiana emergency kits must include waterproof document storage, elevated communication devices, and plans for both vertical evacuation to upper floors and horizontal evacuation out of flood zones.
Should I evacuate or shelter in place during a hurricane in Louisiana?
This depends on your location relative to levee systems, your home construction, and the storm's projected track. Mandatory evacuation orders should always be followed. If you live outside the levee protection system, in a flood-prone area, or in a mobile home, evacuate early. Louisiana activates contraflow on Interstate 10 during major evacuations, reversing all lanes outbound to speed departure. Know your evacuation zone and route before storm season. If you shelter in place inside the levee system, prepare for extended power outages, water system disruptions, and potential isolation from rescue services for several days.
What specific supplies are essential for Louisiana's extreme heat during power outages?
Louisiana's summer heat and humidity create life-threatening conditions when air conditioning fails. Your kit should include battery-powered or USB-powered fans, cooling towels, electrolyte packets, and at least one gallon of water per person per day for drinking alone. Pack waterproof tarps for shade creation, reflective emergency blankets to deflect heat from windows, and lightweight breathable clothing. If you have medical conditions exacerbated by heat, such as heart disease or diabetes, include a seven-day supply of medications and consult your doctor about a heat illness action plan. Never use outdoor cooking equipment indoors due to carbon monoxide poisoning risk.
How do I prepare my important documents for Louisiana flood risk?
Store all critical documents in a waterproof, fireproof container rated for submersion. Place original documents in sealed waterproof bags inside the container. Maintain digital copies on an encrypted USB drive stored separately and in secure cloud storage. Essential documents include photo identification, insurance policies (especially flood insurance through NFIP), property deeds, vehicle titles, birth certificates, Social Security cards, medical records, and a recent family photo for identification purposes. Photograph your home's interior and contents annually for insurance claims. Louisiana's flood risk means paper copies alone are not sufficient.
What role does the Cajun Navy play in Louisiana emergency response?
The Cajun Navy is a volunteer rescue organization that emerged after Hurricane Katrina and has become an essential part of Louisiana's disaster response infrastructure. These civilian volunteers with boats and high-water vehicles conduct water rescues when official first responders are overwhelmed. While you should not rely on volunteer organizations for your survival plan, knowing how to contact them and understanding their role helps you plan realistically. Register with local emergency management and download the Cajun Navy app before storm season. Their efforts during Hurricane Harvey and Hurricane Ida saved thousands of lives, but response times vary by storm severity and area.
Do I need special emergency supplies if I live near Louisiana's petrochemical corridor?
Residents along the Mississippi River petrochemical corridor between Baton Rouge and New Orleans face additional hazards during hurricanes and floods. Industrial facility damage can release toxic chemicals into air and water. Your emergency kit should include N95 respirator masks, protective eyewear, and plastic sheeting with duct tape to seal windows and doors during a chemical release. Monitor the EPA's AirNow system and local emergency broadcast channels for air quality alerts. Keep vehicles fueled above half-tank during hurricane season for rapid evacuation. If you smell unusual odors or receive a shelter-in-place order for chemical exposure, seal your home immediately and move to an interior room above ground level.
How do Louisiana's levee systems affect my emergency planning?
The post-Katrina levee system improvements provide significantly better protection than before 2005, but no levee system is indestructible. The Hurricane and Storm Damage Risk Reduction System protects specific areas, but residents should understand that levee protection levels vary by location and storm intensity. A Category 4 or 5 hurricane can overtop or breach levee sections. Your emergency plan should include both shelter-in-place provisions within the levee system and evacuation routes to areas outside it. Know the specific design storm level for your neighborhood's levee protection and plan accordingly. Never assume levees guarantee safety.
