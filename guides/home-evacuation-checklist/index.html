Home Evacuation Checklist: What to Grab in an Emergency

A complete home evacuation checklist covering fires, floods, hurricanes, and natural disasters. Know exactly what to grab, which documents to save, and how to evacuate safely with your entire family.

Evacuation Basics: The First Five Minutes

When disaster strikes and you need to leave your home immediately, every second counts. Families who have rehearsed their evacuation plan leave calmly and safely. Families who have not waste critical minutes arguing about what to grab, searching for keys, or scrambling to find shoes in the dark. The difference between these two scenarios is preparation.

The first rule of evacuation is simple: people first, possessions second. Never delay leaving to pack a bag, save a sentimental item, or wait for conditions to change. If authorities issue an evacuation order, leave immediately. If you smell gas, see fire approaching, or hear emergency sirens, do not stop to gather anything except your pre-packed go-bag and your family.

Keep your go-bags stored near your home's main exit so you can grab them on the way out the door. Every bag should contain at least 72 hours of water, food, first aid supplies, a flashlight, a phone charger, cash, copies of essential documents, a change of clothes, and personal medications. Store car keys, wallets, and the document bag in the same location so you never waste time searching for them during an emergency. For a complete supply list, see our emergency supplies list.

Priority Items to Grab in an Emergency

When evacuation time is limited, you cannot take everything. Prioritization is essential. The items you should grab first fall into three tiers. Tier one includes life-sustaining items: your go-bags, medications, and eyeglasses. Tier two includes irreplaceable items: the document bag, family photos, and a backup hard drive. Tier three includes practical items: extra clothing, pet supplies, and tools.

In a sudden evacuation like a house fire or flash flood, grab only tier one items. You have seconds, not minutes. In a planned evacuation like a hurricane with a 24 to 48 hour warning window, you can gather tier two and tier three items as well. Do not waste time packing items that are easily replaceable like kitchen appliances, books, or clothing.

Keep a written prioritized grab list on the refrigerator and inside each go-bag. When adrenaline is high and time is short, a written list eliminates decision-making and tells you exactly what to do. Post the list at eye level so every family member can see it. Review and update the list whenever you acquire new valuables or important documents. For guidance on building the core kit you will grab, see our home emergency kit guide.

Documents Go-Bag: What to Save

Documents are among the hardest things to replace and among the easiest to prepare for in advance. Your documents go-bag should be a waterproof, fireproof bag stored in an accessible location. It should contain government-issued IDs, birth certificates, Social Security cards, insurance policies, medical records, property deeds or leases, bank statements, and a USB drive with digital copies of everything.

Store at least $200 in small bills inside the document bag. Cash is essential during power outages when ATMs and card readers stop working. Many gas stations and stores will only accept cash when the grid is down. Keep the bills in denominations of $5, $10, and $20 because change may not be available.

Digitize every document and store the files in at least two cloud services. Take photographs of each document with your phone and save them to a secure cloud folder. Keep a second USB drive in your vehicle's glove compartment. If you have time during a planned evacuation, photograph rooms and their contents for insurance purposes. A quick walk-through video of your home can be invaluable when filing a claim later. For document protection details, see our disaster preparedness plan guide.

Water and Food During Evacuation

During an evacuation you may be stuck in traffic for hours, sheltering in a car, or waiting at an emergency center with limited supplies. Having water and food accessible prevents dehydration and hunger during what is already an extremely stressful event. Pack at least one gallon of water per person for the first day of evacuation and non-perishable snacks that require no preparation.

The best evacuation foods are lightweight and calorie-dense: granola bars, energy bars, peanut butter crackers, trail mix, dried fruit, and jerky. Avoid foods that melt, require refrigeration, or need a can opener. For families with infants, pack enough formula and baby food for at least 72 hours. Include disposable utensils, napkins, and a bottle of hand sanitizer.

If you have time before evacuating, fill your vehicle's gas tank and load a cooler with ice, water bottles, and perishable snacks. A full tank of gas and a cooler of supplies gives you significantly more flexibility and range during an evacuation. Know the locations of gas stations and rest stops along your evacuation routes so you can refuel and rest before supplies run out. For more on food planning, see our emergency food storage guide.

Medical and First Aid Supplies

A compact first aid kit is non-negotiable in any evacuation go-bag. Injuries happen during emergencies: cuts from broken glass, burns from cooking over alternative heat, sprains from navigating debris. Your kit should include adhesive bandages, antiseptic wipes, gauze pads, medical tape, pain relievers, antihistamines, tweezers, scissors, nitrile gloves, and a CPR face shield.

Most importantly, include a 30-day supply of every prescription medication that any household member takes. After a disaster, pharmacies may be closed, inaccessible, or out of stock for days or weeks. Talk to your doctor before disaster season about obtaining an emergency reserve supply. Keep a printed medication list in the document bag showing drug names, dosages, and prescribing physicians.

Additional medical considerations include extra eyeglasses (a spare pair is essential if your primary pair breaks), hearing aid batteries, inhalers for asthmatics, EpiPens for severe allergies, glucose monitors and insulin for diabetics, and a basic first aid manual. For a detailed medical kit breakdown, check our car first aid kit guide, which covers portable medical supplies that work equally well during evacuations.

Clothing and Personal Gear

Pack one complete change of clothing for every family member in your evacuation bags. Choose weather-appropriate layers. Include long pants, a long-sleeve shirt, sturdy closed-toe shoes, a warm jacket, socks, and underwear. In cold climates, add thermal underwear, gloves, a hat, and wool socks. In hot climates, prioritize lightweight, moisture-wicking fabrics and sun protection.

Personal hygiene items help maintain comfort and morale during a stressful evacuation. Pack toothbrushes, toothpaste, soap, hand sanitizer, deodorant, feminine hygiene products, baby wipes, a small towel, and any personal care items you depend on daily. Dust masks (N95 if available) protect against smoke, ash, and debris. A pair of work gloves protects your hands during cleanup or debris navigation.

Include a sleeping bag or emergency space blanket per person. Emergency space blankets are compact, weigh almost nothing, and reflect up to 90 percent of body heat. A headlamp or small flashlight with extra batteries is essential because evacuations frequently occur at night or in dark conditions. Pack a whistle for signaling and a small amount of entertainment for children: a deck of cards, a coloring book, or a favorite stuffed animal. Evacuating with Pets

Pets are family, and your evacuation plan must include them. In an emergency, a panicked animal is harder to catch and transport. Practice evacuating with your pets regularly so they become comfortable with carriers, leashes, and car travel during stressful situations. Keep pet carriers, leashes, and harnesses near your home's main exit so they are immediately accessible.

Pack a separate pet go-bag containing at least three days of food and water, any medications your pet needs, vaccination records, a recent photo of your pet for identification, a comfort item like a favorite toy or blanket, waste bags, and a portable food and water bowl. If you have a cat, include a small litter box and litter. If you have a dog, include a sturdy leash and a collar with current ID tags.

Identify pet-friendly hotels, boarding facilities, and veterinary clinics along your evacuation routes before disaster strikes. Many emergency shelters do not allow animals, so you need a backup plan. Microchip your pets and keep registration information current. A microchip is the most reliable way to reunite with a lost pet. For comprehensive pet emergency planning, see our home emergency kit guide.

Securing Your Home Before Evacuating

If you have advance notice of an evacuation, take steps to protect your home while you are away. Secure all windows and doors. If you are evacuating for a hurricane, install hurricane shutters or board windows with plywood. Move valuable items to upper floors if flooding is a risk. Bring outdoor furniture, trash cans, and decorations inside so they do not become projectiles in high winds.

Turn off utilities if authorities instruct you to do so. Shut off the electricity at the main breaker, close the gas valve with a wrench, and turn off the water main. Never turn off gas if you smell a leak. Leave immediately and contact the utility company from a safe location. Photograph the condition of your home's exterior before leaving for insurance documentation.

Set your home security system if it has battery backup. Notify a trusted neighbor or friend that you are evacuating and when you expect to return. Give them your contact information and ask them to report any suspicious activity or damage to authorities. Collect your mail or have someone collect it for you, because overflowing mailboxes signal that the home is vacant. Lock all doors and close all curtains and blinds. For more on securing your home, see our home fire prevention tips.

Vehicle Evacuation Readiness

Your vehicle is your evacuation lifeline. Keep it maintained and ready to travel at a moment's notice. During disaster season, never let the fuel tank drop below half full. A full tank provides significantly more range and options during an evacuation when gas stations along your route may be closed, out of fuel, or experiencing extremely long lines.

Keep a vehicle emergency kit in your trunk at all times. This should include jumper cables, a tire repair kit, a portable air compressor, basic tools, a flashlight, reflective triangles, a first aid kit, a blanket, and extra water. For a comprehensive roadside kit, see our car emergency kit checklist. Having a reliable vehicle emergency kit means you can handle a breakdown during your evacuation without being stranded.

Practice loading your vehicle quickly. Know how many bags fit in the trunk, whether the back seat can accommodate additional items, and whether your vehicle can tow a small trailer if needed. Store a paper map of your region in the glove compartment because GPS and cell service may be unavailable. Identify at least two gas stations along each evacuation route and plan fuel stops accordingly. If you have a four-wheel-drive vehicle, know its capabilities for navigating flooded or debris-covered roads.

After the Evacuation: What Comes Next

Recovering from a disaster is a process that takes weeks or months. When it is safe to return home, do not rush back. Follow official guidance and wait for authorities to confirm that your area is safe. When you do return, approach your home cautiously. Check for structural damage, gas leaks, downed power lines, and standing water before entering.

Document all damage thoroughly with photographs and video before moving anything. Contact your insurance company as soon as possible to start the claims process. Keep all receipts for temporary housing, food, and other expenses incurred during the evacuation. These costs may be reimbursable under your insurance policy or through FEMA assistance programs.

Take care of your family's emotional health after a disaster. Children may exhibit anxiety, nightmares, or behavioral changes. Adults may experience stress, difficulty sleeping, or feelings of helplessness. Talk openly about the experience, maintain routines as much as possible, and seek professional help if needed. The American Red Cross and local mental health organizations offer free disaster counseling services. Frequently Asked Questions

A well-rehearsed family should be able to evacuate in under five minutes from the moment the decision is made to leave. In a fire scenario, you may have less than two minutes to exit safely. This is why practicing your evacuation plan is so critical. Keep go-bags packed and stored near the main exit, keys and wallets in a designated spot, and vehicles facing outward in the driveway for a fast departure.
Priority documents include government IDs (driver's licenses, passports, birth certificates), Social Security cards, insurance policies (home, auto, health, life), medical records, property deeds or lease agreements, bank and credit card information, and recent tax returns. Store originals in a fireproof document bag and keep digital copies in cloud storage. Always grab the document bag first during an evacuation since these items are the hardest to replace.

For planned evacuations like hurricanes, turn off electricity at the main breaker, close the gas valve with a wrench, and shut off the water main. Never attempt to turn off gas if you smell a leak. Leave immediately and call the gas company from a safe location.

Practice evacuation drills at night with the lights off so children learn to navigate in the dark. Write the family meeting spot address on a card in each child's backpack.

Pack a pet go-bag with at least three days of food and water, medications, vaccination records, a recent photo, and a comfort item. During an evacuation, secure pets in their carriers before loading the car. Never leave pets behind. Identify pet-friendly hotels and boarding facilities along your evacuation route in advance.
