Emergency Kit Washington: Cascadia Earthquake, Wildfire, and Storm Preparedness

Washington state emergency kit guide for Cascadia earthquake risk, wildfire season, winter storms, flooding, and volcanic hazards. State-specific preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Washington State's Multi-Hazard Threat Profile

Washington state faces one of the most diverse and severe threat profiles of any state in the nation. The Cascadia Subduction Zone offshore threatens a magnitude 9.0 earthquake that could cause catastrophic damage from the coast to the Cascade Mountains. Mount Rainier, one of the most dangerous volcanoes in the world, looms over the Tacoma and Puyallup areas with lahar risk that could destroy entire valleys. Eastern Washington endures annual wildfire seasons that burn hundreds of thousands of acres, while western Washington faces severe winter storms, flooding, and the ongoing risk of volcanic ashfall. The Washington Emergency Management Division coordinates preparedness across all these threats, but the scale of potential disasters means every household must be ready to survive independently for an extended period.

Washington's geography creates distinct risk zones across the state. The Puget Sound lowlands from Everett to Tacoma house over 4 million people in earthquake-vulnerable areas. The Cascade Range contains five active volcanic peaks. The eastern plains face wildfire and extreme winter weather, and the San Juan Islands and other Puget Sound island communities depend on ferries that may stop running during emergencies. Whether you live in a Seattle high-rise, a Spokane suburb, or a farm in the Yakima Valley, this guide addresses the specific emergencies you are most likely to face.

Cascadia Subduction Zone Earthquake: Seattle and Puget Sound

The Cascadia Subduction Zone runs from Northern California to Vancouver Island and last ruptured on January 26, 1700. When it breaks again, the resulting magnitude 9.0 earthquake will produce 3 to 5 minutes of violent shaking across western Washington, followed by tsunami waves along the coast. The Puget Sound region faces additional risks from liquefaction, particularly in areas with filled or unstable soils like parts of Seattle's waterfront and industrial districts. Many buildings in Seattle, Tacoma, and Olympia were built before modern seismic codes and could suffer significant damage. The Seattle fault line running through the city adds another layer of seismic risk independent of the Cascadia event.

Every Washington household west of the Cascades should maintain a minimum 14-day emergency supply. Build a comprehensive earthquake emergency kit including heavy-duty work gloves, a pry bar for debris removal, sturdy boots, and a gas shutoff wrench. Store water at a rate of one gallon per person per day for at least 14 days, since municipal water systems may be severely damaged. Seattle high-rise residents should keep supplies on every floor near stairwells and know the building's seismic safety features. Secure heavy furniture, water heaters, and appliances with earthquake straps and brackets.

Mount Rainier Lahar Risk and Volcanic Preparedness

Mount Rainier is considered one of the most dangerous volcanoes in the United States due to its massive glacial ice cap and proximity to the Seattle-Tacoma metropolitan area. A volcanic eruption could trigger lahars, which are fast-moving flows of volcanic debris, mud, and water that travel down river valleys at speeds up to 50 miles per hour, destroying everything in their path. The Puyallup, Carbon, White, and Cowlitz river valleys are designated lahar evacuation zones, with the potential to affect hundreds of thousands of residents. Lahar sirens are installed throughout these valleys, but residents should not wait for an alarm, since lahars can be triggered by eruptions, earthquakes, or even heavy rainfall destabilizing volcanic deposits.

If you live in a lahar zone, know your evacuation route to high ground and practice it with your family at least twice a year. Keep a go-bag by every door with sturdy shoes, a flashlight, identification, medications, and water. During any significant earthquake or volcanic activity near Mount Rainier, move to high ground immediately without waiting for official warnings. Washington also has four other active volcanoes including Mount St. Helens, Mount Baker, Glacier Peak, and Mount Adams. Communities near these peaks should also prepare for ashfall scenarios that could contaminate water supplies and damage vehicles and machinery.

Eastern Washington Wildfire Season

Eastern Washington experiences annual wildfire seasons that have grown in severity and duration over the past two decades. Communities in Okanogan, Chelan, Kittitas, and Yakima counties face regular evacuations as fires burn through grasslands, shrubsteppe, and forest across the region. The 2020 Labor Day wind event drove fires across hundreds of thousands of acres in a single weekend, destroying homes and forcing mass evacuations. Smoke from fires across eastern Washington and neighboring states can blanket the entire Puget Sound region for weeks, creating hazardous air quality that threatens anyone with respiratory conditions.

Follow our wildfire preparedness guide for creating defensible space and planning evacuations. Include N95 respirator masks, protective goggles, and a portable air purifier in your home emergency kit. Register for your county's emergency alert system, keep your vehicle fueled above half during fire season, and maintain go-bags by every exit. If you have asthma or other respiratory conditions, plan to evacuate early when fire activity increases rather than waiting for mandatory orders.

Western Washington Winter Storms and Flooding

Western Washington receives significant rainfall from October through March, with atmospheric river events capable of delivering several inches of rain in 24 hours. The Snoqualmie, Skykomish, Skagit, and other rivers flood regularly, threatening riverside communities and cutting off mountain pass access. Snow events at lower elevations, while infrequent, can paralyze the Seattle metropolitan area, which has minimal snow removal infrastructure. Winter windstorms with gusts exceeding 60 mph can topple trees onto power lines, causing widespread outages that last days in communities surrounded by dense forest.

Include a flood emergency kit with waterproof document bags, sturdy waterproof boots, and a NOAA weather radio. Know whether your property sits in a flood zone and purchase flood insurance, since standard homeowners policies do not cover flood damage. For winter storm preparedness, maintain backup heating capability and a backup power solution. Western Washington's heavily forested power lines make extended outages common during windstorms, and some communities may lose power for a week or more.

Puget Sound Island Community Preparedness

Thousands of Washington residents live on islands in Puget Sound, including San Juan Island, Whidbey Island, Vashon Island, and Bainbridge Island. These communities depend on Washington State Ferries for access to mainland services, medical care, and supply chains. During a major earthquake, tsunami, or severe storm, ferry service may be suspended for days or weeks, effectively isolating island residents. Island communities also face limited medical facilities, with many requiring air or water evacuation for serious medical emergencies even under normal conditions.

Island residents must maintain a minimum 14-to-21-day emergency supply of food, water, and critical medications. Build an expanded family emergency kit that accounts for the possibility of extended isolation. Maintain relationships with neighbors for shared resources and mutual aid. Keep a detailed inventory of all medications and medical equipment, and work with your physician to maintain a larger-than-normal prescription supply. Consider investing in a marine VHF radio for emergency communication when cell networks fail. Our emergency water storage guide provides solutions for island households that may not have municipal water.

Seattle Urban Earthquake Preparedness

Seattle, Washington's largest city with nearly 750,000 residents, faces significant earthquake vulnerability. The city sits near both the Seattle fault and the Cascadia Subduction Zone, creating a dual seismic threat. Many neighborhoods, particularly those built on filled or glacial soils, are susceptible to liquefaction during strong shaking. Seattle's aging infrastructure, including the Alaskan Way Viaduct replacement tunnel, the SR 99 corridor, and multiple bridges, could be damaged or destroyed in a major event. The city's water supply travels through aqueducts crossing seismic zones, meaning municipal water may be unavailable for weeks after a Cascadia earthquake.

Seattle residents in high-rise buildings should identify stairwells as evacuation routes, as elevators will fail during an earthquake. Keep emergency supplies both at home and at your workplace, since you may not be able to travel between them. Secure heavy furniture, bookshelves, and electronics with earthquake straps and museum putty. Store a wrench near your gas meter to shut off the supply after shaking stops. Maintain 14 days of food and water, and register for the city's emergency alert system. Consider joining a Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) program to learn skills that could save lives in the critical hours after a disaster.

Spokane and Eastern Washington Isolation

Spokane and eastern Washington communities east of the Cascade Range face isolation risks that western Washington residents rarely consider. A Cascadia earthquake could damage or destroy I-90 tunnels and mountain pass infrastructure, cutting off eastern Washington from western Washington for weeks or months. Winter storms regularly close I-90 and other mountain passes, stranding travelers and blocking supply trucks for days. Eastern Washington communities must be prepared to function independently from the western part of the state during major emergencies, maintaining their own supplies, medical resources, and communication capabilities.

Maintain at least 14 to 21 days of emergency supplies including food, water, and critical medications. Keep a backup heating source and adequate fuel reserves, as eastern Washington temperatures can drop well below zero during winter storms. Invest in a satellite communication device for areas without reliable cell coverage. Keep vehicles equipped with a winter car survival kit including blankets, hand warmers, a shovel, and traction aids. Many eastern Washington homes depend on well water requiring electric pumps, so install a backup water system as outlined in our emergency water storage guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Cascadia earthquake risk for Washington state?
Washington sits directly on the Cascadia Subduction Zone. A full-rupture magnitude 9.0 earthquake would produce 3 to 5 minutes of shaking, followed by tsunami waves 15 to 30 minutes later on the coast. The Washington Emergency Management Division recommends every household maintain 14 days of emergency supplies, as major infrastructure could be damaged for weeks.
Should I prepare for a Mount Rainier lahar?
Yes, if you live in or near the Puyallup, Carbon, White, or Cowlitz river valleys downstream from Mount Rainier. A lahar is a volcanic mudflow of debris and water that can travel at 50 miles per hour and destroy everything in its path. The lahar evacuation zones in Pierce and King counties are clearly mapped. Know your evacuation route to high ground and practice it with your family.
How does Seattle's urban environment affect earthquake preparedness?
Seattle's soil conditions in some neighborhoods amplify earthquake shaking, and many older buildings lack seismic retrofitting. High-rise residents should identify stairwells as evacuation routes since elevators will fail. Keep emergency supplies at home and at your workplace. Seattle's water supply travels through vulnerable aqueducts, so maintain 14 days of stored water.
What wildfire risks exist in eastern Washington?
Eastern Washington experiences annual wildfire seasons with fires burning across grasslands, shrubsteppe, and forests. Communities like Wenatchee, Omak, and areas near the Okanogan-Wenatchee National Forest face regular evacuations. Smoke from fires can blanket the Puget Sound region for weeks. Include N95 masks, goggles, and air purifiers in your emergency kit.
How should Puget Sound island residents prepare for emergencies?
Island communities like San Juan Island, Whidbey Island, and Vashon depend on ferries for access. During emergencies, ferry service may be suspended, isolating island residents for days or weeks. Maintain at least 14 days of food, water, and medical supplies. Keep a detailed inventory of critical medications and establish agreements with neighbors for shared resources.
What volcanic ash preparedness do Washington residents need?
Washington has five active volcanic peaks including Mount Rainier, Mount St. Helens, and Mount Baker. A significant eruption could blanket nearby communities in ash that damages engines, contaminates water, and causes respiratory problems. Include N95 masks, goggles, and plastic sheeting for windows in your kit. Keep vehicles garaged during ashfall and avoid driving until roads are cleared.
How should Spokane and eastern Washington residents handle isolation?
Eastern Washington communities east of the Cascades can be cut off from western Washington during major earthquakes or winter storms when mountain passes close. Spokane and other communities should maintain 14 to 21 days of supplies, backup heating fuel, and satellite communication devices. Winter storms can drop several feet of snow and close I-90 for extended periods.
