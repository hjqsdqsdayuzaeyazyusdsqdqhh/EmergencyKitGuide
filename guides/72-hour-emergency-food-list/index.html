72-Hour Emergency Food List: What to Stock for 3 Days

Complete 72-hour emergency food list with shelf-stable options, calorie requirements, and storage tips for 3-day disaster preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Understanding Calorie Needs During Emergencies

When building an emergency food supply, the first number you need to know is how many calories each family member requires. The average adult needs 2,000 calories per day under normal conditions, but stress, physical exertion from evacuation, and disrupted sleep patterns can increase this demand to 2,500 or more. Active teenagers who participate in helping carry supplies or set up shelter may need 2,200 to 2,800 calories daily.

Children require fewer total calories but proportionally more nutrition per pound of body weight. Toddlers aged one to three need roughly 1,200 to 1,400 calories per day. Children aged four to eight need 1,400 to 1,800. School-age children aged nine to thirteen need 1,800 to 2,200. For a complete 72-hour supply, plan for 6,000 to 7,500 calories per adult and 3,600 to 6,600 per child depending on age. These numbers help you calculate exactly how much food to store, preventing both dangerous shortages and wasteful overpacking.

Canned Goods: The Backbone of Emergency Food

Canned foods are the most practical foundation for any emergency food supply. They are shelf-stable for two to five years, require no cooking in many cases, and provide substantial nutrition. Stock a variety of canned proteins including tuna, chicken, salmon, sardines, and spam. Canned beans, lentils, and chili provide both protein and fiber. Canned vegetables such as green beans, corn, peas, and carrots round out your nutritional profile.

Fruit canned in juice or light syrup offers essential vitamins and a morale-boosting sweet option. Canned soups and stews can be eaten cold or heated and provide a complete meal in a single can. Always pack a manual can opener in your emergency kit and keep it stored with the food supply. Choose low-sodium versions when available, as excess salt increases dehydration risk during emergencies when water may be limited. Buy what your family already eats and rotate the supply every 12 months to maintain freshness.

No-Cook Foods for When Power Is Out

When the power is out and your stove does not work, no-cook foods become essential. Peanut butter is one of the best emergency foods available. It is calorie-dense at roughly 190 calories per two-tablespoon serving, packed with protein and healthy fats, and requires zero preparation. Pair it with crackers, bread, or celery for a satisfying meal.

Other excellent no-cook options include trail mix, nuts, dried fruit, granola bars, energy bars, cereal, honey, jam, and individually wrapped cheese spreads. Shelf-stable milk boxes and juice boxes are particularly valuable for children. Beef jerky and meat sticks provide protein without refrigeration. Protein shakes in powder form mix easily with water and deliver concentrated nutrition when appetite is low. Pack a selection of items your family genuinely enjoys eating. During a stressful emergency, familiar comfort foods help maintain morale and ensure family members actually consume the calories they need.

Freeze-Dried and Dehydrated Meals

Freeze-dried meals offer the best combination of long shelf life, lightweight packaging, and nutritional completeness. Top brands offer meals that last 25 years or more, making them an excellent long-term investment. These meals typically require only boiling water to prepare, taking 10 to 15 minutes to rehydrate into a full meal.

Dehydrated meals are a lighter and often more affordable alternative, though they typically require longer preparation times. Both options are available in a wide range of meals from pasta dishes to breakfast scrambles. The main drawback is cost, as freeze-dried meals can run $5 to $10 per serving. However, their 25-year shelf life and compact size make them ideal for grab-and-go emergency bags. Supplement freeze-dried meals with shelf-stable staples to keep costs manageable while ensuring complete nutritional coverage over your entire 72-hour period.

Essential Accessories and Supplies

Food is only part of the equation. You also need the tools to access, prepare, and eat it. A manual can opener is the single most overlooked emergency food supply item. Without one, most of your canned food is inaccessible. Pack at least two can openers, as the small pocket-sized versions can break under heavy use.

Include disposable plates, cups, bowls, utensils, and napkins for each family member for all three days. A small bottle of dish soap and a sponge allow you to wash and reuse items if water is available. Heavy-duty aluminum foil serves multiple purposes from covering food to reflecting heat. A compact camp stove with two to four fuel canisters gives you the ability to heat water and cook simple meals. A metal pot or cooking container that can sit on the stove or over a campfire is essential. Pack waterproof matches or a lighter stored separately from the stove.

Dietary Restrictions and Special Needs

Emergency food planning must account for dietary restrictions. Families managing diabetes should stock sugar-free options and foods with consistent carbohydrate content to help maintain blood sugar levels. Gluten-free households can find canned goods, rice, corn tortillas, and gluten-free crackers at most grocery stores. Those with nut allergies must carefully check labels, as many emergency food bars contain traces of peanuts or tree nuts.

Infants require formula, baby food, and appropriate snacks. Elderly family members may need soft foods, nutritional supplement drinks like Ensure, or easily chewed options. Vegetarian and vegan families should stock adequate plant-based proteins including canned beans, nut butters, dried lentils, and protein bars. Kosher and halal households should verify that their emergency food selections meet their dietary standards. Whatever your restrictions, plan them in advance so you are not scrambling during an emergency. Our family 72-hour kit guide covers family-wide planning strategies.

Storage Tips to Maximize Shelf Life

Proper storage dramatically extends the life of your emergency food. Store everything in a cool, dark, dry location with temperatures between 50 and 70 degrees Fahrenheit. Heat is the primary enemy of food shelf life, so avoid garages in hot climates or attics that receive direct sunlight. Moisture causes can corrosion, mold growth, and package degradation.

Keep food off concrete floors by placing it on shelves or pallets. Use airtight plastic containers or repackage bulk dry goods in Mylar bags with oxygen absorbers for maximum longevity. Store food away from chemicals, cleaning supplies, and gasoline, as fumes can permeate packaging. Label everything with the date of purchase and the expiration date. Implement a first-in-first-out rotation system where you use older items in your daily cooking and replace them with fresh stock. Check your emergency food supply every six months for signs of damage, pest activity, or expiration.

Complete 72-Hour Food Checklist

Here is a condensed checklist you can use when building your family's 72-hour food supply. For each adult, plan three breakfasts, three lunches, and three dinners plus snacks. Use the following categories as your guide: canned proteins (tuna, chicken, beans), canned fruits and vegetables, peanut butter and crackers, granola and energy bars, trail mix and nuts, dried fruit, shelf-stable milk and juice boxes, comfort snacks, a manual can opener, disposable utensils and plates, and a portable cooking method.

For a family of four with two adults and two children aged eight and twelve, a three-day supply typically fits in one large plastic tote or two medium duffel bags. Total cost for a quality no-cook food supply runs between $60 and $120. Adding freeze-dried meals increases cost but improves meal quality. Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories should I store per person for 72 hours?
Adults need a minimum of 2,000 calories per day, though active individuals may require 2,500 or more. Children typically need 1,400 to 1,800 calories daily depending on age and size. For a complete 72-hour supply, plan for 6,000 calories per adult and 4,200 to 5,400 calories per child.
What food has the longest shelf life for emergency storage?
Freeze-dried meals can last 25 years or more. Canned goods typically last two to five years. White rice, dried beans, honey, and salt have virtually indefinite shelf lives when stored in cool, dry conditions. Dried pasta, rolled oats, and powdered milk also store well for one to two years.
Do I need a stove or fire to prepare emergency food?
Not necessarily. Many excellent emergency foods require no cooking at all, including canned meats, peanut butter, crackers, trail mix, energy bars, and dried fruit. If you want hot meals, pack a portable camp stove with fuel canisters or include meals that only need boiling water, which you can heat over a Sterno can or campfire.
How should I store emergency food to maximize shelf life?
Store emergency food in a cool, dark, dry location between 50 and 70 degrees Fahrenheit. Keep food off concrete floors and away from walls. Use airtight containers or repackage bulk items in Mylar bags with oxygen absorbers. Avoid areas with temperature fluctuations, humidity, or direct sunlight, all of which accelerate spoilage.
Can I include frozen or refrigerated foods in my emergency supply?
Frozen and refrigerated foods are not reliable for emergency kits because they depend on continuous power. Once the power goes out, frozen food begins thawing within hours. Instead, focus on shelf-stable alternatives. Canned versions of most foods exist and provide the same nutrition without requiring refrigeration.
