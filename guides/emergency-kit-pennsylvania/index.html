Emergency Kit Pennsylvania: Winter Storms, Flooding, and Severe Weather Prep

Pennsylvania emergency kit guide covering nor'easters, flooding, severe thunderstorms, extreme cold, and winter power outages. State-specific preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Pennsylvania's Year-Round Weather Risks

Pennsylvania occupies a transitional climate zone that exposes its 13 million residents to a wide variety of severe weather events throughout the year. The state stretches from the Atlantic coastal plain in the southeast to the Allegheny Plateau and Appalachian Mountains in the west and north, creating distinct microclimates with different hazard profiles. The Philadelphia metropolitan area in the southeast experiences nor'easters, coastal storm remnants from the Atlantic, and summer heat waves. Pittsburgh and western Pennsylvania face heavy lake-effect snow from Lake Erie, ice storms, and river flooding along the Ohio and Monongahela rivers. The Susquehanna and Lehigh river valleys in the central part of the state are particularly prone to catastrophic flooding from tropical system remnants and persistent rain events.

Pennsylvania's emergency management infrastructure is coordinated by the Pennsylvania Emergency Management Agency (PEMA), which works with 67 county emergency management offices to provide hazard alerts, sheltering, and disaster response. The state has experienced devastating flooding events, including Tropical Storm Agnes in 1972 and the September 2011 flooding from Tropical Storm Lee, both of which caused billions of dollars in damage across the Susquehanna Valley. Understanding Pennsylvania's specific hazards, from winter ice storms to summer severe thunderstorms, is essential for building a kit that keeps your family safe through every season. The Pennsylvania Emergency Management Agency provides county-specific hazard information at ready.pa.gov.

Nor'easters and Heavy Snowfall Preparedness

Nor'easters are the most common and disruptive winter weather events in Pennsylvania, producing heavy snow, sleet, freezing rain, and high winds across the state from November through March. The Philadelphia and Pittsburgh metropolitan areas are both prone to major snowfall events that can dump 12 to 24 inches of snow within 24 hours, shutting down schools, businesses, and transportation networks. The February 2010 snowstorm, nicknamed Snowmageddon, dropped over 28 inches on parts of eastern Pennsylvania and paralyzed the region for days. These storms cause roof collapses under heavy snow loads, widespread power outages from downed trees and lines, and dangerous travel conditions on the Pennsylvania Turnpike and interstate highways.

Prepare for winter storms by stocking at least seven days of food and water, extra batteries, flashlights, and a battery-powered NOAA weather radio. Keep rock salt, sand, or ice melt available for walkways and driveways, and maintain snow removal equipment in good working order before the season begins. If your home relies on electric heat, have a backup heating source such as a vented kerosene heater or a properly maintained wood-burning stove, but never use a gas oven, charcoal grill, or camp stove indoors due to carbon monoxide poisoning risk. Trim tree branches near power lines before winter to reduce outage risk during storms. For a complete winter survival supply checklist, review our 72-hour survival kit guide.

Flooding in the Susquehanna and Lehigh Valleys

Pennsylvania's Susquehanna and Lehigh river valleys experience some of the most severe and frequent flooding in the northeastern United States. The Susquehanna River, which flows over 400 miles through the heart of the state, regularly exceeds flood stage during heavy rain events and tropical system remnants. The catastrophic flooding from Tropical Storm Lee in September 2011 inundated entire communities along the Susquehanna, destroying homes, businesses, and critical infrastructure in Wilkes-Barre, Bloomsburg, and Williamsport. The Lehigh Valley, including Allentown and Bethlehem, faces similar flood risks from the Lehigh River and its tributaries, with the devastating July 2006 floods providing a stark reminder of how quickly water levels can rise.

If you live in a flood-prone area of Pennsylvania, purchase flood insurance through the National Flood Insurance Program immediately, as standard homeowners policies do not cover flood damage and new policies require a 30-day waiting period. Know whether your home is within a FEMA-designated floodplain by checking the FEMA flood map service center. Monitor river levels through the National Weather Service office that serves your county and sign up for county flood alerts. Keep a go-bag packed and ready at all times during spring snowmelt and hurricane season, and identify evacuation routes to higher ground that do not cross low-water bridges. Ice Storms and Extended Winter Power Outages

Ice storms are one of Pennsylvania's most dangerous winter hazards, particularly in the central and northern regions where elevation and cold temperatures create ideal conditions for prolonged freezing rain events. Ice storms coat trees, power lines, and roads in a heavy layer of ice that topples trees and snaps utility poles across wide areas. The December 2008 ice storm left hundreds of thousands of Pennsylvania customers without power for up to two weeks in sub-freezing temperatures, and rural communities served by single power line feeds were among the hardest hit. Extended winter power outages in Pennsylvania can be life-threatening, as homes without heat can reach dangerously cold indoor temperatures within hours.

Build a winter power outage kit that includes sleeping bags or blankets rated to zero degrees or below, extra layers of warm clothing including thermal underwear, wool socks, and insulated boots, and a safe indoor heating source with proper ventilation. Install battery-operated carbon monoxide detectors on every level of your home and test them monthly, as carbon monoxide poisoning is one of the deadliest risks during winter power outages. Stock heating fuel such as kerosene, propane, or seasoned firewood well before the winter season begins. Fill your bathtub with water before an ice storm so you can flush toilets if water pressure drops. Contact PPL, PECO, or your local utility to register for outage alerts and learn about their storm restoration priorities. Severe Thunderstorms and Tornado Season

Pennsylvania experiences severe thunderstorms from late spring through early fall that produce damaging hail, straight-line winds exceeding 60 miles per hour, frequent lightning, and flash flooding. These storms can develop rapidly during warm, humid summer afternoons and can cause widespread property damage, power outages, and injuries. The state also experiences tornadoes, though less frequently than the Great Plains states. Pennsylvania averages 10 to 15 tornadoes per year, with the southeastern counties near Philadelphia and the Lehigh Valley seeing the highest concentration. In July 2004, a series of tornadoes swept across the state causing extensive damage to homes and businesses.

Identify the safest room in your home for severe weather, which should be an interior room on the lowest floor away from windows, such as a bathroom, closet, or hallway. Keep a NOAA weather radio with battery backup and alert function in your shelter area at all times during the spring and summer storm season. Pack helmets or heavy blankets to protect against flying debris, which causes the majority of tornado injuries. If you are driving when a tornado warning is issued, seek shelter in a sturdy building immediately or, as a last resort, pull over and lie flat in a low ditch away from your vehicle. Rural vs Urban Emergency Preparedness in Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania's geography creates two very different preparedness challenges for its residents. Urban areas including Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, Allentown, and Erie have access to municipal water systems, emergency services, public shelters, and public transportation that can assist during evacuations. However, urban residents may face challenges with apartment-specific preparation, limited storage for emergency supplies, and dependence on aging infrastructure that can fail during severe weather. Philadelphia residents should be aware of the city's flood-prone neighborhoods along the Schuylkill and Delaware rivers and know their proximity to designated evacuation routes along major highways.

Rural Pennsylvania residents face unique challenges including dependence on well water that requires an electric pump to operate, long distances to hospitals and emergency services, limited cell phone coverage in mountainous areas, and limited road access that may be cut off by fallen trees, ice, or flooding. Many rural homes rely on heating oil or propane delivered by truck, which may be impossible to refill during extended storms. Maintain an extended supply of at least 14 days of food, water, and heating fuel during winter months. Keep a generator with adequate fuel for operating your well pump and essential appliances. Store chainsaw fuel and maintain equipment for clearing fallen trees from your driveway and access roads. Our home emergency kit guide covers both urban and rural preparation strategies in detail.

Evacuation Routes and Emergency Transportation

Effective evacuation planning in Pennsylvania requires understanding the major transportation corridors and the limitations of each route during different types of emergencies. The Pennsylvania Turnpike (I-76) crosses the state east to west through the mountains and can become congested or impassable during major winter storms when steep grades become treacherous. I-76, I-81, I-80, and I-90 are the primary east-west routes, while I-79, I-81, and I-95 serve north-south travel. During flood events, low-lying sections of these highways and secondary roads near rivers can become inundated, cutting off evacuation routes. Map at least two evacuation routes from your home and community that account for different hazard scenarios.

Register with your county's emergency notification system to receive immediate alerts about evacuation orders, shelter openings, and road closures. Pennsylvania's county emergency management offices coordinate with PEMA and the American Red Cross to operate public shelters during major events. If you live in a rural area, coordinate with neighbors to ensure everyone has access to transportation during an evacuation, particularly elderly or disabled residents who may not be able to drive. Keep your vehicle fueled to at least half full during winter storm and hurricane seasons, and store a go-bag in the trunk with at least one day of emergency supplies. Seasonal Preparation Calendar for Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania's four distinct seasons each bring specific hazards that require advance preparation. January through March is peak nor'easter and ice storm season, so maintain full emergency supplies and keep vehicles winterized. April and May bring spring flooding from snowmelt and heavy rain, making this the ideal time to check flood insurance policies, clear gutters and drains, and test sump pumps. June through August is severe thunderstorm season in Pennsylvania, when afternoon and evening storms can produce tornadoes, damaging hail, and flash flooding. Keep your NOAA weather radio active and monitor storm forecasts daily during the warm months.

September through November is the time to prepare your home for winter by insulating exposed pipes, servicing your heating system, stocking heating fuel, and testing backup generators. This is also the period when Atlantic hurricane remnants can bring heavy rain and flooding to Pennsylvania, so maintain flood preparedness through the fall. Schedule an emergency kit review every six months, using daylight saving time clock changes in March and November as convenient reminders. Replace expired food and water, test flashlights and radios, update medications, and review your family evacuation plan with all household members. Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most common emergencies in Pennsylvania?
Pennsylvania faces nor'easters and heavy snowfall particularly in the Philadelphia and Pittsburgh corridors, river and flash flooding along the Susquehanna and Lehigh river valleys, severe thunderstorms with hail and damaging winds, occasional tornadoes, extreme cold and ice storms in winter, and summer heat waves. Rural areas also face challenges with well water dependence, limited road access during snow events, and extended power outages during ice storms.
How do I prepare for nor'easters and heavy snow in Pennsylvania?
Stock at least seven days of food and water, extra batteries, flashlights, a NOAA weather radio, and rock salt or sand for icy walkways. Fill your car with gas before winter storms and have a backup heating source if you rely on electric heat. Trim tree branches near power lines before winter storm season. Philadelphia and Pittsburgh both experience heavy snowfall events that can accumulate over a foot within 24 hours.
How should I prepare for flooding in the Susquehanna or Lehigh valleys?
Know whether your home is in a FEMA-designated floodplain and purchase flood insurance through the National Flood Insurance Program, as standard homeowners policies do not cover flood damage. Monitor river levels through the National Weather Service and sign up for county flood alerts. Keep a go-bag ready and identify higher ground evacuation routes. During heavy rain events, avoid driving through flooded roadways as Pennsylvania river valleys can see rapid water rises.
Do I need to prepare for ice storms and extended power outages in Pennsylvania?
Yes. Pennsylvania experiences ice storms that can leave communities without power for one to two weeks, particularly in rural areas served by single power lines. Stock extra blankets, sleeping bags, a safe backup heating source, extra heating fuel, and at least 14 days of food and water during winter. Install battery-operated carbon monoxide detectors and never use gas appliances for indoor heating.
What should rural Pennsylvania residents do differently for emergency prep?
Rural Pennsylvania residents should maintain extended supplies of at least 14 days of food, water, and heating fuel because road access may be limited during heavy snow or flooding. Many rural homes depend on well water that requires electric pumps, so store backup water and consider a generator for well pump operation. Keep chainsaw fuel and equipment maintained for clearing fallen trees from driveways and roads.
What emergency resources does the Pennsylvania Emergency Management Agency provide?
PEMA coordinates disaster response across Pennsylvania 67 counties and operates the Pennsylvania Emergency Operations Center during major events. PEMA provides county-level hazard information, coordinates with the National Guard for storm response, manages sheltering operations through the Red Cross, and distributes alerts through the Pennsylvania Alert system. Visit ready.pa.gov for preparedness resources and county emergency management contacts.
How do severe thunderstorms and tornadoes affect Pennsylvania?
Pennsylvania experiences severe thunderstorms with damaging hail, straight-line winds, and flash flooding throughout the warmer months. While tornadoes are less frequent than in the Great Plains, the state sees several tornadoes each year, particularly in the southeastern counties. Identify the safest interior room in your home and keep a NOAA weather radio with battery backup to receive severe thunderstorm and tornado warnings even at night.
