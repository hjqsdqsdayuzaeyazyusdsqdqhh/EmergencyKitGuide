72-Hour Kit Checklist: Complete Printable Guide

Download our comprehensive 72-hour kit checklist covering water, food, shelter, first aid, tools, and communication for 3-day emergency preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why a Checklist Is Your Most Important Preparedness Tool

A comprehensive checklist is the foundation of effective emergency preparedness. Without one, you risk overlooking critical supplies, purchasing duplicate items, or leaving dangerous gaps in your kit. FEMA recommends that every household maintain a written checklist aligned with national preparedness guidelines. A good checklist organizes supplies into logical categories, specifies quantities for your family size, and provides a framework for regular review and rotation.

This guide provides a complete, printable 72-hour kit checklist covering every essential category. Whether you are building your first emergency kit or auditing an existing one, use this checklist as your master reference. Each section includes specific items, recommended quantities, and tips for customization based on your family's unique needs. Print it, check off items as you acquire them, and keep a copy stored with your emergency supplies for quick reference during review cycles.

Water Supply Checklist

Water is the most critical item on any emergency checklist. Store a minimum of one gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation. For a family of four, that is at least 12 gallons for three days. In hot climates or for active families, increase to two gallons per person per day. Your water checklist should include: commercially bottled water in sealed containers, a portable water filter or purification tablets, a collapsible water container for fetching additional water, and a list of alternative water sources in and around your home.

Additional water items often overlooked include a small bottle of unscented household bleach for emergency purification (8 drops per gallon of clear water), a large metal pot for boiling water, and a manual siphon pump for extracting water from your water heater tank. Label every container with the fill date and plan to rotate your supply every six months. Our 72-hour water storage guide covers container selection and purification methods in detail.

Food Supply Checklist

Your food checklist should cover three meals per person per day for three days plus snacks. Focus on items that require no refrigeration, minimal preparation, and have a long shelf life. Key items include: canned proteins (tuna, chicken, beans), canned fruits and vegetables, peanut butter, crackers, granola bars, trail mix, dried fruit, shelf-stable milk, and comfort snacks. Do not forget a manual can opener, disposable utensils, plates, and napkins.

If you plan to cook during an emergency, add a portable camp stove with fuel canisters, a metal cooking pot, waterproof matches or a lighter, and aluminum foil. For families with infants, add formula, baby food, bottles, and age-appropriate snacks. For dietary restrictions, include gluten-free, sugar-free, or allergen-free alternatives as needed. Rotate your food supply every 6 to 12 months, checking expiration dates and replacing items that are nearing the end of their shelf life. See our 72-hour emergency food list for calorie planning and product recommendations.

First Aid and Medical Checklist

A well-stocked first aid kit handles everything from minor cuts to stabilizing more serious injuries until professional help arrives. Your checklist should include: adhesive bandages in multiple sizes, sterile gauze pads, medical tape, elastic bandages for sprains, antibiotic ointment, burn cream, hydrocortisone cream, pain relievers (ibuprofen and acetaminophen), antihistamines, anti-diarrheal medication, tweezers, medical scissors, a digital thermometer, and a CPR pocket mask.

Supplement the basic kit with prescription medications for every family member, maintaining at least a 7-day supply. Include copies of prescriptions, a list of medication dosages and prescribing doctors, and any specialized medical devices like EpiPens or inhalers. Add personal protective equipment including N95 masks and disposable gloves. A laminated first aid instruction card provides quick reference when you need it most. Include sunscreen, insect repellent, and any allergy-specific medications. Our car first aid kit guide has additional medical supply recommendations.

Shelter, Warmth, and Clothing Checklist

Even if you shelter in place, disruptions to heating, windows, or roofing can leave your family exposed. Your shelter checklist should include: emergency Mylar space blankets (one per family member), a large tarp or emergency tent, heavy-duty plastic sheeting and duct tape for sealing openings, sleeping bags or compact sleeping pads, and a change of weather-appropriate clothing for each family member stored in waterproof bags.

Each person needs sturdy closed-toe shoes, extra socks, rain gear, warm layers for cold weather, and lightweight breathable clothing for warm weather. Include hats, gloves, and scarves for winter. For infants and toddlers, pack extra onesies, sleep sacks, and swaddling blankets. Consider adding a lightweight emergency bivvy sack per person as a compact backup to sleeping bags. Update clothing sizes twice a year as children grow. For a complete household preparedness approach, review our family 72-hour kit guide.

Lighting, Tools, and Communication Checklist

Reliable lighting and communication tools are essential when power fails. Your checklist should include: at least two LED flashlights per family member, a headlamp for each adult, a generous supply of extra batteries in all sizes used by your devices, a battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio, and a whistle for signaling rescuers.

For tools, include a multi-tool, a wrench for shutting off utilities, sturdy work gloves, duct tape, paracord or rope, and a fire extinguisher. Communication items include a portable power bank fully charged, charging cables for your devices, two-way radios for family communication, and a written list of emergency contact numbers. A local paper map of your area is essential when GPS and cell networks are down. Store important phone numbers on paper in case your phone dies. Check our home emergency kit guide for additional tool recommendations.

Personal Documents and Sanitation Checklist

Identity documents and personal sanitation supplies round out your checklist. Store photocopies of photo IDs, insurance policies, birth certificates, passports, and vehicle registration in a waterproof, fireproof bag. Include digital copies on an encrypted USB drive. Keep $200 to $300 in small bills and coins in a sealed waterproof bag.

Sanitation supplies include: toilet paper, garbage bags, moist towelettes, hand sanitizer, soap, a small shovel for waste disposal, feminine hygiene products, and plastic ties for securing waste bags. Include personal items like extra eyeglasses, hearing aid batteries, and contact lens supplies. For children, add a small comfort item such as a stuffed animal or favorite book. Pack a family photo to aid in identification and reunification if family members become separated. Our complete checklist page has an organized printable version you can download.

Special Considerations for Families with Kids

Families with children need additional checklist items that address the unique needs of younger family members. For babies, add formula, bottles, baby food, diapers, wipes, a portable crib, and a baby carrier. For toddlers, include comfort items, age-appropriate snacks, and extra changes of clothing. For school-age children, pack a small flashlight, a deck of cards, a coloring book, and a family photo.

Every child should have a personal identification card with their name, your phone number, and an out-of-state emergency contact. Place a copy inside their pocket and another in your main document bag. Practice your family evacuation plan at least twice a year, making sure every child knows where emergency supplies are stored and how to grab their personal bag. For kids-specific supply recommendations, consult our 72-hour kit for kids guide.

How to Use and Maintain Your Checklist

A checklist is only useful if you keep it current. Print two copies: one to keep with your emergency supplies and one to post in a convenient location like the inside of a kitchen cabinet door. After each purchase, check the item off both copies. During your semi-annual review, go through the entire checklist item by item, verifying that every supply is present, in good condition, and within its expiration date.

Use the checklist as a shopping guide when adding to your kit. Start with the highest-priority items first: water, food, first aid, and lighting. Build incrementally over several months rather than trying to buy everything at once. Share the checklist with all adult family members so everyone contributes to the effort. After any major life event such as a new baby, a move, or a change in health conditions, review and update the checklist immediately. Preparedness is a continuous process, and your checklist is the roadmap that keeps you on track.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most essential items in a 72-hour kit?
The five most essential categories are water (one gallon per person per day for three days), non-perishable food, a first aid kit, a flashlight with extra batteries, and a whistle for signaling. These items address the most critical survival needs: hydration, nutrition, medical care, visibility, and rescue communication.
Can I buy a pre-made 72-hour kit instead of building one?
Pre-made 72-hour kits are available from many retailers and offer convenience, but they often include lower-quality items and may not match your family's specific needs. Use a checklist to review what the pre-made kit includes, then supplement with additional items for family members, dietary needs, medications, and personal documents. Combining a pre-made base with customized additions gives you the best of both approaches.
How long does it take to assemble a complete 72-hour kit?
Most families can assemble a basic 72-hour kit in one to two shopping trips over a weekend. Start with the essentials on your checklist and add items incrementally over several weeks. You do not need everything at once. Begin with water, food, and first aid, then add tools, communication, and comfort items as your budget allows. Having a partial kit is far better than having nothing.
Should I have separate kits for home, work, and car?
Yes. A full-sized kit should be stored at home. Keep a compact grab-and-go version in your car, and a basic supply kit at your workplace. At minimum, your car kit should include water, a first aid kit, a flashlight, a blanket, and jumper cables. Your workplace kit should have a day's worth of water, snacks, a phone charger, comfortable shoes, and a basic change of clothes.
What should I do after I complete my 72-hour kit?
After assembling your kit, create a family communication plan with emergency contacts and meeting points. Practice an evacuation drill so every family member knows where the kit is stored and how to use its contents. Set a six-month reminder to review and update your supplies. Share your preparedness plan with trusted neighbors or relatives. Preparedness is an ongoing process, not a one-time task.
