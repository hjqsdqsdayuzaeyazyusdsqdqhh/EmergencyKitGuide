Emergency Kit Oregon: Wildfires, Earthquakes, and Pacific Northwest Storm Preparedness

Oregon emergency kit guide covering Cascadia earthquake risk, wildfire season, winter storms, flooding, and tsunami preparedness. State-specific disaster prep.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Oregon's Cascading Disaster Risks

Oregon faces one of the most complex disaster profiles of any state in the nation. The Cascadia Subduction Zone running offshore represents the single greatest seismic threat to the Pacific Northwest, with scientists estimating a 37% chance of a magnitude 9.0 or greater earthquake occurring within the next 50 years. Simultaneously, Oregon battles increasingly severe wildfire seasons, heavy winter flooding in the Willamette Valley, tsunami risk along the entire 363-mile coastline, and volcanic hazards from the Cascade Range. The Oregon Office of Emergency Management works to coordinate preparedness efforts across these multiple threats, but individual households must take primary responsibility for their own survival in the critical days following a major disaster.

Oregon's geography creates distinct emergency zones that each require tailored preparation. The wet, densely forested Coast Range and Willamette Valley face flooding and earthquake risks. The high desert of eastern Oregon experiences wildfires, extreme temperature swings, and isolation from emergency services. The Cascade Range presents volcanic and wildfire hazards, while the Columbia River gorge funnels wind and creates unique storm patterns. This guide covers every major hazard zone in Oregon and provides the specific emergency kit components that each demands.

Cascadia Subduction Zone Earthquake Preparedness

The Cascadia Subduction Zone is a 600-mile fault running from Northern California to Vancouver Island that last ruptured on January 26, 1700. When it ruptures again, it will produce a magnitude 9.0+ earthquake lasting 3 to 5 minutes of intense shaking, followed by a tsunami reaching the coast within 15 to 30 minutes. Inland cities like Portland, Salem, and Eugene will experience severe shaking but no tsunami. The Oregon Office of Emergency Management estimates that a full Cascadia event could leave communities without electricity, water, and road access for weeks or even months. This is not a hypothetical scenario; it is a geological certainty that will happen eventually.

Every Oregon household should maintain a minimum 14-day emergency supply, far exceeding the standard 72-hour recommendation. Include a comprehensive earthquake emergency kit with heavy-duty work gloves, a pry bar for removing debris, sturdy boots for walking over broken glass and concrete, and a wrench to shut off your gas meter. Store water at a rate of one gallon per person per day for 14 days minimum, and keep a water storage system that does not depend on municipal pressure. Secure heavy furniture, water heaters, and appliances to wall studs with anti-tip brackets and earthquake straps.

Tsunami Preparedness for Coastal Communities

Oregon's entire coastline sits in the tsunami inundation zone from a Cascadia earthquake. Cities like Seaside, Cannon Beach, Newport, Florence, and Brookings would have only 15 to 30 minutes between the earthquake and the arrival of the first wave. Unlike earthquake shaking, there is no way to engineered your way out of a tsunami if you are in the inundation zone. The only survival strategy is immediate evacuation to high ground. Tsunami evacuation routes are clearly marked throughout coastal Oregon, and residents should practice these routes with their families at least twice a year.

Your coastal go-bag should be lightweight enough to carry while running uphill and should include a whistle for signaling, a waterproof bag containing identification and insurance documents, a small first aid kit, sturdy closed-toe shoes stored by every bed, and a battery-powered weather radio. Keep a go-bag in your vehicle, at your workplace, and at your children's school. If you feel shaking, do not wait for a tsunami warning. Move immediately to ground at least 100 feet above sea level or at least 2 miles inland. Follow the earthquake preparedness guide for comprehensive coastal evacuation planning.

Wildfire Season: Western and Eastern Oregon Threats

Oregon's wildfire season has grown dramatically in recent years, with the catastrophic 2020 Labor Day fires destroying over 1,000 homes and burning more than a million acres in a single weekend. Western Oregon faces increasing risk as drought conditions dry out the normally moist Coast Range and Cascade foothills forests. Eastern Oregon battles frequent rangeland fires across vast BLM lands where emergency response can take hours. Smoke from fires across the region can blanket the Willamette Valley for weeks, creating air quality that rivals the worst pollution in the world. Wildfire is now a year-round threat in some parts of the state.

Follow our wildfire preparedness guide for defensible space creation and evacuation planning. Include N95 respirator masks for every family member, protective goggles, and a portable air purifier in your home emergency kit. Register for your county's emergency alert system, as the 2020 fires demonstrated that evacuation orders can come with very little lead time. Keep your vehicle fueled at least half full during fire season, and maintain go-bags ready by every exit. If you have respiratory conditions, plan to evacuate early rather than waiting for mandatory orders.

Willamette Valley Winter Storms and Flooding

The Willamette Valley from Portland to Eugene receives most of its annual precipitation between November and March, with atmospheric river events capable of dropping several inches of rain in 24 hours. The Willamette, Columbia, and their tributaries can flood rapidly, inundating low-lying agricultural land and threatening riverside communities. The catastrophic 1996 and 2020 floods demonstrated how quickly water levels can rise, and climate models suggest these events will become more frequent and intense. Southwest Oregon communities along the Rogue and Umpqua rivers face similar risks.

Include a flood emergency kit with waterproof bags for electronics and documents, sturdy waterproof boots, and a NOAA weather radio for flood warnings. Know whether your property sits in a FEMA-designated floodplain and purchase flood insurance if applicable, as standard homeowners policies do not cover flood damage. Keep important documents elevated above potential flood levels or stored digitally in the cloud. Winter storm power outages can last days in rural areas with heavily forested power lines, so maintain backup heating and a backup power solution.

Portland Urban Earthquake Preparedness

Portland, Oregon's largest city with over 650,000 residents, faces significant earthquake vulnerability. Many older buildings, particularly unreinforced masonry structures in the downtown core and older neighborhoods, were not built to modern seismic codes. The city's water system relies on a single main aqueduct from the Bull Run watershed that could be severed by a Cascadia earthquake. Portland's bridges, many of which are seismically vulnerable, could fail simultaneously, cutting off evacuation and emergency response routes. The city has invested in seismic upgrades, but the pace of retrofitting cannot keep pace with the full scope of the threat.

Portland residents should secure heavy furniture, bookshelves, and appliances with earthquake straps and museum putty. Identify safe spots in every room away from windows and heavy objects. Keep a flashlight, sturdy shoes, and a hard hat by every bed. Store 14 days of food and water since municipal water may not function for weeks after a major earthquake. Know how to shut off your gas meter and practice this skill with all adult household members. Consider joining your neighborhood's Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) program to learn basic disaster response skills. Our 72-hour kit guide provides a complete starting framework for Portland households.

Rural Eastern Oregon Isolation and Self-Reliance

Eastern Oregon encompasses vast stretches of high desert, ranchland, and remote mountain communities separated by enormous distances. Counties like Harney, Malheur, and Lake are larger than many eastern U.S. states yet have populations under 10,000. Cell phone coverage is spotty to nonexistent across much of the region, and the nearest hospital may be an hour or more away by road. Wildfires, winter storms, and flooding can all cut off these communities from outside assistance for days or weeks. Residents of rural eastern Oregon must be fundamentally self-reliant, as emergency services cannot be expected to arrive quickly during major events.

Build an expanded 72-hour survival kit scaled to at least 14 days. Include a satellite communicator or personal locator beacon since cell service may be unavailable. Maintain a minimum two-week supply of food and water, backup generator with at least two weeks of fuel, and a comprehensive tool kit for property repairs. Many eastern Oregon properties depend on well water that requires electric pumps, so install a hand pump backup or gravity-fed water storage system. Keep vehicles maintained with full fuel tanks, winter tires from October through April, and a winter car survival kit.

Coast Range Access and Mountain Community Challenges

The Oregon Coast Range and Cascade mountain communities face unique access challenges during emergencies. Many communities depend on a single road through forested mountain terrain that can be blocked by landslides, fallen trees, or wildfire. The Coast Range communities between the Willamette Valley and the coast are particularly vulnerable, as Highway 26 and Highway 18 can be cut off by winter storms and landslides. Mountain communities near Mount Hood, Mount Jefferson, and other Cascade volcanoes must also prepare for volcanic ashfall events that could contaminate water supplies and damage machinery.

Mountain and Coast Range residents should maintain 14-day emergency supplies at minimum, with particular attention to heating fuel storage for rural areas that depend on propane or heating oil. Keep chainsaw equipment accessible for clearing fallen trees from driveways and access roads. Include dust masks and goggles in your kit for volcanic ashfall scenarios. Maintain a family emergency kit with enough supplies for every household member plus pets. Establish communication plans with family members that account for the possibility that cell networks may be down for extended periods.

Frequently Asked Questions

How serious is the Cascadia Subduction Zone earthquake risk in Oregon?
Scientists estimate a 37% chance of a magnitude 9.0+ Cascadia earthquake occurring in the next 50 years. Such an event would cause widespread shaking lasting 3 to 5 minutes, followed by a tsunami reaching the coast within 15 to 30 minutes. The Oregon Office of Emergency Management recommends every household maintain at least 14 days of supplies, as infrastructure damage could isolate communities for weeks.
What makes Oregon wildfire season unique?
Oregon faces a dual wildfire threat. Eastern Oregon experiences frequent rangeland and forest fires in remote areas, while western Oregon faces increasing risk from fires burning into the wildland-urban interface near populated valleys. Smoke from fires across the Pacific Northwest can blanket the Willamette Valley for weeks, creating hazardous air quality that requires N95 masks and indoor shelter.
Do I need a tsunami kit if I live on the Oregon coast?
Yes, coastal Oregon residents and visitors should prepare for a tsunami that could arrive within 15 to 30 minutes of a Cascadia earthquake. Keep a go-bag near every exit, know your evacuation route to high ground, and never wait for an official warning. Tsunami evacuation maps are posted throughout coastal communities. Your kit should include a whistle, sturdy shoes, and documents in a waterproof bag.
How should Portland residents prepare for earthquakes?
Many Portland buildings, particularly older unreinforced masonry structures, are vulnerable to earthquake damage. Secure heavy furniture and appliances to walls, identify safe spots in every room, and keep shoes and a flashlight by your bed. After the shaking stops, expect disrupted water, sewer, and gas lines. Keep 14 days of food and water and a wrench to shut off your gas meter.
What challenges do rural eastern Oregon residents face during emergencies?
Eastern Oregon communities are separated by vast distances with limited cell coverage and long emergency response times. Many properties rely on well water and propane. Residents should maintain 14 to 30 days of supplies, carry satellite communication devices, and keep vehicles maintained with full fuel tanks. Wildfire evacuations in remote areas may require residents to self-evacuate long before help arrives.
How does Oregon winter storm flooding affect emergency preparedness?
The Willamette Valley and southwestern Oregon receive heavy rainfall from November through March, causing river flooding and landslides. The 1996 and 2020 floods demonstrated how quickly water levels can rise. Keep flood emergency supplies ready, monitor river levels, and know if your property sits in a floodplain. Storm-related power outages can last days in rural areas with heavily forested power lines.
