72-Hour Water Storage Guide: How Much Water You Need

Learn how to store water for emergencies with our 72-hour water storage guide. Calculating needs, containers, purification, and rotation schedule.

Last updated: July 25, 2025By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Water Is Your Number One Priority

Water is more important than food, shelter, or any other emergency supply. A healthy adult can survive roughly three weeks without food, but only three days without water. In a disaster scenario, municipal water supplies may become contaminated, cut off entirely, or accessible only through long lines at distribution centers. Having a stored water supply ensures your family can hydrate, prepare food, maintain basic hygiene, and administer first aid without depending on outside help.

According to FEMA and the American Red Cross, every household should store a minimum of one gallon of water per person per day for at least three days. This is the baseline recommendation for drinking and basic sanitation. In reality, most families need more than this minimum, especially in warm climates, during physical exertion, or when caring for infants or elderly family members. This guide explains exactly how much water your family needs, the best containers to use, how to purify alternative water sources, and how to maintain your supply on an ongoing rotation schedule.

Calculating Your Family's Water Needs

The standard calculation is one gallon per person per day. This breaks down to roughly half a gallon for drinking and half a gallon for basic sanitation and cooking. However, several factors can significantly increase your actual needs. Hot climates and high humidity accelerate dehydration, pushing requirements toward two gallons per person per day. Physical activity during evacuation or cleanup efforts further increases demand.

Special populations require additional water. Nursing mothers need extra fluid to maintain milk production. Children and infants need proportionally more water per pound of body weight than adults. Individuals with illnesses, particularly those involving vomiting or diarrhea, need significantly more fluids to prevent dangerous dehydration. For a family of four in a moderate climate, plan for a minimum of 12 gallons for three days. In hot weather or for active families, store 20 to 24 gallons. This may seem like a lot, but it breaks down to four to six one-gallon jugs per person, which is manageable with proper planning. Choosing the Right Containers

Not all containers are suitable for long-term water storage. The safest option is food-grade plastic containers, specifically those made from high-density polyethylene (HDPE), which is marked with recycling symbol number 2. This plastic does not leach chemicals into water over time. You can purchase purpose-built water storage drums in sizes ranging from five to 55 gallons from emergency preparedness suppliers.

Commercially bottled water in sealed containers is the easiest option. The water is already purified and sealed in food-grade plastic. Simply store the bottles and replace them before the printed expiration date. For larger volumes, 2.5-gallon jugs with spigots are convenient for daily use. Never use containers that previously held milk, juice, or chemicals, as residues can harbor bacteria or introduce toxins. Avoid glass containers in earthquake-prone areas because they can shatter. Stainless steel containers are safe but can be heavy and expensive. Whatever containers you choose, clean them thoroughly with mild soap and rinse completely before filling.

Water Purification Methods

Stored water is your primary supply, but knowing how to purify additional water sources extends your safety margin. Boiling is the most reliable method. Bring water to a rolling boil for at least one minute to kill most bacteria, viruses, and parasites. At altitudes above 5,000 feet, boil for three minutes. Boiled water may taste flat, so pour it between two clean containers to re-aerate it.

Water purification tablets using chlorine dioxide or iodine are lightweight and effective. Follow the package instructions precisely, as under-dosing can leave harmful organisms alive. Iodine-treated water should not be consumed by pregnant women or people with thyroid conditions. Portable water filters like the Sawyer Mini or LifeStraw remove bacteria and parasites down to 0.1 microns but do not remove viruses. For comprehensive protection, combine filtration with chemical treatment. Distillation using a solar still or pot-on-pot system removes salt, heavy metals, and most contaminants but requires fuel or solar energy. Learn more about water in our 72-hour survival kit guide.

Rotation and Maintenance Schedule

Water you store yourself should be replaced every six months. Commercially bottled water lasts longer, with most manufacturers printing a two-year shelf life from the date of bottling. Set calendar reminders to rotate your supply. A simple approach is to use your stored water for watering plants or cleaning, then refill the containers with fresh tap water.

When refilling containers, wash them first with a solution of one teaspoon of unscented liquid household bleach per quart of water. Rinse thoroughly, then fill with clean tap water. If your municipal water supply uses chlorine, no additional treatment is needed. If you use well water or a natural source, treat it before storage. Label every container with the fill date using waterproof marker or masking tape. Inspect stored water periodically for discoloration, odor, or sediment, which may indicate contamination. Store water off concrete floors on pallets or shelving, as concrete can leach chemicals into plastic containers over time.

Emergency Water Sources Around Your Home

If your stored water runs out, your home likely contains several hidden water sources. The water heater tank typically holds 30 to 80 gallons of clean water. Turn off the power or gas supply to the heater, then open the drain valve at the bottom to access the water. Ice cubes in your freezer are another quick source of relatively clean water. The water in toilet tanks (not bowls) is also sanitary for drinking if no chemical cleaners have been added recently.

If you have a swimming pool, the water can be used for sanitation and hygiene, though it should be purified before drinking due to chlorine content. Rainwater collection using clean buckets or tarps provides an additional supply. Never drink water from radiators, waterbeds, or hot water boilers, as these contain chemical additives. Always purify any water from unfamiliar sources before drinking. Document your household water sources now so you are not searching for them during a crisis.

Water for Cooking and Hygiene

Most emergency preparedness guidance focuses on drinking water, but cooking and hygiene also require significant amounts. Cooking rice, pasta, or rehydrating freeze-dried meals uses roughly one to two cups of water per serving. For a family of three meals per day over three days, that adds several gallons to your total need.

Basic hygiene including handwashing, face cleaning, and brushing teeth uses approximately one to two gallons per person per day. A family of four might need an additional four to eight gallons for three days of basic sanitation. In warm weather, sponge baths using minimal water are more practical than full washes. Wet wipes and hand sanitizer reduce water demand for hygiene. If you have an outdoor space, a solar shower bag can provide warm water for bathing using minimal stored supply. Consider gray water recycling, where water used for washing can be reused for flushing toilets or cleaning surfaces.

Portable Water Solutions for Evacuation

When you must evacuate, you cannot bring your entire water supply. Pack at least one gallon per person per day in your go-bags, ideally in reusable hard-sided water bottles that are durable enough to survive a bumpy evacuation route. Collapsible water containers allow you to carry empty containers and fill them when you reach a safe water source.

Every family member should carry a personal water bottle. Include water purification tablets or a portable filter in each evacuation kit. A LifeStraw or similar personal filter weighs only a few ounces and can purify up to 1,000 gallons of water. If you are evacuating by car, keep a case of bottled water in the trunk at all times. Gas stations and convenience stores may be closed or out of stock during regional emergencies. Plan your evacuation route to pass by known water sources like public parks with wells or community centers that may have supplies.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should I store per person for 72 hours?
Store a minimum of one gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation. A family of four needs at least 12 gallons for three days. In hot climates, during physical exertion, or for nursing mothers, increase this to two gallons per person per day, bringing the total to 24 gallons for a family of four.
How long does stored water remain safe to drink?
Commercially bottled water stored in a cool, dark location can last indefinitely. Water you fill yourself in clean containers should be replaced every six months. If water develops an off taste, it is still generally safe but may benefit from being poured between containers to re-oxygenate it. Always inspect stored water for discoloration, odor, or sediment before drinking.
What containers are safe for storing emergency water?
Use food-grade plastic containers such as HDPE plastics marked with recycling symbol number 2. Large water storage drums, commercially bottled water jugs, and BPA-free water bottles are all safe options. Never use containers that held milk or juice, as residual sugars promote bacterial growth. Avoid glass containers in earthquake-prone areas. Metal containers should be stainless steel and food-grade.
How can I purify water if my stored supply runs out?
Boil water for at least one minute to kill most pathogens. Water purification tablets using chlorine dioxide or iodine are effective and lightweight. Portable filters like the Sawyer Mini or LifeStraw remove bacteria and parasites. For cloudy water, let sediment settle before filtering. Combine methods for the best results: filter first, then purify with tablets or boiling.
Where is the best place to store emergency water at home?
Store water in a cool, dark place away from direct sunlight and heat sources. A basement, interior closet, or pantry shelf are all good choices. Avoid garages with temperature extremes, attics, or areas near chemicals and gasoline. Keep water off concrete floors by placing containers on pallets or shelving. Store at least one portable water supply near your home's main exit for quick evacuation.
