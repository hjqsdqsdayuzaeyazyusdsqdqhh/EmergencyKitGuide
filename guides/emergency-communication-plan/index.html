Emergency Communication Plan: Staying Connected During Disasters

Build an emergency communication plan for your family. Covers text vs call strategies, backup power, radio communication, and family contact trees.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Cell Networks Fail During Disasters

When a major disaster strikes, cellular networks are among the first services to become unreliable or fail entirely. The causes are multiple and compounding: physical damage to cell towers from wind, flooding, or seismic activity, loss of electrical power to tower sites, and overwhelming demand from thousands of people simultaneously attempting to make calls and send messages. During Hurricane Katrina, cellular networks in the affected area were offline for days. After the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan, networks were so congested that only about 10 percent of calls could connect. Understanding why networks fail helps you prepare communication strategies that work when standard connectivity breaks down.

Even when towers remain standing, they have limited battery backup that typically lasts 8 to 24 hours. Once that battery power is depleted without grid electricity or generator refueling, the tower goes dark. Additionally, network operators may implement priority access for emergency responders, further limiting civilian capacity. Your communication plan must account for these realities by incorporating multiple redundant methods of contact. No single technology is foolproof, so layering your approach across cellular, radio, satellite, and written communication ensures that you can reach your family through at least one channel. Start building your plan using the family emergency plan guide as a foundation.

The Text-Over-Call Strategy

FEMA and the Red Cross both recommend texting rather than calling during the initial hours of a disaster. The reason is bandwidth. A single voice call requires a dedicated channel on the cellular network that can handle approximately 50 concurrent calls per tower sector. A text message uses a signaling channel that can handle thousands of simultaneous transmissions. Texts are also stored by the network and delivered when capacity becomes available, meaning your message will eventually reach its destination even if it is delayed by minutes or hours. A voice call either connects immediately or fails entirely.

When composing emergency texts, keep messages brief and factual. Include your name, your current location or general area, your safety status, and your next intended action. For example: "Maria here. Safe at Lincoln School. Evacuating to Aunt Rosa's in Portland. Will text again when I can." This format gives your family the essential information they need without requiring a back-and-forth conversation that ties up network resources. Avoid sending group texts during the initial emergency period as they consume more bandwidth. Program your phone with pre-written emergency text templates that you can send with a single tap. Pair this strategy with a complete family emergency plan for integrated preparedness.

Designing Your Family Contact Tree

A contact tree is the backbone of family emergency communication. It eliminates confusion about who calls whom and ensures that information flows efficiently even when not everyone can reach each other directly. Start by designating an out-of-area contact person. This individual should live at least 100 miles from your home, preferably in a different state. The rationale is that long-distance phone routes are often less congested than local ones after a regional disaster. Every family member's first action during an emergency is to contact this person with a brief status update using the text-first strategy.

Build the tree in tiers. The out-of-area contact is tier one. Below that, designate secondary contacts who are each responsible for reaching a specific subset of the family. For example, parent A calls the out-of-area contact and is responsible for checking on the children's school. Parent B calls parent A and coordinates with the babysitter or after-school caregiver. Grandparents call parent B. Write the complete tree on a laminated card and distribute copies to every family member, including children old enough to carry a card in a pocket. Program all numbers into phones as ICE (In Case of Emergency) contacts and practice activating the tree quarterly. Our family 72-hour emergency kit guide includes a printable contact card template.

ICE Contacts and Emergency Information on Your Phone

ICE (In Case of Emergency) contacts are pre-programmed phone entries that first responders can access to identify you and contact your family if you are injured or unconscious. Most smartphones now include a medical ID feature in the health settings that can be accessed from the lock screen without unlocking the phone. Enable this feature and fill in your full name, date of birth, blood type, known allergies, current medications, emergency contact numbers, and any relevant medical conditions. This information can save critical minutes when you cannot speak for yourself.

Beyond digital contacts, maintain a written contact list in your wallet or purse. Include all family members' phone numbers, the out-of-area contact, your physician, your pharmacist, your insurance agent, your children's school, and local emergency services. Write this list on waterproof paper and update it every six months. Store a copy in your home emergency kit and in each family member's personal go-bag. When cell phones are dead or damaged, this written list becomes your only link to the people who matter most.

Two-Way Radio Basics: FRS and GMRS

When cellular networks are down, two-way radios provide immediate, reliable communication without any infrastructure dependency. FRS (Family Radio Service) radios are the most accessible option for families because they require no license, are inexpensive (typically $25 to $50 for a pair), and offer one to two miles of range in open terrain. They operate on UHF frequencies that penetrate buildings reasonably well, making them useful for neighborhood communication during a shelter-in-place scenario. GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service) radios offer higher power output and greater range, potentially reaching five to twenty-five miles when using repeater stations, but they require an FCC license that costs $35 for a ten-year period with no examination required.

Program your radios with a set of designated channels and establish simple communication protocols before an emergency. Assign channel 1 for primary family communication, channel 2 as a backup, and channel 9 for emergency monitoring. Use plain language rather than jargon: "This is Dad at the gas station on Main Street. Heading home now. Over." Teach children how to use the radios, including the importance of releasing the push-to-talk button before listening for a response. Store spare batteries in the radio case and test the radios monthly. Two-way radios pair well with a car emergency kit for road evacuations where vehicles may become separated in traffic.

NOAA Weather Radio and Emergency Broadcasts

A NOAA Weather Radio is one of the most underappreciated emergency communication tools available. These dedicated receivers automatically broadcast alerts from the National Weather Service for your programmed area, including severe thunderstorm warnings, tornado watches, flood advisories, and civil emergency messages. They operate 24 hours a day on VHF frequencies and work even when cellular networks and internet services are completely offline. A quality NOAA weather radio costs between $20 and $60 and should be a standard component of every household emergency kit.

Look for a model with Specific Area Message Encoding (SAME) technology that allows you to program your county so you only receive alerts relevant to your location. Battery backup is essential so the radio continues working during power outages. Many models include a hand-crank generator, a USB charging port for phones, and an LED flashlight, combining multiple emergency functions in one device. Keep the radio in an accessible location near your bed or in the kitchen where your family gathers. Test it monthly using the weekly test broadcast schedule published by the National Weather Service. For families in wildfire-prone areas, a weather radio supplements the wildfire preparedness guide with real-time hazard notifications.

Satellite Communicators for Off-Grid Connectivity

Satellite communicators represent the gold standard for emergency communication in areas where all ground-based infrastructure has failed. Devices like the Garmin inReach Mini or the Zoleo satellite communicator use the Iridium satellite network to send and receive two-way text messages from anywhere on Earth, including regions with no cellular coverage whatsoever. They also feature a dedicated SOS button that connects directly to a 24/7 international emergency coordination center, which can dispatch search and rescue teams to your exact GPS coordinates.

Satellite communicators require a subscription plan that typically ranges from $15 to $50 per month depending on message volume. The devices themselves cost between $300 and $450. While this is a significant investment, for families in rural areas, regions prone to hurricanes or wildfires, or households with members who work in remote locations, the ability to communicate when all other channels fail can be genuinely lifesaving. These devices also provide real-time GPS tracking so family members can monitor each other's locations during an evacuation. Keep the satellite communicator charged and stored in a waterproof case within your go-bag. Our road trip emergency kit guide covers additional off-grid communication options for travelers.

Social Media, Internet Apps, and Backup Charging Solutions

Social media platforms have become important emergency communication tools. Facebook's Safety Check feature allows users to mark themselves as safe during a disaster, providing a quick way for friends and family to confirm a loved one's status without making a call. Apps like Zello function as walkie-talkies over Wi-Fi or cellular data and became widely used during Hurricane Harvey when residents used them to communicate rescue requests. Twitter and other microblogging platforms provide real-time situational awareness from official sources like FEMA, the National Weather Service, and local emergency management agencies.

However, internet-dependent tools are only useful if your phone has power and a data connection. Invest in at least two portable power banks rated at 10,000 mAh or higher, kept fully charged at all times. A solar charger panel rated for at least 10 watts provides extended recharging capability during multi-day outages. A car charger adapter allows you to recharge phones while driving during an evacuation. Hand-crank emergency radios with USB charging ports offer a last-resort power option when all other sources are exhausted. Reduce phone power consumption by enabling low-power mode, dimming the screen, disabling Bluetooth and Wi-Fi scanning, and closing unnecessary background apps. Pair digital communication tools with a physical emergency plan that does not depend on technology.

Written Contact Lists and Low-Tech Communication

In an era of smartphones, the humble written contact list remains an essential emergency communication tool. Phones break, batteries die, chargers are lost, and in the chaos of an evacuation, you may grab the wrong device or no device at all. A handwritten list of critical phone numbers, addresses, and meeting locations printed on waterproof paper costs nothing and works without electricity. Include full names, mobile numbers, landline numbers, work addresses, home addresses, email addresses, and any special notes such as "uses insulin" or "wheelchair-dependent."

Distribute copies of this list to every family member and store them in multiple locations: wallets, purses, glove compartments, school backpacks, office drawers, and the home emergency kit. Update the list every six months or whenever any contact information changes. In addition to phone numbers, include the address of your designated meeting locations, the out-of-area contact's information, and the phone numbers for local emergency services, hospitals, and poison control. For an additional low-tech communication method, establish a family message board protocol: if phone communication fails, leave a written note at the primary meeting location with your name, the time you were there, and your intended destination. Running Communication Drills and Maintaining Readiness

A communication plan is only effective if every family member knows their role and has practiced executing it. Schedule quarterly communication drills where you simulate a disaster scenario and have each family member activate the contact tree. Set a timer and measure how long it takes for every person to report in. Identify bottlenecks where a family member struggled to remember a phone number, could not locate their written contact list, or did not know which channel to use on the two-way radio. Use these drills to update and improve the plan.

Practice with different communication methods during each drill. One quarter, use only text messages. The next, use only two-way radios. The third, use only written messages left at the meeting location. This builds versatility and ensures your family can communicate regardless of which technologies are available. Test all backup power sources during drills to confirm they hold a charge. Review and update contact information after every drill. Involve children in age-appropriate ways by teaching them to send a specific text message to a designated contact or to use a two-way radio to check in. When every family member can independently execute the communication plan, your household is prepared for even the most challenging disaster scenarios. Frequently Asked Questions

Why should I text instead of call during an emergency?
Text messages require significantly less bandwidth than voice calls, making them far more likely to get through when cellular networks are congested. During a disaster, thousands of people simultaneously try to make calls, overwhelming tower capacity. Texts use a fraction of the bandwidth and can often be delivered even when calls fail. The FEMA-recommended strategy is to text first, call only if texting fails, and keep voice calls brief when they do connect. Send a brief message stating your safety status and location rather than attempting a lengthy conversation.
How do I design an effective family contact tree?
Start with an out-of-area contact person who lives at least 100 miles away. Every family member contacts this person first with a brief status update. Then create a chain where each person is responsible for reaching the next person on the list. Write the tree on a laminated card, program all numbers into phones as ICE contacts, and practice the tree quarterly. The out-of-area relay works because long-distance phone lines often remain operational when local networks are saturated with emergency traffic.
What is the difference between FRS and GMRS two-way radios?
FRS (Family Radio Service) radios are license-free, lower-power devices with a typical range of one to two miles in open terrain. They are ideal for family communication within a neighborhood or evacuation route. GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service) radios offer higher power output and can reach five to twenty-five miles with repeater access, but require an FCC license that costs $35 for ten years with no exam. For most families, FRS radios provide adequate range for emergency communication within their immediate area.
Are satellite communicators worth the investment?
Satellite communicators like the Garmin inReach Mini provide two-way text messaging and SOS capability anywhere on Earth without cellular coverage. They are especially valuable for families in rural areas, frequent hikers, or anyone who may need to communicate from remote locations during a disaster. Monthly subscription plans start around $15 to $50 depending on message volume. For families in areas prone to hurricanes, wildfires, or earthquakes where cell towers may be destroyed, a satellite communicator can be a lifesaving investment.
How do I keep my phone charged during a prolonged power outage?
Pack at least two portable power banks with at least 10,000 mAh capacity each, fully charged before any forecasted emergency. A 10,000 mAh bank can charge a smartphone approximately two to three times. Additionally, store a solar charger panel rated for at least 10 watts for multi-day outages. Car chargers work when vehicles are running but consume fuel. Hand-crank emergency radios with USB charging ports provide a last-resort option. Reduce phone power consumption by enabling low power mode, dimming the screen, and turning off Bluetooth and Wi-Fi when not needed.
What should I include in a written contact list?
Include full names, phone numbers (mobile and landline), email addresses, and physical addresses for all family members, out-of-area contacts, neighbors, schools, workplaces, physicians, pharmacies, insurance agents, and local emergency services. Write the list on waterproof paper and store copies in each family member's emergency kit, in the car, at the office, and in the home emergency kit. Update the list every six months and whenever any contact information changes.
