Emergency Kit Hawaii: Tsunami, Volcanic, and Hurricane Preparedness

Hawaii emergency kit guide covering tsunami warnings, volcanic eruptions, hurricanes, flooding, and island isolation. State-specific tropical disaster preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Island Life Demands Island-Level Preparedness

Hawaii occupies a unique position in American emergency preparedness because island residents face natural hazards found nowhere else in the United States while simultaneously dealing with the reality that they cannot simply drive to safety when disaster strikes. The Hawaiian Islands sit on the Pacific Ring of Fire, making them vulnerable to earthquakes and volcanic eruptions. They are exposed to Central Pacific hurricanes from June through November. They face tsunami risk from earthquakes occurring anywhere around the Pacific Basin. And flash flooding on windward mountain slopes can develop in minutes during rain events. Each of these hazards requires specific preparation, and the combined risk profile makes Hawaii one of the most complex preparedness environments in the nation.

The most critical factor that separates Hawaii from mainland preparedness is isolation. Hawaii imports approximately 85 to 90 percent of its food supply, virtually all of its fuel, and most consumer goods by ship. When port facilities are damaged or shipping routes are disrupted, replacement supplies may not arrive for weeks. Commercial flights between islands and to the mainland can be grounded for extended periods after major events. There is no driving to the mainland and no quick escape route. The Hawaii Emergency Management Agency urges residents to maintain at minimum two weeks of self-sufficiency, and many experienced preparedness experts recommend four weeks or more. Building on the principles in our family 72-hour emergency kit, Hawaii residents need to think on a much larger scale.

Tsunami Preparedness: When the Ocean Withdraws

Hawaii's tsunamis can originate from earthquakes occurring thousands of miles away across the Pacific Ocean. The 1946 Aleutian Island tsunami devastated Hilo, killing 159 people and destroying much of the downtown waterfront. The 1960 Chilean tsunami generated waves that reached Hawaii over 15 hours after the earthquake, and the 2011 Japan tsunami caused significant damage to harbors across the state. Tsunami waves are not single events but arrive in multiple surges that can continue for six to eight hours, meaning the first wave is often not the largest. When you feel strong shaking near the coast or receive a tsunami warning, move to high ground above 100 feet elevation immediately without waiting to see the wave.

Every coastal Hawaii household should have a tsunami evacuation plan with a designated route to high ground that you can complete on foot within 15 minutes. Keep a grab-and-go bag near your front door containing identification, medications, a water bottle, sturdy shoes, and important documents. Know the tsunami evacuation zone for your address through the Hawaii State GIS mapping tool. Many coastal areas in Honolulu, Hilo, Lahaina, and Lihue fall within designated zones. Practice your evacuation route with all family members and identify a meeting point on high ground in case you are separated. Volcanic Eruptions and Vog Preparedness

Hawaii is home to some of the world's most active volcanoes. Kilauea on the Big Island has erupted almost continuously for decades, and Mauna Loa, the largest active volcano on Earth, erupted in November 2022 for the first time since 1984. Volcanic hazards in Hawaii include lava flows that can destroy entire communities as demonstrated in the 2018 lower Puna eruption that covered over 700 homes, volcanic ash that damages engines and respiratory systems, and volcanic vog, a persistent mixture of sulfur dioxide gas and fine particles that can blanket entire islands for weeks or months. The 2018 eruption created a health crisis on the Big Island as toxic laze plumes and sulfur dioxide levels reached dangerous concentrations across the Kau district.

Volcanic preparedness supplies should include P100 respirator masks or gas masks with sulfur dioxide cartridges for every household member, protective goggles, sealed water in closed containers since vog can infiltrate open supplies, and HEPA air purifiers for indoor use during vog events. If you live on the Big Island or Maui, monitor USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory updates and Hawaii Department of Health air quality advisories. Develop an evacuation plan that accounts for lava flow directions, which can cut off roads without warning. For homeowners near volcanic zones, maintain a home emergency kit that includes supplies for extended indoor sheltering when outdoor air quality is hazardous.

Hurricane Preparedness for Island Residents

Hawaii sits in the Central Pacific hurricane basin, and while direct hits are less frequent than in the Caribbean, the consequences are severe because there is nowhere to evacuate. Hurricane Iniki in 1992 struck Kauai as a Category 4 storm with 145 mph winds, destroying or damaging more than 90 percent of the island's structures and leaving residents without power for weeks. Hurricane Lane in 2018 brought catastrophic flooding to the Big Island with over 50 inches of rain in some areas. Hurricanes produce four distinct threats in Hawaii: destructive winds, catastrophic flooding, storm surge that can reach 15 feet or more, and dangerous surf that can damage coastal infrastructure days before the storm arrives.

When a hurricane watch is issued for your island, immediately begin preparations. Board or tape all windows, bring outdoor furniture and loose objects indoors, fill every vehicle fuel tank, stock at least seven days of food and water beyond your normal supply, and charge all electronic devices and power banks. Move essential documents and your go-bag to the safest interior room of your home. If you are in a coastal or low-lying evacuation zone, leave immediately while roads are still passable. Once a hurricane warning is in effect, travel may become impossible. Keep a battery-powered NOAA weather radio monitoring the Central Pacific Hurricane Center for updates. Fresh Water Scarcity and Storage

Fresh water is a precious and limited resource in Hawaii. The islands depend on rainfall-fed aquifers for drinking water, and drought conditions periodically stress supply levels across the state. After a major earthquake or volcanic event, water treatment plants and distribution systems can be damaged or contaminated. Saltwater intrusion from tsunami surge or storm flooding can compromise coastal freshwater sources. The 2018 Kilauea eruption contaminated freshwater wells in the lower Puna district, forcing residents to rely on trucked water deliveries for months. Even routine events like heavy rain can cause turbidity issues that temporarily reduce water treatment capacity.

Store a minimum of 14 days of drinking water for every household member, with 21 days recommended for Hawaii's unique supply chain risks. Use food-grade containers stored in cool dark locations and rotate the supply every six months. A family of four needs at least 56 gallons for two weeks at the standard one gallon per person per day rate. Consider investing in a rainwater catchment system with proper filtration if you live on a neighbor island where municipal water infrastructure is limited. Keep water purification tablets and a portable filter as backup options. Our emergency water storage guide covers container types, purification methods, and rotation schedules in detail.

Neighbor Island Challenges: Maui, Big Island, Kauai, and Molokai

While Honolulu on Oahu has the state's largest concentration of emergency services, hospitals, and government resources, neighbor island residents face far greater vulnerability during disasters. Maui's Maui Memorial Medical Center is the only hospital serving the entire island and was overwhelmed during the 2023 Lahaina wildfire disaster that killed 101 people and destroyed the historic town. The Big Island's vast geography means some communities are hours away from the nearest hospital. Kauai's North Shore has been cut off for days during flood events when the only road through Wainiha was washed out. Molokai and Lanai have even more limited medical and emergency infrastructure.

Neighbor island residents must plan for significantly longer emergency response times and more limited medical care. Maintain a more comprehensive first aid and trauma kit than standard recommendations, including prescription medications for at least 30 days. Know the location and capacity of your island's nearest hospital and understand that medical evacuation to Oahu may be impossible during a disaster. Build community-level emergency plans with neighbors, since mutual aid networks are often the first line of response. Keep vehicles maintained and fueled, since road closures on island can isolate entire communities. Our home emergency kit guide covers medical supplies and community planning in detail.

Flash Flooding on Windward Slopes

The windward sides of Hawaii's volcanic mountains receive extraordinary amounts of rainfall, with Mount Waialeale on Kauai averaging over 460 inches per year, making it one of the wettest spots on Earth. This constant rainfall creates steep, narrow valleys and stream channels that can transform from dry washes to raging floodwaters in minutes during heavy rain events. Flash floods are the most common natural disaster in Hawaii and claim lives every year when campers, hikers, or drivers are caught in rising water. The August 2023 flash floods on Oahu trapped drivers on Kamehameha Highway and forced water rescues in multiple locations across the island.

Never camp, park, or hike in streambeds, valleys, or low-lying coastal areas when rain is forecast, even if the sky appears clear at your location. Rain falling miles upwind in the mountains can send a wall of water downstream with no local warning. Monitor National Weather Service flash flood watches and warnings for your area and take them seriously. If you live near a stream or gulch, keep sandbags, a sump pump, and elevated storage for valuables. Know how to move to higher floors in your home if flooding begins. Keep your 72-hour emergency supplies in waterproof containers stored above potential flood levels.

Supply Chain Dependence and Extended Self-Sufficiency

Hawaii's geographic isolation is both its greatest beauty and its greatest vulnerability in an emergency. Container ships from the West Coast take approximately five to seven days to reach Hawaiian ports, and fuel tankers follow similar schedules. When these supply chains are disrupted by port damage, fuel shortages, or global shipping disruptions, store shelves empty within hours. During the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic, Hawaii experienced food and supply shortages that gave residents a preview of what a major disaster could bring. Local food production accounts for only 10 to 15 percent of the state's consumption, meaning the vast majority of what Hawaiians eat arrives by ship.

Build an emergency food supply that can sustain your household for a minimum of two weeks, with four weeks as the target for well-prepared families. Focus on calorie-dense, shelf-stable foods including canned meats, rice, beans, pasta, peanut butter, dried fruit, and freeze-dried meals. Invest in a backup cooking method such as a propane camp stove or solar oven. Grow food-producing plants if possible, even container gardens on lanais can supplement stored food. Join a community garden or cooperative food storage group to share resources and reduce individual costs. Stock pet food, baby formula, and any specialty dietary items your family requires. Honolulu Urban Preparedness and Evacuation Planning

Honolulu presents unique urban emergency challenges amplified by island geography. The city is the state's largest population center with approximately 350,000 residents in the urban core and over one million in the metropolitan area. Dense development along the coastal plain means that tsunami evacuation requires moving significant numbers of people uphill through limited routes. High-rise buildings face earthquake risk, and older structures may not meet current seismic codes. The H-1 freeway is the primary east-west transportation artery and a single major incident could paralyze evacuation across southern Oahu. Military installations and Pearl Harbor add additional complexity to emergency planning in the Honolulu area.

Honolulu residents should identify their tsunami evacuation zone and know at least two routes to high ground from every location they frequent, including home, work, and your children's school. Keep a grab-and-go bag at home, at work, and in your vehicle. Register for the City and County of Honolulu's emergency notification system to receive alerts about tsunamis, hurricanes, and other developing emergencies. Know the location of your nearest emergency shelter and understand the shelter rules including what supplies to bring. For high-rise residents, practice stairwell evacuation since elevators will not function during earthquakes or power outages. Maintain a backup power system capable of running essential communication and medical devices during extended outages.

Frequently Asked Questions

How quickly can a tsunami reach Hawaii after an earthquake?
A tsunami generated by an earthquake along the Pacific Ring of Fire can reach Hawaiian shores in as little as 30 minutes to several hours depending on the distance and magnitude of the source event. The 2011 Japan tsunami arrived in Hawaii roughly 5 hours after the earthquake, while a closer source like a Cascadia event could produce waves in under an hour. This is why immediate evacuation to high ground after feeling strong shaking near the coast is critical in Hawaii.
How does volcanic vog affect emergency preparedness in Hawaii?
Volcanic vog is a mixture of sulfur dioxide gas and fine ash particles emitted by active volcanoes like Kilauea. It causes respiratory problems, eye irritation, and can worsen asthma and heart conditions. During prolonged eruption events, vog can blanket entire islands for weeks. Keep N95 masks, eye drops, sealed water supplies, and HEPA air filters in your emergency kit. Monitor Hawaii Department of Health air quality advisories and keep windows and doors closed during heavy vog conditions.
How much food and water should I store for island isolation?
Hawaii imports approximately 85 to 90 percent of its food supply, and all fuel and most goods arrive by ship. After a major disaster, shipping disruptions could leave island residents without resupply for weeks or longer. Store at least two weeks of food and water for every household member, with four weeks recommended. Fresh water is also limited since Hawaii depends on rainfall-fed aquifers. A family of four should store at least 56 gallons of water for a two-week supply.
What should I do during a hurricane warning in Hawaii?
When a hurricane warning is issued, immediately board or tape windows, move outdoor furniture and loose items indoors, fill your vehicle fuel tank, stock extra water and food for at least seven days, and charge all electronic devices. Identify the safest room in your home away from windows. If you are in an evacuation zone, leave immediately. Hawaii hurricanes can produce catastrophic flooding, storm surge up to 15 feet, and winds exceeding 150 mph. The lack of evacuation routes off-island means you must shelter in place once a hurricane threatens.
What makes neighbor island preparedness different from Honolulu?
Neighbor islands like Maui, the Big Island, Kauai, and Molokai have smaller populations, fewer emergency services, limited hospital capacity, and more restricted evacuation routes. Medical evacuations to Oahu may be impossible during a disaster. Stores carry smaller inventories that can be depleted quickly. Rural areas on each island may have only one road in and out. Neighbor island residents should maintain larger emergency food stores and more comprehensive medical supplies than urban Honolulu residents.
How do I prepare for flash flooding on windward sides of the islands?
Windward sides of Hawaiian islands receive heavy rainfall year-round, and flash floods can develop rapidly in narrow valleys and stream beds. Never camp or park in streambeds or low-lying areas during rain events. Keep sandbags and a sump pump ready if you live in a flood-prone area near a stream or gulch. Monitor National Weather Service flash flood watches and warnings. Know your home elevation relative to nearby streams, and have a plan to move to higher floors or evacuate if water begins rising.
Can I really be stuck on my island for weeks after a disaster?
Yes, this is a real and serious possibility in Hawaii. After a major earthquake, tsunami, or hurricane, port facilities may be damaged and commercial flights may be grounded for days to weeks. The inter-island ferry system has limited capacity and was disrupted for extended periods after previous disasters. Hurricane Iniki in 1992 left Kauai residents isolated for days. During the 2018 Kilauea eruption, entire communities on the Big Island were cut off by lava flows for months. Extended self-sufficiency planning is not optional in Hawaii.
