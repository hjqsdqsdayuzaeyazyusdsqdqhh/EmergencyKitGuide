Home Emergency Kit vs Car Kit: Which Do You Need?

Home emergency kit vs car kit â€” understand the differences in contents, purpose, and storage. Learn why you need both for complete preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Understanding the Home Emergency Kit

A home emergency kit is designed to sustain your entire household during a shelter-in-place scenario or the critical first hours after a disaster. It assumes you have access to your home's structure, your kitchen, and your closets, but that external services like power, water, gas, and food delivery have been disrupted. The kit supplements what you already have at home with critical supplies that may be unavailable for days or even weeks.

A comprehensive home emergency kit includes large quantities of water, enough non-perishable food for every family member for at least 72 hours, a robust first aid kit, multiple flashlights and lanterns with extra batteries, a battery-powered or hand-crank weather radio, tools for turning off utilities, important document copies, cash, hygiene supplies, and seasonal clothing. Because it is stored in a fixed location, weight and size are less of a concern than with a portable kit. Our home emergency kit guide provides a complete breakdown of what to include.

Understanding the Car Emergency Kit

A car emergency kit is a compact, portable set of supplies stored in your vehicle. It is designed for roadside emergencies like flat tires, dead batteries, engine trouble, and minor accidents, as well as larger scenarios where you might be stranded or need to evacuate. The car kit is your safety net when you are away from home and cannot access your household supplies. Considering the average American spends nearly an hour per day in their vehicle, being prepared in your car is just as important as being prepared at home.

Car kits prioritize compactness and portability because trunk space is limited. They typically include jumper cables or a portable jump starter, a tire repair kit or portable air compressor, basic tools, a flashlight, reflective triangles or road flares, a first aid kit, emergency blankets, water pouches, high-calorie snack bars, a whistle, a seatbelt cutter, and a window breaker. Seasonal additions might include an ice scraper in winter, sun protection in summer, or sand for traction in icy conditions. Our car emergency kit guide covers the full list.

Purpose Comparison: Why They Differ

The fundamental difference between a home kit and a car kit is the scenario each one addresses. A home kit is built for disasters that disrupt infrastructure while you remain at your residence: power outages, severe storms, earthquakes, floods, or chemical incidents that require sheltering in place. The goal is to keep your family fed, hydrated, warm, and informed until normal services resume.

A car kit serves a dual purpose. First, it addresses everyday vehicle emergencies: flat tires, dead batteries, overheated engines, and minor injuries from roadside incidents. Second, it serves as a portable emergency supply if you are away from home when a disaster strikes. If an earthquake damages buildings while you are at work, your car kit provides essential supplies until you can get home or reach a shelter. For fire emergencies in your vehicle, review our fire blanket guide for an important addition to consider.

Content Differences: What Goes Where

Home kits contain bulk supplies: gallons of water, cases of canned food, full-size first aid kits, sleeping bags, multiple flashlights and lanterns, large battery-powered radios, and enough supplies for the entire family. The emphasis is on quantity and completeness. A family of four needs at least 12 gallons of water and enough food for 72 hours in their home kit.

Car kits use compact, space-efficient alternatives. Water comes in pouches or small bottles. Food comes in calorie-dense bars or compact snack packs. First aid kits are smaller and focused on wound care and pain relief. Car kits add vehicle-specific items like jumper cables, tire gauges, reflective triangles, and basic tools that have no place in a home kit. The goal is to pack the essentials into a bag that fits in your trunk alongside your other belongings. For water solutions that work in both settings, see our emergency water storage guide.

Storage Considerations for Each Kit

Home kits are stored in closets, garages, basements, or under beds. The ideal location is cool, dry, and easily accessible. A hall closet near the front door is a popular choice because it is easy to grab in an emergency. Avoid storing your kit in areas prone to flooding or extreme temperatures. Waterproof containers protect supplies from moisture and pests.

Car kits face unique storage challenges. Vehicles experience extreme temperature swings that can damage food, melt plastic containers, corrode batteries, and degrade adhesives. Store your car kit in an insulated bag and check it seasonally. In summer, move heat-sensitive items to the passenger compartment or remove them entirely. In winter, ensure you have warm clothing and blankets. The trunk is generally better than the backseat for temperature stability. Our car emergency kit guide covers seasonal storage strategies.

Overlapping Items: What Both Kits Share

Several items appear in both home and car kits: water, non-perishable food, a flashlight, a first aid kit, emergency blankets, a whistle, and a multi-tool or knife. The specific versions of these items may differ based on portability requirements, but the core need is the same. Having overlapping items means you are never more than a few feet from essential supplies, whether you are at home or on the road.

This overlap also creates an opportunity for savings. When purchasing supplies, buy in bulk and distribute items between your home kit and car kit. For example, a case of emergency water pouches can be split between both kits. A multi-pack of flashlights provides one for the house and one for the car. This approach reduces waste and ensures consistency in the quality of your gear. For power solutions that work across both settings, check our emergency power backup guide.

Why You Need Both Kits

Relying on only one kit leaves dangerous gaps. A family with only a home kit is vulnerable if a disaster strikes while they are driving. An earthquake during your commute, a wildfire that cuts off your route home, or a vehicle breakdown during a winter storm all leave you without supplies if your car is empty. Conversely, a family with only a car kit is under-equipped at home, where they spend most of their time and where a major disaster is most likely to affect them.

The American Red Cross and FEMA recommend having emergency supplies both at home and in your vehicle. This dual-kit approach ensures you are prepared regardless of your location. If you are at home when an emergency occurs, your home kit sustains you. If you are on the road, your car kit bridges the gap until you can reach safety. For families in hurricane zones, our hurricane emergency kit guide emphasizes why having supplies in both locations is critical during evacuation scenarios.

Seasonal Considerations for Each Kit

Both kits need seasonal updates to remain effective. In winter, your home kit should include extra warm blankets, hand warmers, and cold-weather clothing. Your car kit needs an ice scraper, a small shovel, kitty litter or sand for traction, warm gloves, and a heavy blanket or sleeping bag. Winter is the most dangerous season for vehicle emergencies because of ice, snow, and freezing temperatures.

In summer, both kits need heat-appropriate items. Your home kit should include sun protection, extra water, and lightweight clothing. Your car kit needs extra water, sunscreen, and sunshade for the windshield. Heat-related vehicle breakdowns increase in summer, making a well-stocked car kit especially important. For families in earthquake zones, our earthquake emergency kit guide recommends year-round readiness since earthquakes have no seasonal pattern.

Building Both Kits on a Budget

You do not need to build both kits at once. Start with whichever kit fills your most immediate gap. If you have no supplies at home, begin there with a basic 72-hour kit covering water, food, first aid, and lighting. If you have nothing in your car, add a compact kit with jumper cables, a flashlight, water, snacks, and a first aid kit before tackling your home supplies.

Spread purchases over several months to manage costs. Buy one or two items per week and add them to the appropriate kit. Shop sales during preparedness month in September, after-holiday clearance sales, and back-to-school promotions for discounts on flashlights, batteries, and backpacks. Reuse containers you already own: a sturdy duffel bag or storage bin works well for a home kit, and a compact backpack fits perfectly in most trunks. Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need both a home kit and a car kit?
Yes, both serve different purposes. A home kit is designed for shelter-in-place scenarios where you remain at home during a disaster. A car kit is designed for roadside emergencies, breakdowns, and evacuation scenarios where you are away from home. Having both ensures you are prepared regardless of where you are when an emergency occurs. Most people spend significant time commuting and traveling, making a car kit just as essential as a home kit.
What should be in a home emergency kit that is not in a car kit?
A home emergency kit should include larger items that cannot be stored in a vehicle: gallons of water, cases of canned food, sleeping bags, a larger first aid kit, multiple flashlights, a battery-powered weather radio, important documents, cash, hygiene supplies for the whole family, and seasonal clothing. Home kits are designed for multiple people over several days, while car kits are typically for one to two people for 24 to 48 hours.
How do I keep my car emergency kit from getting too hot?
Store your car kit in an insulated bag or cooler to protect temperature-sensitive items. Avoid leaving water bottles in direct sunlight for extended periods, as chemicals from the plastic can leach into the water. Choose LED flashlights over incandescent, and use lithium batteries which handle heat better than alkaline. Check your car kit every season and replace melted or expired items. Some people keep their car kit in the trunk rather than the passenger compartment to reduce heat exposure.
What items overlap between a home kit and a car kit?
Both kits share core emergency items: water, non-perishable food, first aid supplies, a flashlight, emergency blankets, a whistle, and a multi-tool. However, the form factor differs. Car kits use compact water pouches or purification tablets instead of gallon jugs. They use calorie-dense ration bars instead of canned goods. The overlap means you can maintain some items in common while specializing the rest for each location.
How often should I update each kit?
Update both kits every six months. For your home kit, replace expired food and water, rotate seasonal clothing, refresh medications, and test electronics. For your car kit, check that batteries have not corroded, replace water pouches, update seasonal items like sun protection in summer and warm gear in winter, and verify that jumper cables and tools are still in good condition. A good schedule is to check both kits when the clocks change for daylight saving time.
