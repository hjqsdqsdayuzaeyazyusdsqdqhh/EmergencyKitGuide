Family 72-Hour Emergency Kit: Complete Guide for Every Household

Build a family 72-hour emergency kit with our complete guide. Learn what supplies every family member needs for 3 days of emergency preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Every Family Needs a 72-Hour Emergency Kit

Disasters rarely announce themselves. Whether it is a severe hurricane, an earthquake, a wildfire, or a widespread power outage, emergencies can strike with little to no warning. According to FEMA, every household should be prepared to be self-sufficient for at least 72 hours following a major emergency. That means having enough water, food, medical supplies, and essential gear to keep your entire family safe and healthy until help arrives or normal services resume.

A family 72-hour kit is not just a collection of random items thrown into a backpack. It is a carefully planned inventory designed to address the specific needs of every person in your household. Infants require different supplies than teenagers, and elderly family members may need medications or mobility aids that others do not. In this guide, we walk you through every category of supplies, explain how to calculate quantities for your family size, and share storage and rotation strategies that keep your kit ready at all times.

Water: The Most Critical Supply

Water is the single most important item in any emergency kit. FEMA and the Red Cross recommend storing at least one gallon of water per person per day, split between drinking and sanitation. For a family of four, that translates to a minimum of 12 gallons for three days. In hot climates or for families with nursing mothers or young children, two gallons per person per day is a safer target.

Store water in food-grade plastic containers such as commercially bottled water or specially designed water storage drums. Avoid containers that previously held milk or juice, as residual sugars can promote bacterial growth. Label each container with the date it was filled and replace the supply every six months. If space is limited, consider compact water purification tablets or a portable filter like a Sawyer Mini as a backup water source. Our 72-hour water storage guide covers purification methods and container options in greater detail.

Non-Perishable Food for the Whole Family

Your family's emergency food supply should provide at least 2,000 calories per adult per day and roughly 1,400 to 1,800 calories for children, depending on age and activity level. Focus on foods that require no refrigeration, minimal preparation, and have a long shelf life. Canned goods, freeze-dried meals, peanut butter, crackers, granola bars, dried fruit, and powdered milk are all excellent choices.

Pay attention to dietary restrictions and preferences. Families with infants should stock formula, baby food, and bottled water for mixing. Those with elderly members may need soft foods or nutritional supplement drinks. Pack a manual can opener, disposable utensils, paper plates, and wet wipes for sanitation. Rotate your food supply every six to twelve months to ensure freshness. Check our complete 72-hour emergency food list for specific product recommendations and calorie planning.

First Aid and Medical Supplies

A well-stocked first aid kit is essential for any family emergency supply. Start with a comprehensive commercial first aid kit and supplement it with items specific to your family's needs. Include adhesive bandages in various sizes, sterile gauze pads, medical tape, antibiotic ointment, burn cream, pain relievers such as ibuprofen and acetaminophen, antihistamines for allergic reactions, and an elastic bandage for sprains.

Beyond basic first aid, include a supply of any prescription medications your family members take regularly. Aim for at least a seven-day supply, as emergencies may disrupt pharmacy access for longer than 72 hours. Keep a written list of all medications, dosages, and prescribing doctors. Include a basic first aid manual or laminated instruction card. Consider taking a CPR and first aid course through the American Red Cross so your family has the knowledge to respond effectively when every second counts.

Shelter, Warmth, and Clothing

Even if you shelter in place during an emergency, you may need to deal with a broken window, a leaking roof, or a loss of heating. Pack emergency Mylar space blankets for each family member, along with at least one larger tarp or emergency tent that can provide additional shelter. Heavy-duty plastic sheeting and duct tape can seal broken windows and doors temporarily.

Each family member should have a complete change of weather-appropriate clothing stored in a waterproof bag. Include sturdy closed-toe shoes, extra socks, rain gear, and warm layers for cold weather. In warm climates, pack lightweight breathable clothing and sun hats. Sleeping bags or compact sleeping pads provide comfort and insulation during overnight situations. Remember to include items for infants and toddlers such as extra onesies, sleep sacks, and swaddling blankets.

Lighting, Tools, and Communication

When the power goes out, reliable lighting becomes a top priority. Pack at least two LED flashlights per family member, along with a generous supply of extra batteries. Headlamps are particularly useful because they keep your hands free for tasks. A battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio keeps you informed about ongoing hazards and rescue operations.

Your tool kit should include a multi-tool, a Swiss Army knife or similar folding tool, a wrench for turning off utilities, sturdy work gloves, and a whistle for signaling rescuers. For communication, pack a portable power bank charged to full capacity, a set of charging cables compatible with your family's devices, and a list of emergency contact numbers written on paper in case your phone dies. Two-way radios provide reliable short-range communication when cell networks are overwhelmed. Learn more about building a comprehensive home emergency kit.

Personal Documents and Cash

In a disaster, you may need to evacuate quickly and prove your identity or insurance coverage. Store photocopies of essential documents in a waterproof, fireproof bag. Include photo IDs, insurance policies, birth certificates, social security cards, passports, vehicle registration, bank account information, and a recent family photo. Digital copies on an encrypted USB drive provide an additional backup.

Cash is critical during power outages when credit card machines and ATMs are offline. Keep a mix of small bills, coins, and at least $200 to $300 in your family emergency kit. Store it in a sealed waterproof bag. Do not forget important personal items like eyeglasses, hearing aid batteries, contact lens supplies, and comfort items for children such as a small toy, a deck of cards, or coloring supplies to reduce stress during a frightening situation.

Special Considerations: Babies, Seniors, and Pets

Families with babies need to plan for formula, bottles, disposable diapers, wipes, baby food, and any over-the-counter infant medications such as fever reducers. Pack a portable crib or travel bassinet and a baby carrier so you can keep your hands free. For elderly family members, ensure an adequate supply of prescription medications, extra eyeglasses, hearing aid batteries, and any assistive devices they depend on daily.

Pets are family too. Pack at least a three-day supply of pet food, water bowls, a leash and collar with identification tags, any needed medications, copies of vaccination records, and a pet carrier. Include a comfort item like a favorite toy or blanket to reduce anxiety. Some emergency shelters do not accept animals, so identify pet-friendly evacuation locations in advance. Our kids-specific emergency kit guide has more detailed advice for families with young children.

Storage, Rotation, and Maintenance Schedule

Building your kit is only half the equation. Proper storage and regular maintenance ensure your supplies are ready when you need them. Store your family emergency kit in a cool, dry, easily accessible location. A hall closet near the front door or a shelf in the garage are good choices. Keep a smaller, portable version in each vehicle and at each family member's workplace if possible.

Set a reminder to review and update your kit every six months. Replace expired food and water, test flashlights and radios, rotate seasonal clothing, and refresh medications. As your children grow, update clothing sizes and adjust calorie requirements. Involve every family member in the review process so everyone knows what is in the kit and where items are located. Practice your evacuation plan at least twice a year so that in a real emergency, your family can move quickly and confidently. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to build a family 72-hour kit?
A complete family 72-hour kit typically costs between $150 and $400 depending on family size and quality of supplies. Budget-friendly options start around $75 for basics, while premium kits with higher-quality gear can run $500 or more. Spreading purchases over several months is an effective way to manage costs without sacrificing preparedness.
How much water should I store for my family?
Store one gallon of water per person per day for a minimum of three days. A family of four needs at least 12 gallons. For added comfort, including water for cooking and basic hygiene, aim for two gallons per person per day. This means a family of four should store 24 gallons for a 72-hour period.
Should each family member have their own kit?
The best approach is a combination. Keep a large shared kit with bulk supplies like water, food, and tools in one central location. Then pack individual grab-and-go bags for each family member containing personal items like medications, a change of clothes, comfortable shoes, personal hygiene products, and a small flashlight. If your family must evacuate quickly, everyone can grab their personal bag.
How often should I update our family emergency kit?
Review your family emergency kit every six months. Replace expired food and water, test batteries and electronics, update prescription medications, and rotate seasonal clothing. A convenient schedule is to check your kit every time the clocks change for daylight saving time in spring and fall.
What special items should I include for babies or elderly family members?
For babies, include formula, bottles, diapers, wipes, baby food, a change of clothes, and comfort items like a favorite toy. For elderly family members, pack any prescription medications, extra eyeglasses, hearing aid batteries, mobility aids, and medical alert information. Consider dietary needs such as soft foods or nutritional supplements for those with difficulty eating regular emergency rations.
