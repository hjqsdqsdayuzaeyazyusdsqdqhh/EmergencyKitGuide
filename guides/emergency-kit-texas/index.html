Emergency Kit Texas: Hurricane, Tornado, and Extreme Heat Preparedness

Texas emergency kit guide covering hurricanes on the Gulf Coast, tornadoes in North Texas, extreme heat, flooding, and winter storms. State-specific prep tips.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Texas's Diverse Disaster Landscape

Texas is the second-largest state in the nation, and its sheer geographic diversity means residents face a wide spectrum of natural and human-caused disasters. Gulf Coast communities from Corpus Christi to Galveston to Beaumont contend with hurricane landfalls, catastrophic storm surge, and tropical rainfall that can dump 20 or more inches of rain in a single event. North Texas and the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex sit in the heart of Tornado Alley, where supercell thunderstorms generate violent tornadoes during spring and early summer. Central Texas Hill Country is one of the most flash-flood-prone regions in the United States, with narrow canyons channeling stormwater into deadly surges with alarming speed.

Beyond these seasonal threats, Texas endures extreme heat waves with temperatures regularly exceeding 100 degrees Fahrenheit for weeks at a time, causing dangerous heat-related illnesses. West Texas faces dust storms and wildfire risk during dry periods. The February 2021 winter storm, known as Uri, exposed the state's vulnerability to extreme cold events that paralyzed the electrical grid for days, leaving millions without power, heat, or running water. This multi-hazard environment means a Texas emergency kit must be far more versatile than a kit designed for a single-threat region. Your preparedness strategy must account for storms that arrive with hours of warning alongside events like flash floods and tornadoes that can develop in minutes.

Hurricane Preparedness for Gulf Coast Texas

The Texas Gulf Coast is highly vulnerable to hurricane landfalls, and the warm waters of the Gulf of Mexico can rapidly intensify tropical systems before they reach shore. Hurricane Harvey in 2017 demonstrated the devastating potential of slow-moving tropical systems, dumping over 60 inches of rain on parts of the Houston metropolitan area and causing catastrophic flooding that damaged hundreds of thousands of homes. Residents within 50 miles of the coast should build a hurricane-specific go-bag that includes at least seven days of water and food, important documents in a waterproof pouch, a battery-powered weather radio, a full tank of gas, and cash in small bills.

Install hurricane shutters or pre-cut plywood panels for all windows well before the start of hurricane season on June 1. Reinforce garage doors, which are the most common point of structural failure during high winds. Elevate critical electrical panels and appliances if your home is in a flood zone, and consider purchasing flood insurance through the National Flood Insurance Program, as standard homeowners policies do not cover flood damage. Know your evacuation route along I-10, I-45, or US-59 and follow mandatory evacuation orders without delay. Tornado Safety Across North and Central Texas

North Texas and the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex experience some of the highest tornado frequencies in the world. Spring severe weather season, typically from March through June, brings supercell thunderstorms capable of producing violent EF4 and EF5 tornadoes with winds exceeding 200 miles per hour. The 2016 Garland-Rowlett tornado and the 2015 Van tornado are reminders that Texas tornadoes strike populated areas with devastating consequences. Unlike hurricanes, tornadoes provide almost no advance warning, making immediate shelter and family communication plans essential components of your preparedness strategy.

Identify the safest room in your home, which should be an interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows, such as a bathroom, closet, or hallway. Install a FEMA-rated tornado safe room or reinforced shelter if your home lacks an adequate safe space, especially if you live in a mobile home or manufactured housing, which offers virtually no protection in a tornado. Keep a NOAA weather radio with battery backup and alert function in your shelter area at all times during storm season. Pack helmets or heavy blankets to protect against flying debris, which causes the majority of tornado injuries. Practice your family tornado drill at least twice each spring so every household member knows exactly where to go and what to do. Extreme Heat Survival in the Texas Summer

Texas summers are punishingly hot, with July and August averages routinely pushing temperatures above 100 degrees Fahrenheit across much of the state. Heat-related illness is the leading cause of weather-related death in Texas, claiming more lives each year than hurricanes, tornadoes, and floods combined. Extended power outages during heat waves are particularly dangerous, as air conditioning failure in poorly insulated Texas homes can turn living spaces into ovens within hours. The elderly, young children, outdoor workers, and people without access to air conditioning are at the highest risk of heat exhaustion and heat stroke.

Build a heat emergency component into your kit that includes extra water beyond the standard one-gallon-per-person-per-day guideline, electrolyte replacement packets, a battery-powered portable fan, cooling towels, lightweight and light-colored clothing, and a wide-brimmed sun hat. Identify the nearest cooling center operated by your city or county, as many Texas municipalities open public buildings with air conditioning during declared heat emergencies. Learn the warning signs of heat exhaustion, including heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, and dizziness, and know that heat stroke is a medical emergency requiring immediate 911 response. Keep your car's air conditioning system serviced and never leave children or pets in a parked vehicle. Our home emergency kit guide includes heat-specific preparation steps.

Flash Flooding and the Hill Country

The Texas Hill Country, stretching from Austin through San Antonio and westward, is one of the most flash-flood-prone regions in the United States. Steep terrain, thin rocky soils, and narrow creek valleys funnel rainwater into rapid, powerful surges that can sweep away vehicles, erode roads, and inundate homes in minutes. The Memorial Day flood of 2015 and the Halloween flood of 2015 in Central Texas killed dozens of people, many of whom were trapped in vehicles on flooded roads. Flash flooding is the leading cause of storm-related deaths in Texas, and the saying "turn around, do not drown" could not be more relevant for Texas drivers.

Never drive through flooded roadways, regardless of how shallow the water appears. Six inches of moving water can knock an adult off their feet, and two feet of water can float most vehicles. Know the flood-prone areas near your home, workplace, and regular travel routes. Install a NOAA weather radio with the alert function activated so you receive flash flood warnings immediately, even at night. If you live near a creek or river in the Hill Country, establish a family plan to move to higher ground the moment a flash flood watch is issued. Keep a go-bag in your car with at least one day of food, water, and basic supplies in case you are stranded by rising water. Winter Storm Preparedness and the 2021 Grid Failure

The February 2021 winter storm Uri was a wake-up call for Texas residents who had never considered cold-weather emergencies a serious threat. An unprecedented Arctic blast sent temperatures plummeting to single digits across the state, freezing natural gas wells, wind turbines, and power lines simultaneously. The resulting grid failure left more than four million homes without electricity for days in temperatures well below freezing. Burst pipes caused billions of dollars in water damage, and hundreds of Texans died from hypothermia, carbon monoxide poisoning from improper generator and heater use, and medical equipment failures.

Every Texas household should now prepare for the possibility of a multi-day winter power outage. Stock extra blankets, sleeping bags rated to zero degrees or below, layered warm clothing, and a safe backup heating source such as a vented kerosene heater or a properly ventilated propane heater. Never use a gas stove, oven, or charcoal grill for indoor heating, as carbon monoxide poisoning is one of the deadliest risks during power outages. Insulate exposed water pipes with foam covers and know how to shut off your home's water main to prevent burst pipe damage. Fill your bathtub with water before a freeze begins so you can flush toilets if water pressure drops. Keep at least seven days of non-perishable food and a manual can opener. Evacuation Planning and Texas Transportation Routes

Effective evacuation planning in Texas requires understanding the specific routes and challenges of your region. Gulf Coast evacuations typically use I-10 westbound toward San Antonio, I-45 northbound toward Dallas, or US-59 northbound toward Lufkin. These routes become severely congested during mandatory evacuations, so leaving early when a hurricane watch is issued is far safer than waiting for the mandatory order. The Texas Department of Transportation activates contraflow lanes on major highways during large-scale evacuations, reversing traffic flow to double outbound capacity, but this process takes hours to implement.

Inland Texas residents also need evacuation plans for flash floods, wildfires, and tornado events. Know the location of the nearest public shelter in your county, including pet-friendly shelters if you have animals. Register with your county's emergency notification system to receive reverse-911 alerts and text message notifications. Keep your vehicle fueled to at least half full at all times during hurricane and severe weather seasons, and store a go-bag in the trunk with a day's worth of supplies for each family member. For multi-hazard evacuation preparation, review our family 72-hour emergency kit guide to ensure every household member has a personal grab-and-go bag ready.

Regional Considerations Across Texas

Texas is enormous, and the specific disaster risks vary dramatically by region. Houston and the upper Gulf Coast face hurricane storm surge, catastrophic rainfall flooding from slow-moving tropical systems, and industrial chemical hazards from the petrochemical corridor along the Ship Channel. San Antonio and Austin sit at the edge of Flash Flood Alley, where steep terrain turns ordinary thunderstorms into deadly flood events. Dallas-Fort Worth must prepare for tornadoes, severe hail, straight-line winds, and ice storms during winter. El Paso faces extreme desert heat, wildfire risk, and flash flooding from monsoon thunderstorms.

West Texas rural communities face unique challenges including long distances to medical facilities, limited cell phone coverage, and dependence on well water that may be compromised after seismic activity or flooding. The Texas Panhandle contends with blizzards, high winds, and wildfire risk during dry periods. Regardless of where you live in Texas, your emergency kit should address at least two or three different hazard types, and your evacuation plan should account for the possibility that normal travel routes may be impassable. Contact your county emergency management coordinator for region-specific hazard information and visit tdem.texas.gov for state-level preparedness resources and disaster updates.

Texas-Specific Kit Additions and Seasonal Maintenance

Beyond the standard emergency supplies of water, food, first aid, and shelter materials, Texas residents should add several state-specific items to their kits. Pack sunscreen with SPF 50 or higher and lip balm with UV protection for extreme sun exposure during summer emergencies. Include insect repellent with DEET, as standing water after floods and hurricanes creates massive mosquito breeding grounds that increase the risk of West Nile virus and other mosquito-borne illnesses. Add waterproof dry bags to protect electronics and documents during flood evacuations. If you have livestock or large animals, develop a separate evacuation plan with trailer arrangements and temporary pasture identification in advance.

Follow a seasonal maintenance schedule to keep your Texas emergency kit ready year-round. In January and February, review winter storm supplies and check pipe insulation. March through May is tornado season preparation time, so test your NOAA weather radio, restock first aid supplies, and practice your shelter drills. June through November is hurricane season, so keep your go-bags packed, your car fueled, and your evacuation routes mapped. July and August demand extra water storage and heat safety supplies. Frequently Asked Questions

What disasters does Texas face most frequently?
Texas faces hurricanes along the Gulf Coast from Corpus Christi to Beaumont, tornadoes across North and Central Texas in Tornado Alley, extreme heat with summer temperatures regularly exceeding 100 degrees, flash flooding in the Hill Country and urban areas, severe thunderstorms with hail, and winter storms such as the devastating 2021 freeze that caused widespread power grid failure.
How should I prepare my Texas home for hurricane season?
Gulf Coast residents should install hurricane shutters or pre-cut plywood for windows, reinforce garage doors, trim trees near structures, and elevate critical utilities if in a flood zone. Stock at least seven days of water and food, fill prescriptions, and have a full tank of gas before a storm watches. Know your evacuation route along I-10 or I-45 and identify shelters in advance.
What should I do to prepare for tornadoes in Texas?
Identify the safest room in your home, typically an interior room on the lowest floor away from windows. Install a tornado safe room or reinforced shelter if possible. Keep a NOAA weather radio with battery backup at all times during spring storm season. Wear a helmet or heavy blankets during a tornado to protect against flying debris. Practice your family tornado drill at least twice per year.
How do I prepare for Texas extreme heat emergencies?
Store at least two gallons of water per person per day and add electrolyte packets. Identify cooling centers near your home through your city or county emergency management office. Keep a battery-powered fan, cooling towels, and lightweight clothing in your emergency kit. Never leave children or pets in parked vehicles. Heat-related illness can develop rapidly in Texas summers, so recognize symptoms of heat exhaustion and heat stroke.
Should I prepare for winter storms in Texas?
Yes. The February 2021 winter storm proved that Texas is vulnerable to extreme cold events that can disable the power grid for days. Stock extra blankets, sleeping bags rated to zero degrees, a backup heating source such as a vented kerosene heater, extra water pipes insulation, and at least seven days of food. Fill bathtubs with water before a freeze for flushing toilets.
How do I prepare for flash flooding in the Texas Hill Country?
Never drive through flooded roadways. Turn around, do not drown. Know the flood-prone areas near your home and avoid camping or parking along streams and creeks during threatening weather. Install a NOAA weather radio and sign up for county flood alerts. Keep a go-bag in your car with at least one day of supplies and move to higher ground immediately if flash flood warnings are issued for your area.
What emergency resources does the Texas Division of Emergency Management provide?
The Texas Division of Emergency Management (TDEM) coordinates state disaster response through the Texas Emergency Management Council. TDEM operates the Texas Emergency Operations Center, manages evacuation routes, coordinates sheltering through the American Red Cross, and distributes emergency alerts through the Texas Wireless Emergency Alert system. Visit tdem.texas.gov for county-specific hazard information and preparedness resources.
