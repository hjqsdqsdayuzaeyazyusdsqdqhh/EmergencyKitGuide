Kitchen Fire Safety: Prevent Fires and Know What to Do

Kitchen fires are the leading cause of home fires. Learn how to prevent cooking fires, respond to grease and oven fires, and keep your family safe with proven fire safety practices.

Last updated: February 20, 2025By: EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

More home fires start in the kitchen than anywhere else in the house. Most are cooking fires, and most of those are preventable with the right habits. This guide covers the most common causes of kitchen fires, how to respond when a greasy pan flames up or an oven catches, which fire extinguisher belongs in your kitchen, and the simple routines that stop fires before they start.

Why Kitchen Fires Are So Common

Kitchens combine heat, flammable oils, electrical appliances, and busy schedules, which makes them the most likely room in the home to catch fire. Cooking equipment is involved in nearly half of all reported home fires and is the leading cause of home fire injuries. The single biggest contributor is unattended cooking: a pot left on a burner while someone answers the phone, checks the laundry, or starts another task.

A fire builds fast. Cooking oil can reach its ignition point and burst into flame in seconds once it gets too hot, and grease fires spread quickly across a stovetop. Because so many kitchen fires start small, a person who is present and knows what to do can often contain them with a lid or a fire extinguisher before they grow. That presence is the difference between a close call and a house fire.

The Most Common Causes of Cooking Fires

Understanding what starts a cooking fire is the first step to preventing one. The most common causes are:

  • Unattended cooking, especially with oil on high heat.
  • Grease igniting on a stove, in a pan, or inside a broiler or grill.
  • Ovens and toaster ovens left on with food or crumbs inside.
  • Microwaves overheating, arcing, or igniting packaging.
  • Loose clothing, towels, oven mitts, or wooden utensils too close to the burner.
  • Electrical shorts from overloaded outlets or frayed appliance cords.

About half of all cooking fires happen because someone left the kitchen while something was cooking. Stay near the stove whenever anything is on the heat, set a timer as a reminder, and keep anything flammable — towels, curtains, paper towels, packaging — at least three feet away from the burners.

How to Respond to a Grease Fire

A grease fire happens when cooking oil reaches its flash point and ignites. It is dangerous because the flames spread across the surface of the oil, and pouring water on it makes things dramatically worse: the water sinks under the oil, vaporizes instantly, and blasts burning oil across the room.

If a grease fire starts in a pan on your stovetop, do this:

  • Turn off the heat immediately.
  • Cover the pan with a metal lid or a metal baking sheet. Do not use glass, which can shatter.
  • Leave the lid on — do not lift it to peek. The fire dies when it runs out of oxygen.
  • If a lid is not within reach, pour a large amount of baking soda over the flames. Never use flour or baking powder.
  • Use a fire extinguisher rated for cooking fires if the fire is too large for a lid.
  • If the fire is on a deep fryer or the pan cannot be covered safely, get everyone out and call the fire department.

Never move a burning pan. Carry it only if the fire is small and you have a clear, unobstructed path to a heat-safe surface, and even then only as a last resort. And never throw water on any cooking fire.

What to Do About Oven and Microwave Fires

Oven fires usually start from drippings or food stuck to the bottom, or from built-up grease that ignites when the oven is heated. If a fire breaks out inside your oven, keep the oven door closed, turn the oven off, and leave the door shut. Cutting off the oxygen will starve the fire. Once the fire is fully out and the oven has cooled, clean the interior and clear out any grease or debris before using it again.

Microwave fires are typically caused by metal objects, arcing, or overheating food. If something catches fire in the microwave, press stop, unplug the appliance if you can reach the outlet safely, and keep the door closed to contain the flames. Do not open the door until the fire is clearly out. If the fire continues or spreads, evacuate and call the fire department.

Toaster ovens follow the same rule: unplug them if it is safe, keep the window or door closed to cut the airflow, and let the fire suffocate. Keep toaster ovens away from the wall and cleared of crumbs, which are a common ignition source.

Fire Extinguishers: Types and How to Use Them

Every kitchen should have an accessible fire extinguisher, and it should be the right type. Fire extinguishers are labeled by the class of fire they handle: Class K ratings cover cooking oils and grease; Class B covers flammable liquids; Class ABC covers ordinary combustibles, flammable liquids, and electrical fires, which makes them the practical choice for a home kitchen.

Position the extinguisher away from the stove, where you can reach it even if a fire blocks the burners — near an exit or on a wall close to a doorway is ideal. Mount it where adults can grab it quickly but where small children cannot reach it freely.

Before you ever need it, learn the P.A.S.S. method:

  • Pull the pin at the top of the extinguisher.
  • Aim the nozzle at the base of the fire, not at the flames.
  • Squeeze the handle to release the agent.
  • Sweep from side to side until the fire is out.

Check the pressure gauge monthly and replace the extinguisher when the gauge reads low or after any use, even a partial one.

Daily Habits That Prevent Kitchen Fires

Prevention in the kitchen comes down to a few repeatable habits that take seconds but eliminate most fire risk:

  • Stay in the kitchen while frying, grilling, or broiling. Set a timer before you start.
  • Keep a three-foot "kid-free and clutter-free" zone around the stove.
  • Turn pan handles toward the back of the stove so they cannot be knocked or reached by children.
  • Keep oven mitts, towels, and wooden utensils away from the burners while cooking.
  • Clean grease from the stove, oven, and range hood regularly — built-up grease feeds a fire once it starts.
  • Unplug countertop appliances after use, and roll cords away from the counter edge.
  • Know your limits: if the pan of oil is too hot before you have changed your habits, practice the lid-over-pan response so it becomes automatic.

Deep-frying is the highest-risk cooking method in the home, and it demands the same rules with even more discipline: use a deep fryer on a level surface, fill it no more than two-thirds full, keep it away from walls and cabinets, and never leave it unattended.

Kitchen Fire Safety with Kids at Home

Children are frequent victims in kitchen fires, often because a child is present when a burn or fire occurs. Set firm rules: children stay at least three feet from the stove, never play with the knobs, and do not open hot ovens or microwaves. Use tamper-resistant knob covers if a curious toddler can reach the burners.

Practice fire safety as a family, not just as a rule. Teach children what the smoke alarm sounds like, that the family meets outside at a designated spot, and that they get out and stay out during any fire. Turn kitchen fire prevention into the same family drill you use for your home fire escape plan, so the response is automatic for everyone.

Smoke Alarms and Escape Routes

Your kitchen fire safety plan only works if your smoke alarms work. Install alarms on every level of the home and in every bedroom, test them monthly, and replace the batteries at least once a year. Keep alarms at least ten feet from the stove so cooking heat and steam do not trigger nuisance alarms that lead people to disable them.

Work smoke alarms into a planned escape: two ways out of every room, a meeting spot outside the home, and a rule that no one returns inside once they are out. For the full process of mapping exits and practicing the drill, follow a fire evacuation plan for your household, and check your smoke alarm setup and maintenance at least twice a year alongside it.

Electrical Safety in the Kitchen

Electrical fires in the kitchen usually come from overloaded outlets, damaged cords, or faulty appliances. Plug high-wattage appliances — toasters, air fryers, coffeemakers, mixers — into wall outlets rather than extension cords, and avoid running multiple high-power appliances on the same outlet or circuit. Replace any cord that is frayed, pinched, or warm to the touch, and never run cords under rugs or across counters where they can be cut or heated by the stove.

If an appliance sparks, smells like burning, or trips a breaker repeatedly, stop using it and have it serviced or replaced. Keep outlets behind the counter away from the sink and any standing water, and ensure ground fault circuit interrupters are installed where required, especially near water.

What to Do When a Kitchen Fire Starts

Your response depends on the size of the fire, but the decision rule is simple: if it is small and you can contain it safely, act immediately; if it is growing, spreading, or blocking your exit, get out and call the fire department.

  • Small, contained fire: turn off the heat, cover the pan with a metal lid or bake the fire out by closing the oven door, or use the right extinguisher.
  • Fire you cannot contain: close the door on the way out to slow the spread, leave the house, meet at your family meeting spot, and call 911 from outside.
  • Never use water on a grease or electrical fire.
  • Never re-enter the home once you are outside, no matter what was left behind.

If your clothes catch fire, remember stop, drop, and roll. Covering the person with a rug or jacket can help smother the flames. Once a fire is out, do not assume the danger is over: have the affected appliance and any smoke damage checked before you use the kitchen again.

A Kitchen Fire Safety Checklist

Use this checklist to confirm your kitchen and family are ready before the next cooking session:

  • Stay in the kitchen or set a timer during all frying, grilling, and broiling.
  • Keep a three-foot clear zone around the stove, free of children, towels, and clutter.
  • Store a metal lid and a box of baking soda within arm's reach of the cooktop.
  • Keep an ABC fire extinguisher mounted near the kitchen exit, checked monthly.
  • Practicing the P.A.S.S. method so you know it before you need it.
  • Test smoke alarms monthly and replace batteries twice a year.
  • Clean grease from the stove, range hood, oven, and toaster oven regularly.
  • Replace frayed cords and avoid overloaded outlets and extension cords.
  • Review the family escape plan and meeting spot with everyone in the household.
  • Teach children to stay clear of the stove and to get out and stay out if the alarm sounds.

Beyond the kitchen, learn what to do for fires elsewhere in the home with our home fire prevention tips, review your smoke alarm maintenance schedule, and make sure the whole household knows the fire escape plan and has a home evacuation checklist ready.
