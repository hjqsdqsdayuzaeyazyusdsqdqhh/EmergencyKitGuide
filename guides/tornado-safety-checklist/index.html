Tornado Safety Checklist: Before, During, and After a Tornado

Complete tornado safety checklist for storm season. Learn what to do before, during, and after a tornado to protect your family and home.

Last updated: July 25, 2025By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Understanding Tornado Watch Versus Tornado Warning

The first item on any tornado safety checklist is knowing the difference between a tornado watch and a tornado warning. A tornado watch is issued by the Storm Prediction Center when atmospheric conditions are favorable for tornado development across a broad region, typically covering multiple counties or even several states. Watches may remain in effect for four to eight hours. During a watch, you should review your family tornado plan, check that your shelter kit is stocked and accessible, and monitor weather updates closely. It is a time for preparation, not panic.

A tornado warning is far more urgent. It is issued by your local National Weather Service office when a tornado has been spotted on the ground or detected by Doppler radar. A warning covers a specific polygon-shaped area and typically lasts 30 to 45 minutes. When a warning is issued for your location, you have minutes, not hours, to reach safety. Every household member must know that a warning means immediate action: move to your designated safe room, basement, or interior hallway right now. Preparing Your Home Before Tornado Season

Before tornado season begins, walk around your property and identify every object that could become a projectile in high winds. Patio furniture, grills, trampolines, garden tools, potted plants, and decorative items are all dangerous when hurled by tornado-force winds. Create a plan for securing or bringing these items indoors within minutes of hearing a tornado watch. Consider installing permanent anchors or hooks on your deck and patio so you can quickly tie down furniture with bungee cords or straps. Trim dead or weak branches from trees near your home and remove any dead trees that could fall on your house.

Reinforce your home's weakest points. Garage doors are a common failure point during tornadoes; when a garage door buckles, wind enters the structure and can blow the roof off. Install garage door bracing kits, which are affordable and available at most home improvement stores. Reinforce entry doors with deadbolts and strike plates. Consider installing storm shutters or pre-cutting 5/8-inch plywood panels to fit every window. Identify your safe room: the smallest interior room on the lowest floor with no windows, ideally a bathroom, closet, or hallway beneath a staircase. Identifying and Equipping Your Safe Room

Your safe room is the single most important element of tornado safety. The ideal safe room is an underground basement or storm cellar. If your home has a basement, choose the corner farthest from any windows or exterior doors, ideally beneath a staircase. If you have no basement, move to the lowest floor and seek out the smallest interior room with the most walls between you and the outside. Bathrooms are often recommended because plumbing in the walls adds structural reinforcement and the room is typically small and interior.

Equip your safe room with essential supplies pre-positioned for immediate use. Store helmets for every family member on a shelf or hook within arm's reach. Keep a battery-powered NOAA weather radio, flashlights with spare batteries, a first aid kit, sturdy shoes, and a whistle in the room. A small waterproof container holding copies of important documents, cash, and a list of emergency contacts should also be there. For families willing to invest further, FEMA offers cost-share grants through the Hazard Mitigation Grant Program to help fund construction of FEMA-compliant safe rooms. What to Do During a Tornado Warning

When a tornado warning is issued for your area, execute your plan immediately. Move your entire family to the designated safe room or shelter location. If you are in a mobile home, leave it immediately and seek shelter in a sturdy building or community storm shelter. Do not waste time covering windows or trying to save property. Every second counts. Once in the safe room, get low and cover your head. The Drop, Cover, and Hold On position is recommended: kneel on the floor, bend over, and protect your head and neck with your arms. If possible, get under a sturdy piece of furniture such as a heavy table.

If you are in a vehicle, do not try to outrun a tornado. Pull over, keep your seatbelt on, put your head below the windows, and cover yourself with a blanket or jacket. Vehicles offer minimal protection and should only be used as a last resort if no building is reachable. Stay in the shelter until the National Weather Service lifts the warning for your area or until you hear an all-clear from your NOAA weather radio. Monitor your phone for text alerts if cell service is available. For a complete review of what every household needs, consult our family 72-hour emergency kit guide.

Mobile Home Tornado Safety Protocols

Mobile homes, manufactured homes, and trailer homes offer virtually no protection from tornadoes. Even a weak EF0 tornado with winds of 65 to 85 miles per hour can flip or destroy a mobile home. This is not a matter of opinion; it is a well-documented fact supported by decades of National Weather Service research and post-storm damage surveys. If you live in a mobile home, your tornado safety plan must begin with knowing exactly where the nearest sturdy building or community storm shelter is located.

Pre-identify at least two shelter locations within a short walking or driving distance. Contact your mobile home park management to ask whether a community shelter exists. Many parks in tornado-prone states have invested in community storm shelters for their residents. Practice getting to these shelters from your home so the route is muscle memory. If no shelter is available and a tornado is imminent, lie flat in the nearest ditch or low-lying area and cover your head with your arms. This is genuinely a last resort, but it is far safer than remaining inside a mobile home. Our disaster preparedness plan includes mobile home specific guidance.

School Tornado Procedures and Child Safety

Schools in tornado-prone regions must have a comprehensive, practiced tornado safety plan. During a tornado warning, students and staff move to interior hallways on the lowest floor of the building, away from all windows, gymnasiums, auditoriums, and cafeterias. Wide-span roof structures like gyms and auditoriums are extremely vulnerable to collapse during tornadoes and must never be used as shelter locations. Students should sit facing the hallway walls with their heads down and hands clasped behind their necks for protection.

Schools should conduct tornado drills at least twice per year, ideally once at the start of the school year and once during peak tornado season. Teachers should be trained in their specific roles, including accountability for every student on their roster. Parents should discuss the school's tornado plan with their children and reassure them that the school practices safety regularly. Ask your school district about their tornado preparedness plan and whether they have a designated FEMA-compliant safe room. If your school does not, advocate for one through your PTA or school board. After the Tornado: Immediate Safety Steps

Once the tornado has passed, your immediate priority is assessing safety before taking any other action. Do not exit the shelter until you are confident the danger has passed, as tornadoes can sometimes be followed by additional tornadoes in the same storm system. When you do emerge, watch for downed power lines, broken glass, exposed nails, and structural damage. Assume every downed power line is live and stay at least 35 feet away from it. If you smell natural gas, leave the building immediately and call 911 from a safe distance.

Administer first aid to anyone who needs it and call 911 for serious injuries. Use the trauma first aid supplies from your tornado shelter kit to control bleeding and stabilize injuries until paramedics arrive. Turn off your home's main water, gas, and electrical switches if you suspect damage to any utility lines. Photograph all property damage thoroughly before beginning any cleanup, as these images will be essential for insurance claims. Contact your insurance company as soon as possible. Do not enter any building that appears structurally compromised until it has been inspected by a qualified professional. Building a Complete Tornado Emergency Kit

A tornado emergency kit is distinct from other emergency kits because it is designed for shelter-in-place rather than evacuation. Store it in or immediately adjacent to your designated safe room so you can access it in seconds. The core items include helmets for every family member, a battery-powered NOAA weather radio, flashlights with extra batteries, a first aid kit stocked for trauma injuries, N95 dust masks, rescue whistles, sturdy closed-toe shoes, water for three days, non-perishable food, and a waterproof document bag with identification and insurance copies.

Beyond the basics, add items for comfort and practicality. Mylar emergency blankets provide warmth on cold basement floors. Compact sleeping pads cushion hard surfaces. Include a whistle for each family member in case of entrapment. Pack a hand-crank or battery-powered phone charger so you can communicate after power is out. For children, include comfort items like a small stuffed animal, coloring supplies, and noise-reducing headphones. Store cash in small bills and keep a printed list of emergency contacts. Rotate food, water, and battery supplies every six months. Review our tornado emergency kit guide for a complete item-by-item breakdown of every supply you need.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a tornado watch and a tornado warning?
A tornado watch means conditions are favorable for tornadoes in your area and lasts several hours over a large region. During a watch, review your safety plan and prepare your shelter. A tornado warning means a tornado has been sighted or detected by radar and covers a specific area for 30 minutes or more. During a warning, take shelter immediately in the lowest interior room or basement.
Where is the safest place to take shelter during a tornado?
The safest location is an underground storm cellar or basement. If none is available, go to the lowest floor of a sturdy building and move to a small interior room with no windows such as a bathroom, closet, or hallway. Put as many walls as possible between you and the outside. Avoid mobile homes entirely as they offer no tornado protection.
Are mobile homes safe during tornadoes?
No. Mobile homes provide essentially zero protection from tornadoes even weak ones rated EF0 or EF1. Research shows they are flipped or destroyed by winds as low as 60 to 70 miles per hour. If you live in a mobile home evacuate to a sturdy building or community storm shelter at the first tornado warning.
What should I do immediately after a tornado passes?
After the tornado passes watch for downed power lines broken glass gas leaks and unstable structures. Do not enter damaged buildings until inspected. Turn off utilities at the main switches if you smell gas. Administer first aid and call 911 for serious injuries. Photograph property damage for insurance before cleaning up.
What should schools do during a tornado?
Schools should have a written tornado safety plan with regular drills. During a warning students move to interior hallways on the lowest floor away from windows and crouch facing the wall with heads covered. Gymnasiums auditoriums and cafeterias with wide-span roofs are dangerous and should be avoided.
How do I secure my home before tornado season?
Identify and prepare your safe room. Secure or bring indoors outdoor furniture grills trampolines and loose objects. Trim dead branches from trees. Prepare plywood panels for windows. Reinforce garage doors. Check your insurance covers tornado damage. Assemble a tornado shelter kit with helmets water first aid a weather radio and flashlights.
