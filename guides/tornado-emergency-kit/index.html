Tornado Emergency Kit: Storm Shelter Supplies and Go-Bags

Build a tornado emergency kit designed for storm shelters and safe rooms. Includes protective gear, communication tools, and shelter-in-place essentials.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Tornado Kits Are Different from Other Emergency Kits

A tornado emergency kit is fundamentally different from a hurricane evacuation bag or a wildfire go-bag. Tornadoes give families almost no lead time. While a hurricane may be days away when a watch is issued, a tornado warning can arrive with as little as five to ten minutes before impact. That means you cannot evacuate. You must shelter in place, and the supplies you need must already be stored in your shelter location. Your tornado kit is not a bag you pack when danger approaches; it is a pre-positioned cache of supplies sitting in your basement, safe room, or interior closet right now, waiting for the day you need it.

The contents of a tornado shelter kit also differ because the primary threats are flying debris, structural collapse, and power loss rather than flooding or wildfire smoke. Head injuries are the leading cause of tornado fatalities, which means helmet protection is not optional. Dust and pulverized building materials fill the air after a tornado strikes, making dust masks essential. Concrete basement floors and shelter floors are cold and uncomfortable, so sleeping pads and blankets matter more than you might expect. This guide covers every item you need, organized by category, so you can build a tornado shelter kit that is comprehensive, organized, and ready at a moment's notice.

Head Protection: Helmets and Hard Hats

Head injuries account for approximately 70 percent of tornado fatalities according to research from the National Weather Service and Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation. A helmet is the single most important life-saving item in your tornado shelter kit. Store one helmet for every member of your household in or immediately adjacent to your tornado shelter. Motorcycle helmets offer the best protection because they are designed to withstand high-impact collisions at speed. Full-face motorcycle helmets provide the added benefit of protecting your face and jaw from debris.

If motorcycle helmets are not available, bicycle helmets, batting helmets, ski helmets, and construction hard hats all provide meaningful protection. A hard hat with an integrated suspension system is an affordable option that can be stored flat against a wall. For children, ensure the helmet fits properly and does not slide over their eyes. Store helmets on a hook or shelf directly above your shelter area so you can grab them in seconds. Even a bicycle helmet reduces the risk of skull fracture and traumatic brain injury from the kind of low-to-medium velocity debris that causes most tornado deaths. Rescue Whistle and Dust Protection

After a tornado strikes, you may be trapped under debris with no ability to shout for help. A loud rescue whistle attached to every family member's clothing or helmet allows first responders to locate you even if you cannot call out. Choose a pealess whistle design such as the Fox 40 or similar safety whistles, which work when wet and do not have moving parts that can jam. Attach each whistle to a lanyard that can be worn around the neck so it remains accessible even if you are pinned.

After a tornado passes, the air inside damaged structures is often filled with dust, fiberglass particles, pulverized insulation, and mold spores. N95 or P100 respirator masks protect your lungs from these airborne hazards. Pack at least two masks per person in your shelter kit. Unlike cloth face coverings, N95 masks filter out particles as small as 0.3 microns, which is essential when navigating through damaged buildings. Store masks in sealed plastic bags to keep them clean and dry until needed. Combined with safety goggles to protect your eyes from dust and debris, these items form a critical post-tornado safety kit. Communication and Weather Monitoring

A battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio is the backbone of tornado shelter communication. When cell towers are damaged or overloaded and internet service is down, the NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards network continues to broadcast life-saving alerts and updates from the National Weather Service. Choose a radio with Specific Area Message Encoding (SAME) technology that allows you to program it to alert only for your county, reducing false alarm fatigue. Store at least two sets of spare batteries in your shelter kit and test the radio monthly during tornado season.

In addition to your weather radio, keep two fully charged portable power banks in your tornado shelter kit along with charging cables compatible with your family's phones. A hand-crank emergency radio with a built-in USB charging port serves double duty as both a communication device and a power source. Two-way radios provide short-range communication between family members if you become separated in your home after the tornado. Program them to the same channel before storm season begins. Keep a printed list of emergency contact numbers, including your out-of-state contact person, in each family member's go-bag. First Aid for Flying Debris Injuries

Tornado injuries differ from those in other disasters because they typically involve blunt force trauma from flying debris, lacerations from broken glass and metal, and puncture wounds from nails and screws. Your tornado shelter first aid kit should be stocked specifically for these injury types. Include trauma-rated Israeli bandages or compression wraps, QuikClot or Celox hemostatic gauze for controlling severe bleeding, adhesive bandages in multiple sizes, medical tape, butterfly closure strips for lacerations, antiseptic wipes, and a tourniquet with instructions on proper use.

Beyond wound care, include over-the-counter pain relievers such as ibuprofen and acetaminophen, antihistamines for allergic reactions to dust and mold, anti-diarrheal medication, and a basic first aid manual. Store prescription medications for each family member in your shelter kit, ideally a seven-day supply in addition to what you keep in your regular medication storage. Write down all medication names, dosages, and prescribing physician contact information on an index card. Include disposable gloves, trauma shears for cutting clothing away from wounds, and a flashlight for examining injuries when the power is out. Water and Food for Shelter-in-Place

After a tornado passes, municipal water supplies may be contaminated or cut off entirely. Store at least one gallon of water per person per day for a minimum of three days in your tornado shelter kit. Use commercially sealed bottled water or water stored in food-grade containers with tight-fitting lids. Keep the containers in a cool, dark corner of your basement or shelter. For a family of four, that means a minimum of 12 gallons, which can be stored in cases of bottled water or in stackable water bricks.

For food, choose items that require no preparation and no refrigeration. Energy bars, granola bars, peanut butter crackers, dried fruit, beef jerky, and canned meals with pull-tab lids are excellent choices. Pack a manual can opener even if you choose pull-tab cans, as you may need it for other supplies. Include disposable utensils, paper plates, and wet wipes for hygiene. Rotate your food supply every six to twelve months to maintain freshness. Compact water purification tablets or a small portable filter serve as a backup if your stored water runs low. Our 72-hour water storage guide provides detailed recommendations for storage containers and purification methods.

Shelter Comfort: Sleeping Pads, Blankets, and Sturdy Shoes

Concrete basement floors and storm shelter surfaces are cold, hard, and unforgiving, especially if your family spends several hours sheltering during a long storm system. Compact sleeping pads or folding foam mats provide insulation from the cold floor and cushion your body. Mylar emergency space blankets are lightweight, compact, and retain up to 90 percent of body heat. Pack at least two per person. A larger fleece or wool blanket provides additional comfort and warmth, especially for children and elderly family members.

Sturdy closed-toe shoes are an often-overlooked but critical item. If a tornado damages your home, the floor will likely be covered in broken glass, nails, splintered wood, and metal fragments. Every family member should have a pair of sturdy shoes stored in or near the shelter. Old sneakers or work boots work perfectly. Pair them with thick socks and long pants to protect your legs and feet when you emerge from the shelter. Additionally, pack a waterproof document bag containing photocopies of identification, insurance policies, birth certificates, and bank information. Include cash in small bills since ATMs and card readers will be offline. For more on document protection strategies, consult our emergency supplies list.

Phone Charging and Power Backup

Your smartphone is likely your primary communication device during and after a tornado. Power outages can last days or even weeks after a major tornado, making portable power essential. Store at least two high-capacity power banks, each with a minimum of 20,000mAh, in your tornado shelter kit. These can charge a standard smartphone three to four times each. Keep them fully charged and rotate them every three months by using and recharging them. Pack the appropriate charging cables for every device your family owns.

A hand-crank emergency radio with a built-in USB port provides unlimited charging capability as long as you have the strength to crank it. Solar-powered chargers are another option, though they require sunlight and time to generate meaningful power. Keep a small battery-powered LED lantern in the shelter as well, since flashlights require one hand to hold. The lantern illuminates the entire shelter area so your family can navigate safely and tend to any injuries. Comfort Items for Children in the Shelter

Tornadoes are terrifying for children. The roar of the wind, the shaking of the house, and the sudden loss of power create an overwhelming sensory experience. Packing comfort items specifically for children can dramatically reduce their anxiety and help them remain calm in the shelter. Include a favorite stuffed animal or small blanket that your child associates with comfort and safety. A small, child-friendly LED lantern or glow stick gives them something to hold and provides a sense of control over their environment.

Coloring books with crayons, card games, a tablet pre-loaded with movies or games, and age-appropriate books provide distraction during extended sheltering. Noise-canceling headphones or earplugs can protect sensitive ears from the overwhelming sound of wind and debris. Write your family reunification plan and emergency contact numbers on an index card and place one in each child's pocket. Practice tornado drills with your children at least twice a year so they know exactly where to go and what to do without hesitation. Teach them the Drop, Cover, and Hold On position and make it a familiar routine rather than a frightening surprise. For more kid-specific preparedness guidance, see our 72-hour kit checklist.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do helmets really protect against tornado debris?
Yes, helmets significantly reduce the risk of head injury from flying debris during a tornado. Studies from the National Weather Service and storm shelter researchers show that head injuries are the leading cause of tornado fatalities, and helmets provide critical protection. Motorcycle helmets, bicycle helmets, batting helmets, or even a sturdy construction hard hat can reduce the severity of injuries from debris impacts. Every member of your household should have a helmet stored in your tornado shelter kit.
How much water should I keep in my tornado shelter kit?
Store at least one gallon of water per person per day for a minimum of three days. For a family of four, this means 12 gallons stored in your shelter area. Use commercially sealed bottled water or water stored in food-grade containers. Rotate the supply every six months to maintain freshness. Consider compact water purification tablets as a backup in case your stored supply runs low or becomes contaminated by debris.
Should I evacuate or shelter in place during a tornado?
Shelter in place. Unlike hurricanes, tornadoes develop rapidly and give very little warning time, sometimes only minutes. Attempting to drive away from a tornado is extremely dangerous because tornadoes can change direction unpredictably and move faster than most vehicles. The safest action is to move immediately to the lowest interior room of your home, a storm shelter, or a basement. Only evacuate if you are in a mobile home, which provides no tornado protection.
What should people in mobile homes do during a tornado?
Mobile homes offer virtually no protection from tornadoes, even in weak ones. If you live in a mobile home and a tornado warning is issued, evacuate immediately to the nearest sturdy building, community storm shelter, or designated safe location. Do not wait to see the tornado. Pre-identify at least two nearby shelter locations and practice getting to them quickly. If no shelter is available, lie flat in the nearest ditch or low-lying area and cover your head.
What comfort items should I include for children in a tornado shelter?
Pack small comfort items that reduce stress and fear for children during a tornado event. Include a favorite stuffed animal or blanket, a small battery-powered LED lantern they can hold, coloring books with crayons, a tablet or phone pre-loaded with games and movies, earplugs or noise-canceling headphones to muffle the roar of wind, and a few favorite snacks. Write your family reunification plan and emergency contact numbers on an index card and place it in each child's pocket.
How often should I update my tornado shelter kit?
Review and update your tornado shelter kit at least twice per year, ideally at the start and end of tornado season in your region. Check expiration dates on food and water, replace batteries in flashlights and radios, update prescription medications, and refresh clothing sizes for growing children. Test your weather radio monthly during tornado season to confirm it receives alerts from the National Weather Service.
