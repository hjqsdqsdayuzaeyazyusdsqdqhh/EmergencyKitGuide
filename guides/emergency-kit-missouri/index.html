Emergency Kit Missouri: Tornadoes, Severe Storms, and River Flooding Preparedness

Missouri emergency kit guide covering tornadoes, severe thunderstorms, flooding along the Mississippi and Missouri rivers, ice storms, and extreme heat.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Missouri's Multi-Hazard Threat Profile

Missouri occupies a volatile position in the nation's weather landscape. The state sits squarely in Tornado Alley, where warm, moist air from the Gulf of Mexico collides with cold, dry air from Canada and dry air from the desert Southwest. This convergence produces some of the most violent tornadoes and severe thunderstorms on Earth. The historic Joplin tornado of 2011 killed 158 people and remains one of the deadliest tornado events in U.S. history. Beyond tornadoes, Missouri faces severe thunderstorms with large hail, damaging straight-line winds, catastrophic river flooding along the Mississippi and Missouri rivers, and winter ice storms that can paralyze entire regions for days.

The Missouri State Emergency Management Agency (SEMA) coordinates disaster response across the state's 114 counties and the independent city of St. Louis. However, individual and family preparedness remains the foundation of survival. Missouri's geography creates dramatically different risk profiles between urban and rural areas. The Kansas City and St. Louis metropolitan areas face unique urban flooding and infrastructure challenges, while rural communities in the Ozarks may be isolated for days without access to emergency services. This guide addresses the full spectrum of Missouri-specific hazards and provides practical supply recommendations for every region.

Tornado Preparedness: Your Most Urgent Priority

Missouri averages between 40 and 50 tornadoes per year, with the peak season running from March through June, though tornadoes can occur in any month. The state has been struck by violent EF4 and EF5 tornadoes repeatedly throughout recorded history. The average tornado warning lead time is approximately 13 minutes, but some tornadoes develop with little or no warning at all. This reality demands that every Missouri household have a tornado safety plan that can be executed within minutes, including at night when family members are sleeping.

Identify the safest room in your home, which is always on the lowest floor, in the interior, and away from windows. A basement is ideal, but if you do not have one, an interior closet, bathroom, or hallway works. Install a bicycle helmet or climbing helmet in your shelter area for each family member to protect against flying debris, which causes the majority of tornado injuries. Keep a tornado emergency kit in your shelter location containing a NOAA weather radio with battery backup, flashlights, sturdy shoes for every family member, a first aid kit, sturdy work gloves, and your grab-and-go bag. Mobile home residents must evacuate to a sturdy building before severe weather arrives, as mobile homes offer zero tornado protection regardless of tie-down systems. Severe Thunderstorms, Hail, and Straight-Line Winds

While tornadoes receive the most attention, severe thunderstorms cause more cumulative damage in Missouri than tornadoes in most years. Straight-line winds from derechos and severe thunderstorm complexes can exceed 100 miles per hour, equivalent to a weak tornado but affecting a much wider area. Large hail, sometimes exceeding baseball size, can shatter windows, destroy roofing, and injure anyone caught outside. These storms can develop rapidly during Missouri's spring and summer months, often with only minutes of warning.

Your emergency kit should include window coverings or emergency shutters to protect against hail damage, and a basic home repair kit with tarps, nails, and tools for temporary roof patching. Keep a cordless phone charged and available in your shelter area, as cordless phones that require base station power will fail during outages. After any severe thunderstorm, inspect your home for damage carefully before entering, watch for downed power lines, and avoid walking through debris that may conceal hazards. River Flooding Along the Mississippi and Missouri Rivers

Missouri sits at the convergence of two of North America's greatest rivers, and their combined flood risk defines the state's geography. The Great Flood of 1993 inundated thousands of square miles across Missouri and Illinois, destroying entire towns and displacing tens of thousands of residents. The Mississippi River flood of 2019 demonstrated that these threats remain acute. Communities along both rivers, as well as tributaries like the Osage, Gasconade, and Meramec rivers, face seasonal flooding that can develop slowly or with shocking speed depending on upstream conditions.

If you live in a flood-prone area, maintain a go-bag ready during spring flood season that includes waterproof boots, waterproof bags for documents and electronics, and a life jacket if you are in an area where rapid water rise is possible. Know your property's flood zone designation and understand the difference between a flood watch and a flood warning. Never drive through flooded roadways. Missouri experiences drowning deaths every year from people attempting to cross flooded roads. The saying "turn around, don't drown" exists because of these preventable tragedies. Keep your vehicle fueled above half-tank during flood season and have alternate routes planned that avoid low-lying roads, underpasses, and areas near river crossings. Winter Ice Storms and Extreme Cold

Missouri's winter weather ranges from mild to brutally severe, and ice storms represent one of the state's most dangerous cold-weather threats. An ice storm can deposit inches of ice on power lines, trees, and roads, causing widespread outages that last days or weeks in rural areas. The ice storm of 2009 left hundreds of thousands of Missourians without power for extended periods in freezing temperatures. Unlike snowstorms, which can be plowed, ice accumulation makes every surface treacherous and paralyzes emergency vehicle response.

Your winter emergency kit should include sleeping bags or blankets rated for zero-degree temperatures or below, chemical hand and body warmers, a battery-powered space heater approved for indoor use with proper ventilation provisions, and at least seven days of non-perishable food that does not require cooking. Stock extra water, as pipes may freeze and break, cutting off your water supply. Keep vehicles above half-tank during winter months and store an ice scraper, sand or cat litter for traction, and a small shovel in your car. If you rely on propane or heating oil, fill your tank before winter weather arrives. Rural Missouri residents who depend on well water face a double threat during ice storms: no electricity means no water pump, and frozen pipes can prevent well operation even when power returns. Extreme Summer Heat and Humidity

Missouri summers bring temperatures routinely exceeding 100 degrees Fahrenheit with high humidity, particularly in the southeastern lowlands and the Missouri and Mississippi river valleys. The combination of heat and humidity creates heat index values that can exceed 110 degrees, posing serious risks for heat exhaustion and heat stroke. Power outages during summer heat events, often caused by severe thunderstorms knocking out electrical infrastructure, create life-threatening conditions especially for elderly residents, young children, and those with chronic health conditions.

Include cooling supplies in your emergency kit regardless of season, as summer storms can knock out power with no advance notice. Battery-powered fans, cooling towels, and electrolyte replacement packets are inexpensive but potentially life-saving additions. Plan to shelter on the lowest floor of your home during heat events, as heat rises. Identify public cooling centers in your community that may open during extended outages. Never leave children or pets in parked vehicles, and check on elderly neighbors who may lack the ability to cool themselves. Rural Ozarks Preparedness and Well Water Dependence

The Ozarks region of southern Missouri presents unique emergency preparedness challenges that differ significantly from urban areas. Many rural residents depend on private wells for water, which stop functioning when power is lost. Winding mountain roads can become impassable after severe weather, isolating communities for days. Cell phone coverage is spotty in many areas, and emergency response times can stretch to hours rather than minutes. The terrain itself complicates evacuation, as flash flooding can cut off escape routes through valleys before residents realize water levels are rising.

Rural Ozarks residents should build emergency kits for a minimum of 14 days of self-sufficiency. Store at least 28 gallons of water per person for a two-week supply, or include a reliable water purification system. Invest in a backup generator or alternative power source to keep your well pump operational during outages. Maintain a stockpile of non-perishable food, a comprehensive first aid kit, and prescription medications for at least 30 days. Keep a ham radio or CB radio for communication when cell networks fail. Consider joining or forming a neighborhood emergency response group to share resources and coordinate rescue efforts. The community resilience model that the Cajun Navy exemplifies in Louisiana has parallels in Ozarks communities where neighbors routinely assist each other during severe weather events. Kansas City and St. Louis Metro Differences

Missouri's two major metropolitan areas face distinct emergency preparedness challenges despite sharing many of the same statewide hazards. Kansas City sits at the confluence of the Kansas and Missouri rivers and faces significant urban flooding risk, particularly in low-lying areas near the river bottoms. The city's position in the western part of the state places it in the most active tornado corridor, and the flat terrain surrounding the metro provides unobstructed paths for severe storms. St. Louis, positioned directly on the Mississippi River, faces river flooding, urban flash flooding from overwhelmed storm sewer systems during heavy rainfall, and the additional complexity of coordinating emergency response across a bi-state metropolitan area that spans Missouri and Illinois.

Both metros should prioritize tornado shelter planning, as high-rise living creates unique challenges for reaching safe shelter quickly. Apartment and condo residents on upper floors should identify the lowest accessible interior space in their building and plan their route there before severe weather strikes. Keep shoes and a flashlight near your bed for nighttime tornado warnings. Urban residents should also prepare for extended disruption of city services including water pressure loss, sewage backup, and traffic signal failure after severe storms. Sign up for local emergency alert systems through your county or city emergency management office. Our emergency power backup guide is particularly relevant for urban residents who depend on electrical infrastructure for water pressure, elevator access, and communication.

Cave and Karst Terrain Flooding

Southern Missouri's Ozarks region contains extensive cave systems and karst topography, a landscape formed by the dissolution of soluble bedrock such as limestone. While caves attract tourists and outdoor enthusiasts, this terrain creates hidden flooding hazards that few residents consider. Heavy rainfall can cause rapid underground water level rises in caves and sinkholes, potentially flooding basements and foundations through underground connections that are invisible from the surface. Karst terrain also means that contaminants can travel quickly through underground channels, threatening well water supplies far from the source of contamination.

If you own property in karst terrain, have your foundation inspected for potential underground water pathways. Install sump pumps with battery backup in basements and crawl spaces. Test your well water after any significant flooding event, as contamination can travel rapidly through karst systems. Avoid caving during or after heavy rainfall, as underground water levels can rise with deadly speed. Keep waterproof barriers and a bilge pump available for sudden basement flooding. Monitor local geologic survey information for sinkhole activity in your area. These geological hazards are unique to Missouri's Ozarks and require awareness that goes beyond standard flood preparedness.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much warning time do I have before a tornado hits in Missouri?
Average tornado warning lead time in Missouri is approximately 13 minutes, though some tornadoes provide little or no warning at all. The Joplin tornado of 2011 struck with essentially no advance warning due to its rapid development. This means your tornado safety plan must be something your family can execute within minutes at any time of day or night. Keep shoes, a flashlight, and a portable weather radio in your designated shelter area so you can react immediately when an alert sounds.
What is the best shelter during a tornado in Missouri?
The safest shelter is a reinforced safe room or storm cellar specifically designed to withstand tornado-force winds. If you do not have one, go to the lowest floor of your home, to an interior room without windows such as a bathroom, closet, or hallway. Get under a sturdy piece of furniture like a heavy table and protect your head and neck with your arms. Mobile homes offer virtually no tornado protection regardless of tie-down status. If you live in a mobile home, identify a nearby sturdy building where you can take shelter before severe weather season begins.
How do ice storms in Missouri affect emergency preparedness?
Missouri experiences significant ice storms, particularly in the western and central parts of the state, that can bring the entire region to a standstill. Ice accumulation on power lines causes widespread outages that may last a week or longer in rural areas. Roads become impassable, isolating communities from emergency services. Your winter emergency kit should include extra blankets or sleeping bags rated for below-freezing temperatures, a battery-powered heater or chemical hand warmers, non-perishable food that does not require heating, and a week's supply of water. Keep vehicles above half-tank during winter and carry tire chains or traction mats.
Should I prepare differently if I live in rural Ozarks versus a Missouri city?
Yes, rural Ozarks residents face significantly different challenges than Kansas City or St. Louis metro residents. Rural residents often depend on well water that stops flowing during power outages, may be located far from hospitals and emergency services, and face roads that can be impassable for days after severe weather. Build a more extensive kit with at least 14 days of supplies, include water purification capability, stock additional fuel, and consider a backup power source. City residents should focus on evacuation planning, high-rise shelter protocols, and urban flood risks. Both need communication plans that account for cell tower failures.
What supplies do I need specifically for Missouri river flooding?
Missouri's position at the confluence of the Mississippi and Missouri rivers creates significant flooding risk that affects both urban and rural areas. Essential flood-specific supplies include waterproof bags for documents, high-water boots, life jackets if you live in a flood-prone area, a waterproof container for electronics, and rope for emergency situations. Know your property's flood zone designation and have sandbags or flood barriers ready before flood season. Never attempt to walk or drive through floodwater, as even six inches of moving water can knock you down and two feet can float a vehicle. Floodwater in Missouri often carries agricultural runoff and sewage contamination.
How does the Missouri State Emergency Management Agency help residents prepare?
The Missouri State Emergency Management Agency (SEMA) coordinates disaster response across the state and provides resources for individual preparedness. SEMA publishes county-specific hazard assessments, maintains the State Emergency Operations Center, and coordinates with local emergency managers. Residents can sign up for local emergency alerts through their county's emergency management office. SEMA also administers hazard mitigation grants that can fund safe room construction and flood mitigation projects. Visit the SEMA website for Missouri-specific preparedness guides, evacuation route maps, and shelter location information. Register for the Missouri Alert system for real-time emergency notifications.
