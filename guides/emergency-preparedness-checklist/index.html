Emergency Preparedness Checklist: Everything You Need

Why You Need a Complete Emergency Preparedness Checklist

Disasters strike with little to no warning. Whether it is a hurricane bearing down on the coast, a wildfire sweeping through dry brush, a winter storm knocking out power for days, or a sudden earthquake, the families who recover fastest are the ones who prepared in advance. An emergency preparedness checklist removes the guesswork and panic from the equation. Instead of standing in your hallway wondering what to grab, you follow a proven plan that ensures nothing critical gets left behind.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) reports that nearly 60 percent of American households have done little to no emergency planning. That means the majority of families in any given neighborhood are not ready when disaster strikes. First responders cannot reach everyone immediately. In major events, it can take two to five days for organized relief to arrive at your doorstep. During that window, you and your family are entirely self-reliant.

A thorough checklist also reduces stress and decision fatigue. When adrenaline is high and the situation is chaotic, the human brain struggles to make clear choices. A written plan, posted on the refrigerator or saved on your phone, guides you through each step methodically. It tells you exactly what to pack, who to call, and where to go. This guide walks through every category you need to cover, from water and food to documents, communication, and special-needs items for children, elderly family members, and pets.

Water and Hydration Supply

Water is the single most important item on any emergency checklist. The human body can survive only about three days without water, and in hot conditions or during physical exertion, dehydration sets in much faster. FEMA recommends storing at least one gallon of water per person per day. For a family of four aiming for a two-week supply, that means 56 gallons of water. While that number sounds intimidating, you can supplement purchased bottled water with water stored in cleaned two-liter soda bottles and food-grade containers.

Beyond stored water, include water purification methods in your kit. Water purification tablets are lightweight, inexpensive, and have a long shelf life. A portable filter such as a Sawyer Squeeze or LifeStraw lets you drink from rivers and streams if your stored supply runs out. A metal pot or boiling container is essential because boiling remains the most reliable way to kill bacteria, viruses, and parasites.

Rotate stored water every six months. Plastic containers can slowly leach chemicals and develop off-tastes over time. Label each container with the fill date using masking tape. If you live in an apartment or have limited storage space, start with a three-day supply for each household member and gradually build toward two weeks. Even a partial supply is far better than nothing when municipal water lines are compromised after a flood or earthquake.

Food and Nutrition

Non-perishable food is the backbone of any emergency supply. When power is out and grocery stores are closed or stripped bare, your emergency food stash becomes your pantry. Aim for a two-week supply of food that requires minimal or no cooking, because you may not have reliable access to gas, electricity, or even a working stove. For practical guidance on building your reserves, see our detailed emergency food storage guide.

Prioritize calorie-dense, shelf-stable foods: canned meats, canned fruits and vegetables, peanut butter, granola bars, trail mix, dried beans, rice, pasta, and powdered milk. Freeze-dried meals are another excellent option because they are lightweight, have a 25-year shelf life when sealed, and require only hot water to prepare. Include a manual can opener, because electric openers are useless without power.

Do not forget about dietary restrictions and allergies. If anyone in your household is gluten-free, diabetic, or on a specialized diet, stock foods that meet those needs. Baby food and formula are critical if you have an infant. Pack comfort foods like hard candy, instant coffee, or chocolate. In a high-stress situation, small comforts can make a significant psychological difference. Check expiration dates every six months and use the first-in-first-out method so nothing goes to waste.

First Aid and Medical Supplies

A well-stocked first aid kit can mean the difference between a minor injury and a medical emergency. Your kit should go beyond basic bandages and include supplies to handle the most common disaster-related injuries: cuts from broken glass, burns from cooking over alternative heat sources, sprains from navigating debris, and allergic reactions from insect stings or exposure to unfamiliar plants.

Essential first aid items include adhesive bandages in multiple sizes, sterile gauze pads, medical tape, antiseptic wipes, antibiotic ointment, hydrocortisone cream, pain relievers (ibuprofen and acetaminophen), antihistamines, tweezers, scissors, a digital thermometer, nitrile gloves, and an instant cold pack. Add a CPR face shield and a basic first aid manual. The American Red Cross sells compact, well-organized kits that cover most scenarios.

Stock a 30-day supply of any prescription medications every household member takes. Talk to your doctor about obtaining an emergency reserve. Keep a printed list of all medications, dosages, and allergies in a waterproof bag inside your go-bag. Include medical devices such as extra eyeglasses, hearing aid batteries, and glucose monitoring supplies. For a comprehensive list of first aid essentials, check our car first aid kit guide, which covers portable medical supplies that work equally well at home.

Lighting and Tools

Power outages are one of the most common effects of nearly every type of disaster. Without electricity, you lose light, refrigeration, climate control, and communication. A reliable light source is not a luxury; it is a necessity. LED flashlights are the best option because they are bright, durable, and run for dozens of hours on a single set of batteries. Pack at least two flashlights per household member plus several headlamps for hands-free tasks.

Beyond flashlights, include battery-powered or hand-crank lanterns to illuminate an entire room. Chemical light sticks are an inexpensive backup that provide several hours of soft light and are completely safe around children. Always store extra batteries in a cool, dry location and rotate them annually. Rechargeable battery packs that can be solar-charged offer a sustainable long-term solution.

Your tool kit should include a multi-tool, a sturdy knife, duct tape, rope or paracord, a whistle for signaling, work gloves, and a wrench or pliers for shutting off utilities. A battery-powered or hand-crank weather radio keeps you informed when cell towers are down. Consider adding a fire extinguisher rated for multiple fire types. For more detail on essential tools and their uses, see our home emergency kit guide.

Communication Plan

When disaster strikes, your phone is both your most valuable tool and your most fragile lifeline. Cell networks become congested, towers go down, and batteries drain quickly. A family communication plan solves these problems by giving everyone a clear protocol to follow. Start by establishing an out-of-area emergency contact. After a disaster, it is often easier to make a long-distance call than a local one because long-distance lines are less congested.

Write down all emergency phone numbers on a laminated card. Program ICE (In Case of Emergency) contacts into every family member's phone. Designate two meeting places: one near your home for sudden evacuations and one outside your neighborhood for events that make your area inaccessible. Every family member, including children old enough to understand, should know both locations by heart.

Pack a portable solar or hand-crank charger to keep your phone alive when the grid is down. A battery-powered AM/FM/NOAA weather radio provides critical alerts when cell service is unavailable. Consider walkie-talkies for local communication within your family when networks are overloaded. Text messages use less bandwidth than voice calls, so if you can get through, text instead of calling. Practice your communication plan with the whole family at least twice a year.

Documents and Records

In the aftermath of a disaster, you may need to prove your identity, file insurance claims, or access medical records. Important documents are among the hardest things to replace and among the easiest to prepare in advance. Gather originals or certified copies of identification documents (driver's licenses, passports, birth certificates, Social Security cards), insurance policies (home, auto, health, life), property deeds or lease agreements, medical records, vaccination cards, and recent bank statements.

Store all documents in a waterproof, fireproof document bag. Keep a second copy in a cloud-based storage service like Google Drive or Dropbox so you can access them from any device with internet. USB flash drives are another reliable backup option. Include a small amount of cash in small bills. ATMs and credit card machines stop working during power outages, and many emergency supplies can only be purchased with cash.

Photograph each important document with your smartphone and store the images in a secure cloud folder. Keep emergency contact numbers written on paper, not just stored in your phone. Phones break, batteries die, and contacts get lost. A simple paper contact list can be lifesaving. Update your document bag every six months to reflect any changes in insurance, medications, or family status.

Shelter and Warmth

If your home becomes uninhabitable due to structural damage, flooding, or contamination, you need a plan for temporary shelter. FEMA recommends sleeping bags or emergency space blankets for every family member. Emergency space blankets (also called Mylar blankets) are compact, lightweight, and reflect up to 90 percent of body heat. They cost less than two dollars each and take up almost no space in a go-bag.

For more extended shelter needs, pack a lightweight tarp and paracord to construct a basic shelter. If you live in a cold climate, include hand and body warmers, thermal underwear, wool socks, and an extra set of warm clothing per person. Hypothermia is a real risk even in temperatures above freezing, especially when someone is wet or exposed to wind.

Include personal sanitation supplies: toilet paper, garbage bags, hand sanitizer, soap, and a small shovel for digging a latrine if plumbing is out. Feminine hygiene products and baby wipes are important for maintaining cleanliness and morale. Dust masks (N95 if possible) protect your lungs from smoke, ash, mold, and debris. For more details on building a complete shelter-ready kit, see our 72-hour kit guide.

Special Needs and Pets

No emergency checklist is complete unless it accounts for the unique needs of every household member. Infants require formula, baby food, diapers, wipes, a change of clothes, and any prescribed medications. Keep a favorite toy or comfort item in the go-bag; familiar objects reduce anxiety in young children during frightening situations.

Elderly family members may need hearing aid batteries, extra prescription glasses, mobility aids such as a folding cane, and specific medical equipment like a CPAP machine. Arrange for a backup power source or battery pack for any life-sustaining devices. Keep a list of medical conditions and the names and phone numbers of all treating physicians in the emergency document bag.

Pets are family too. Pack a pet emergency kit with at least three days of food and water, a leash and harness, medication, vaccination records, a recent photo for identification, and a comfortable carrier. Microchip your pets and keep registration information current. Many emergency shelters do not allow animals, so identify pet-friendly hotels or boarding facilities along your evacuation route in advance. For more on keeping animals safe, see our home emergency preparedness guide.

Building Your Evacuation Go-Bag

An evacuation go-bag is a pre-packed bag you can grab and leave with in under five minutes. It consolidates the most critical items from every category above into a single, portable package. Choose a durable backpack or duffel bag in a bright color that is easy to spot in low light. Each adult should carry their own go-bag, and the family should have at least one shared bag with communal supplies.

Pack water and food for at least 72 hours, a compact first aid kit, flashlights with extra batteries, a portable phone charger, a whistle, dust masks, a change of clothes, sturdy shoes, copies of essential documents, cash, a local map, and personal hygiene items. Add a emergency blanket, a small tool kit, and a fire-starting method such as waterproof matches or a ferro rod. For a detailed breakdown of what to include, review our home evacuation checklist.

Store your go-bag in an accessible location near your home's main exit. Keep a second, smaller bag in your vehicle for roadside emergencies. Practice grabbing your go-bag and leaving the house within five minutes. Time each drill and identify bottlenecks. Adjust the contents and the bag's location based on what you learn. The goal is for evacuation to become a calm, practiced routine rather than a panicked scramble.

The reason is that after major disasters, it can take days or even weeks for aid to reach your neighborhood. A solid checklist starts at 72 hours and builds toward a two-week supply of water, food, medications, and essential items for every family member.

A family of four needs a minimum of 12 gallons for a three-day supply. In addition to stored water, include water purification tablets or a portable filter so you can extend your supply by treating available water sources.
Having a core universal checklist plus supplementary lists for specific threats is the best approach. A universal checklist covers water, food, first aid, light, and communication. Supplementary checklists add items for your specific risks, such as an evacuation bag for hurricanes, extra warm layers for winter storms, or N95 masks and goggles for wildfire smoke. This layered system keeps you ready without duplicating supplies.
Review and update your checklist twice a year, ideally when you change clocks for daylight saving time. During each review, check expiration dates on food and medications, replace water, test batteries, update contact information, and confirm that seasonal items are appropriate. Also update the checklist after any major life change such as a new baby, a move to a new home, or new medical needs.
