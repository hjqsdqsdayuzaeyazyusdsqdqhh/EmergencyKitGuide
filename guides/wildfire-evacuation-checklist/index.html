Wildfire Evacuation Checklist: What to Pack & When to Leave

Wildfire evacuation checklist for families. Go-bag essentials, important documents, pet evacuation supplies, and when to evacuate during wildfire season.

When to Evacuate During Wildfire Season

The single most important decision you will make during a wildfire is when to leave. The leading cause of wildfire-related deaths is not the fire itself but delayed evacuation. When authorities issue a mandatory evacuation order, leave immediately. Do not wait to see flames, do not wait for a neighbor to leave first, and do not assume the fire will change direction. Wildfires can move at speeds of 14 miles per hour or more through grass and even faster through dry brush and timber, often outrunning vehicles on narrow rural roads.

Even without an official order, you should evacuate if you feel threatened by the fire. Smoke, falling ash, an orange or red sky, and the smell of burning are all signs that a wildfire may be approaching your area. If you are in a evacuation warning zone, pack your go-bag, load your vehicle, and be prepared to leave within five minutes. If conditions worsen, do not wait for the warning to become a mandatory order. Leaving early gives you the best chance of reaching safety before traffic congestion on evacuation routes becomes a problem.

Prepare your evacuation plan before wildfire season begins. Know at least two escape routes from your neighborhood, establish a meeting point for family members who may be apart when an evacuation begins, and keep your vehicle fueled to at least half a tank at all times during fire season. Practice your evacuation plan with every member of your household, including children, so that the process becomes automatic when urgency is high and time is limited.

Building Your Wildfire Go-Bag

A go-bag, also called a bug-out bag, is a pre-packed emergency kit that you can grab and take with you during an evacuation. For wildfire evacuations, your go-bag should contain everything you need to survive and stay comfortable for at least 72 hours, though a week of supplies is preferable if space allows. The key is to have this bag packed and ready well before wildfire season begins so that you are not scrambling to gather items during an actual emergency.

Start with the essentials: water (at least one gallon per person per day for three days), non-perishable food such as energy bars, canned goods with pop-tops, and dried fruit, a flashlight with extra batteries, a battery-powered or hand-crank weather radio, a whistle, dust masks (N95 or P100 rated), a first aid kit, and personal hygiene items including wet wipes, hand sanitizer, and any prescription medications you take regularly. Include a portable phone charger or power bank with a full charge.

Add items specific to your family's needs: infant supplies if you have a baby, extra eyeglasses or contact lenses and solution, copies of insurance documents and identification, a list of emergency contacts, and cash in small denominations. ATMs and card readers may not function during power outages, making cash essential. Store your go-bag in an easily accessible location, such as near the front door or in the trunk of your car. Review and update the contents every six months to ensure nothing has expired and that the bag still meets your family's current needs. Protecting Important Documents

Wildfires can destroy everything in your home, making document preparation one of the most critical aspects of evacuation planning. Gather all essential documents before wildfire season and store copies in a waterproof, fire-resistant portable container that you can grab during evacuation. At minimum, you should have copies of identification documents (driver's licenses, passports, birth certificates), Social Security cards, insurance policies (homeowner's, renter's, auto, life), and financial records.

Create digital backups of all important documents by scanning them and storing the files in a secure cloud storage service. Services like Google Drive, Dropbox, or iCloud provide encrypted storage that is accessible from any device with internet access. Email copies to yourself or a trusted family member who lives outside your area as an additional backup. This ensures that even if your physical copies and home computer are both destroyed, you can still access your documents from another location.

Include additional critical items in your document kit: recent photos of your home and valuables for insurance claims, vehicle titles and registration, medical records and prescription information, pet vaccination records, a copy of your home inventory list, and any legal documents such as wills or power of attorney forms. Keep these documents in a fireproof bag or small safe that fits inside your go-bag. Taking fifteen minutes to prepare this document kit before wildfire season could save you months of headaches dealing with insurance companies and government agencies after a disaster.

Clothing and Personal Items

When packing clothing for wildfire evacuation, prioritize practicality and protection over comfort. Wildfire evacuations often involve driving through areas with heavy smoke, ash, and potentially burning embers. Pack long-sleeved shirts, long pants, closed-toe shoes or boots, and a sturdy hat to protect your skin from heat and falling debris. Avoid synthetic fabrics that can melt when exposed to extreme heat, and choose natural fibers like cotton and wool that provide better heat resistance.

Pack at least three days of clothing per person, including undergarments, socks, and a warm layer for cool evenings. Include a rain jacket or windbreaker, as weather conditions during wildfires can be unpredictable. Sunglasses protect your eyes from smoke irritation and ash. A bandana or neck gaiter can serve as an improvised smoke filter when used with a wet cloth, providing additional respiratory protection beyond your N95 masks.

Personal hygiene items are easy to overlook but important for maintaining health and comfort during an extended evacuation. Pack travel-sized toiletries including a toothbrush, toothpaste, deodorant, soap, and shampoo. Include feminine hygiene products if needed, hand sanitizer, sunscreen, and lip balm. Prescription medications should be packed in sufficient quantity for at least seven days, along with copies of prescriptions in case you need to refill them at a pharmacy in your evacuation area. Small comfort items like a favorite book, a deck of cards, or a small toy for children can make a significant difference in maintaining morale during a stressful evacuation.

Food and Water Supplies

Proper food and water supplies are essential for sustaining your family during a wildfire evacuation that may last longer than anticipated. Pack at least one gallon of water per person per day for a minimum of three days, though five to seven days is preferable. In hot conditions typical of wildfire season, you may need more. Store water in sealed, durable containers that will not leak or break during transport.

Choose non-perishable foods that require no cooking or refrigeration. Energy bars, granola bars, nuts, dried fruit, peanut butter crackers, canned tuna or chicken with pop-top lids, and beef jerky are all excellent options. Pack a manual can opener if you include canned goods without pull-tab lids. Include disposable plates, cups, utensils, and paper towels. Avoid salty foods that increase thirst, and include electrolyte packets or sports drink powder to help maintain hydration in hot conditions.

For infants and young children, pack formula, baby food, bottles, diapers, wipes, and any comfort items they need. For family members with dietary restrictions or food allergies, ensure your go-bag includes suitable alternatives. Store all food in airtight containers to protect against pests and moisture. Check your food and water supplies every six months and replace anything that has reached its expiration date. Having adequate supplies means you will not need to search for food and water during the most dangerous phase of an evacuation, allowing you to focus on reaching safety.

Pet Evacuation Supplies

Your pets are family members, and their evacuation needs must be planned just as carefully as those of human household members. Never leave pets behind during a wildfire evacuation. Start by ensuring your pets are wearing current identification tags with your cell phone number. Microchip your pets and keep the registration information up to date, as collars can come off during the chaos of evacuation. A photo of each pet on your phone helps with identification if you become separated.

Pack a separate bag for each pet containing at least seven days of food and water, medications, vaccination records, a leash and collar, a pet carrier or crate large enough for the animal to stand and turn around in, waste bags, a small litter box and litter for cats, and a favorite toy or blanket for comfort. For dogs, include a muzzle as stressed animals may bite. For birds, pack a travel cage and cover it with a cloth to reduce stress during transport.

Research pet-friendly hotels, motels, boarding facilities, and animal shelters along your evacuation routes before wildfire season begins. Not all evacuation centers accept pets, so having a predetermined destination is critical. Contact friends or family members outside your area who could house your pets temporarily. Keep a list of these contacts in your go-bag along with the phone numbers of local animal hospitals and veterinary clinics that may be operating during the emergency. Vehicle Preparation for Evacuation

Your vehicle is your primary evacuation tool during a wildfire, and keeping it prepared throughout fire season is essential. Maintain at least half a tank of fuel at all times during wildfire season. In a rapidly evolving emergency, gas stations may be closed, out of fuel, or overwhelmed with long lines. A half-full tank gives you the range needed to reach a safe evacuation area without stopping for fuel.

Park your vehicle facing outward in the driveway with the keys in the ignition or on a hook nearby. Keep windows closed and the vehicle locked. Load your go-bags, pet supplies, and any irreplaceable items into the vehicle as soon as you become aware of a nearby fire. Do not wait for an official evacuation order to begin loading your car. The goal is to be ready to drive away within five minutes of making the decision to evacuate.

Keep an emergency car kit in your vehicle that includes jumper cables, a flashlight, basic tools, a tire repair kit, reflective triangles, and a first aid kit. These items help you handle mechanical issues that could strand you during evacuation. Program your phone with the addresses of your evacuation destinations and print paper maps as a backup, since GPS and cell service may be unreliable during a large-scale wildfire. Drive with your headlights on, follow designated evacuation routes, and never drive through heavy smoke. If visibility drops to the point where you cannot see the road, pull over safely, turn off your engine, and stay in the vehicle with the windows closed until conditions improve.

Before You Leave Home

In the minutes before you evacuate, completing a quick series of tasks can help protect your home and ensure a smoother evacuation. Close all windows and doors, but leave them unlocked so firefighters can access your home if needed. Remove curtains and move furniture away from windows. Move combustible items like patio furniture, doormats, and trash cans away from the house. Close the fireplace damper.

Shut off the gas at the meter if you know how to do so safely. Turn off air conditioning and heating systems to prevent them from circulating smoke into the house. Turn on all exterior lights, including porch lights and pool lights, to make your home visible through smoke. Leave a note on the door with your evacuation destination and contact information in case emergency responders need to reach you.

Do not waste precious time on non-essential tasks. Do not try to protect your home with garden hoses or sprinkler systems, as this is extremely dangerous and rarely effective against wildfire. Do not pack unnecessary items. Do not stop to take photographs for insurance purposes. Focus on the essentials: grab your go-bag, load your vehicle, secure your pets, and leave. If you have livestock or large animals, follow your pre-established evacuation plan for their removal. Every minute you spend on non-essential tasks is a minute the fire uses to get closer.

Planning Your Escape Route

Effective wildfire evacuation depends on knowing your escape routes before you need them. Identify at least two different routes out of your neighborhood that lead to safety. Study these routes by driving them regularly so you know every turn, every potential bottleneck, and every alternate path. Rural roads and mountain passes can become congested quickly during mass evacuations, and knowing an alternate route can save your life.

Share your evacuation plan with every member of your household, including older children. Designate a meeting point outside your neighborhood where family members can reunite if you are separated when an evacuation begins. Establish contact with a friend or relative who lives outside your immediate area as a central communication point. During wildfires, local phone lines may be overloaded, but texts and social media messages often still get through.

Identify multiple evacuation destinations at varying distances from your home. A nearby hotel or friend's house serves as a short-term option, while a location fifty or more miles away provides a longer-term refuge if the fire covers a wide area. Keep a printed list of addresses and phone numbers for these destinations in your go-bag. Check local emergency management websites and apps for real-time evacuation route information during an active fire, as authorities may close roads and open contraflow lanes to improve evacuation traffic flow. Preparation and flexibility are both critical during rapidly changing wildfire conditions.

After Evacuation: What to Do Next

Once you have evacuated safely, your priorities shift to communication, documentation, and planning for the next steps. Contact your designated out-of-area emergency contact to report that you are safe. Check in with other family members and friends to ensure everyone has evacuated. Monitor local news, emergency management social media accounts, and your county's emergency alert system for updates on the fire's progress, road closures, and when it may be safe to return home.

Contact your insurance company as soon as possible to begin the claims process, even if you are unsure whether your home has been damaged. Document everything: take photos of any damage you can see, keep receipts for all evacuation-related expenses including hotel rooms, food, and fuel, and save all communication with emergency services and insurance representatives. These records will be essential for filing accurate claims and receiving the compensation you are entitled to.

When authorities give the all-clear to return home, do so cautiously. Watch for downed power lines, weakened structures, and hot spots that could reignite. Check your home for gas leaks before entering. Do not drink tap water until authorities confirm it is safe. Be prepared for the emotional impact of returning to a damaged home, and seek support from community resources, counselors, and disaster relief organizations. The Red Cross, FEMA, and local community organizations provide assistance with temporary housing, food, and financial aid for wildfire victims.
