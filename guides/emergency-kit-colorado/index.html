Emergency Kit Colorado: Wildfires, Blizzards, and Mountain Emergency Preparedness

Colorado emergency kit guide covering wildfires, blizzards, flooding, extreme altitude risks, and mountain emergencies. State-specific disaster preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Colorado's Unique Emergency Landscape

Colorado is a state of dramatic extremes that demand comprehensive emergency preparedness. From the towering peaks of the Rocky Mountains exceeding 14,000 feet to the sweeping high plains along the Front Range, the state faces an interconnected web of natural hazards including wildfires, blizzards, flash floods, tornadoes, and earthquakes. The Colorado Division of Homeland Security and Emergency Management coordinates disaster response across the state, but the reality is that Colorado's vast geography and mountain terrain mean many communities face extended isolation during major events. A well-prepared emergency kit tailored to Colorado's specific threats is not just a convenience but a necessity for every household.

The state's population has grown rapidly along the Front Range corridor from Fort Collins to Colorado Springs, pushing residential development deeper into wildfire-prone areas. Meanwhile, mountain communities along I-70 and in the San Juan range depend on roads that close regularly during winter storms. The devastating Marshall Fire of December 2021, which destroyed over 1,000 homes in Boulder County suburban neighborhoods, demonstrated that wildfire is not just a mountain problem. Whether you live in a Denver high-rise or a remote cabin near Telluride, this guide addresses the emergencies you are most likely to face and how to prepare for them.

Wildfire Preparedness: Lessons from the Marshall Fire and Beyond

Colorado's wildfire risk extends across every elevation and ecosystem in the state. The wildland-urban interface along the Front Range, the mountain communities in Summit and Eagle counties, and the pine forests surrounding Colorado Springs all face significant threat. The Marshall Fire proved that wind-driven embers can ignite suburban subdivisions miles from any flame front, with drought conditions and 100-mile-per-hour winds creating catastrophic conditions even in December. Wildfire season in Colorado now effectively runs year-round, though the peak risk periods are April through June in the foothills and July through October in the high mountains.

Every Colorado household should maintain a go-bag ready for immediate evacuation during fire season. Include N95 respirator masks to protect against smoke, protective goggles, a comprehensive 72-hour survival kit, copies of important documents, and enough cash to cover several days of expenses. Keep your vehicle facing outward in the driveway and maintain at least half a tank of fuel at all times during fire season. Register for your county's emergency alert system and download the FEMA app for real-time notifications. Follow our wildfire preparedness guide for defensible space and home hardening strategies.

Blizzard and Extreme Cold Survival

Colorado's winter storms can deliver several feet of snow in a single event, with blizzard conditions shutting down highways and entire communities for days. The Front Range corridor from Fort Collins to Pueblo is particularly vulnerable to upslope storms that combine heavy snowfall with high winds, reducing visibility to zero and creating dangerous wind chill temperatures well below negative 20 degrees. Mountain communities at elevations above 8,000 feet regularly receive over 300 inches of annual snowfall, and backcountry areas can see even more. Hypothermia and frostbite are genuine risks for anyone caught outdoors, and power outages during winter storms can leave homes without heat for extended periods.

Your winter emergency kit should include heavy sleeping bags rated to at least zero degrees, thermal base layers, waterproof outer shells, insulated boots, and hand and toe warmers. Pack a home emergency kit that includes non-perishable food requiring no cooking, a manual can opener, and at least seven days of water stored where it will not freeze. For heating, maintain a backup source such as a wood stove, kerosene heater, or propane heater, along with adequate fuel reserves. Always include a battery-powered carbon monoxide detector, as CO poisoning from improper indoor heating is one of the leading causes of winter emergency deaths in Colorado.

Flash Flooding and Burn Scar Debris Flows

Colorado faces a persistent flash flood risk that intensifies dramatically in the years following major wildfires. Burned hillside areas lose the vegetation that normally absorbs rainfall, and subsequent storms can trigger devastating debris flows even from moderate precipitation. Communities downstream from the Cameron Peak Fire, East Troublesome Fire, and Marshall Fire burn scars continue to face elevated flood risk years after the original fires. Flash floods can occur with little warning in the steep terrain of the Front Range and mountain valleys, and the force of debris-laden floodwaters can destroy vehicles and structures.

Include a flood emergency kit with waterproof bags for electronics and documents, sturdy waterproof boots, and a portable weather radio. If you live downstream from a burn scar area, know your evacuation routes and keep go-bags packed during spring and summer storm season. Monitor the National Weather Service alerts for your area and never drive through flooded roadways. Colorado's steep terrain means floodwaters can be far deeper and faster-moving than they appear, and debris carried by mountain floods includes rocks, logs, and other projectiles that can be deadly.

Altitude Sickness and Mountain Health Risks

Colorado's elevation presents health risks that residents of lower-altitude states rarely encounter. Above 7,000 feet, the reduced oxygen levels cause the body to dehydrate faster, burn more calories, and struggle with physical exertion. Altitude sickness can affect anyone regardless of fitness level, and symptoms including headache, nausea, dizziness, and fatigue can escalate to life-threatening conditions like high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) or high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE). Visitors coming from sea level are particularly vulnerable, but even long-time Colorado residents can experience altitude-related illness when traveling to higher elevations or during rapid weather changes.

Your mountain emergency kit should include acetazolamide (Diamox) or other altitude sickness medication, electrolyte powder for rapid rehydration, and a pulse oximeter to monitor blood oxygen levels. Increase water intake to at least three gallons per person per day at high elevation. Pack high-calorie, easy-to-digest snacks and understand the warning signs that require immediate descent. If you live in a mountain community, ensure your family 72-hour emergency kit includes altitude-specific medical supplies and that every family member knows how to recognize altitude sickness symptoms.

Mountain Road Closures and I-70 Corridor Preparedness

Colorado's mountain highway system is both a lifeline and a vulnerability. Interstate 70, the primary corridor connecting Denver to mountain communities and the Western Slope, closes regularly during winter storms, with closures sometimes lasting 12 to 24 hours or more. Mountain passes like Loveland, Vail, and Wolf Creek can accumulate several feet of snow in a single storm cycle, and avalanche control work can further delay travel. Secondary mountain roads leading to smaller communities may not be cleared as quickly, leaving residents isolated for extended periods. Even summer travel on mountain highways carries risk from rockslides, wildfire evacuations, and sudden thunderstorms.

Mountain residents should maintain a minimum seven-day emergency supply at home and keep their vehicles equipped for extended stranding. Pack a winter car survival kit with blankets, hand warmers, a small shovel, kitty litter for traction, jumper cables, a flashlight, and non-perishable snacks. Keep your gas tank above half during winter months. Learn multiple routes to reach your home in case primary roads are blocked, and consider a satellite communicator like a Garmin inReach for areas without cell coverage. Front Range Urban vs. Mountain Rural Preparedness

Colorado's population is heavily concentrated along the Front Range urban corridor, but a significant portion of the state's residents live in mountain and rural areas with very different emergency profiles. Denver and its suburbs benefit from robust municipal services, multiple hospital systems, and relatively quick emergency response times. Mountain communities like those in Grand County, Pitkin County, or San Juan County may rely on a single road for access, depend on volunteer fire departments, and face response times measured in hours rather than minutes. Understanding your community's specific vulnerabilities is the first step in building an appropriate emergency kit.

Urban residents should focus on home emergency kit supplies for sheltering in place, earthquake preparedness given Colorado's proximity to active fault zones, and evacuation plans for wildfire scenarios. Mountain and rural residents need to expand their kits to include well water backup systems, extended food and fuel supplies, comprehensive tool kits for property repairs, and satellite communication devices. Both populations should invest in emergency water storage and backup power solutions appropriate for their specific situation.

Well Water and Propane Dependence in Mountain Communities

Many rural Colorado homes depend on private wells for water and propane for heating and cooking. During extended power outages, electric well pumps stop working, cutting off the primary water supply entirely. Propane deliveries can be delayed by days or weeks during severe weather, leaving homes without heat in dangerously cold conditions. The combination of no water and no heat during a Colorado winter storm represents one of the most serious emergency scenarios for rural residents. Planning for these contingencies before they occur is essential for survival.

Install a hand pump on your well or invest in a generator capable of running your well pump during outages. Maintain a minimum 50-gallon gravity-fed water reserve for emergency use. For propane dependence, ensure your tank is at least half full entering winter and have a backup heating source like a wood stove. Store at least two full cords of firewood if you rely on wood heat. Include a battery-powered carbon monoxide detector in your emergency supplies, and always have fire extinguishers rated for grease and propane fires. Frequently Asked Questions

How does altitude affect emergency preparedness in Colorado?
At elevations above 7,000 feet, the body requires more water and calories to function properly. Dehydration occurs faster, and altitude sickness can strike anyone regardless of fitness level. Your emergency kit should include extra water, electrolyte supplements, and altitude sickness medication like acetazolamide. Residents above 10,000 feet should maintain a 7-day supply rather than 3 days due to mountain isolation.
What did the Marshall Fire teach Colorado about wildfire preparedness?
The December 2021 Marshall Fire destroyed over 1,000 homes in Boulder County, demonstrating that wildfire can strike suburban neighborhoods in any season. Wind-driven embers can ignite structures miles from the fire front, so defensible space and ember-resistant construction matter in urban areas too. It highlighted the need for ready go-bags in every home, even in subdivisions far from forest land.
How should I prepare for Colorado blizzards?
Colorado can receive heavy snowfall from September through May, and blizzard conditions can strand motorists on mountain passes for hours. Keep winter survival gear in your vehicle including blankets, snacks, water, a small shovel, and sand or cat litter for traction. At home, maintain a 7-day food and water supply and ensure your heating fuel is stocked.
Are mountain road closures a real concern for emergency supplies?
Absolutely. I-70, I-25, and numerous mountain highways close regularly during winter storms, avalanches, and rockslides. Mountain communities can be cut off for days. Residents should stock at least 7 to 14 days of supplies, keep vehicles fueled above half tank, and have multiple evacuation routes mapped in case primary roads are blocked.
Do I need special preparation if I rely on well water in Colorado?
Yes, many rural Colorado properties depend on electric well pumps. During power outages, you lose all water access. Install a hand pump on your well as a backup, or keep a large gravity-fed water storage system. A minimum 50-gallon reserve is recommended for rural households.
How should mountain communities prepare for propane heating emergencies?
Many mountain homes rely on propane for heating and cooking. During extended outages, propane deliveries may be delayed. Keep at least a half-full tank heading into winter, and have a backup heat source like a wood stove or kerosene heater. Include a carbon monoxide detector in your kit and ensure adequate ventilation when using any combustion-based heating indoors.
What burn scar flooding risks should Colorado residents know about?
After wildfires, burned hillside areas lose vegetation that normally absorbs rainfall. Subsequent storms can trigger dangerous debris flows and flooding in burn scar zones. Residents downstream should monitor weather alerts closely, maintain evacuation go-bags, and know their flood zone status.
