Emergency Kit Alaska: Extreme Cold, Earthquakes, and Remote Wilderness Preparedness

Alaska emergency kit guide covering extreme cold, earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanic hazards, and remote area survival. State-specific wilderness preparedness.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Alaska Demands the Most Rigorous Emergency Preparedness

Alaska is the most extreme preparedness challenge in the United States. No other state combines such severe cold, such powerful earthquakes, such active volcanoes, and such vast distances between communities. The state experiences winter temperatures that plunge past minus 40 degrees Fahrenheit, earthquakes that exceed magnitude 8.0, volcanic eruptions that send ash clouds across the entire North Pacific, and tsunamis that can race across the ocean at jetliner speed. Add to this the reality that many Alaskan communities are accessible only by air or water, and it becomes clear that a standard 72-hour emergency kit is simply not enough for most Alaskan households.

The Alaska Division of Homeland Security and Emergency Management coordinates disaster response across a landmass larger than Texas, California, and Montana combined, and the logistical challenge of reaching remote communities during emergencies cannot be overstated. Whether you live in Anchorage, Fairbanks, Juneau, or a bush village on the Yukon River, your emergency planning must account for extended isolation, limited medical facilities, extreme darkness during winter months, and the complete absence of road connections to the outside world. Building the right emergency kit for Alaska means preparing not just for days, but potentially for weeks of self-sufficiency.

Surviving Extreme Cold: Alaska's Deadliest Threat

Cold exposure is the most immediate and persistent life-threatening hazard for Alaskans. Interior and northern communities experience regular temperatures below minus 40 degrees from November through March, and wind chill can push effective temperatures to minus 60 or lower. Frostbite can develop on exposed skin in under five minutes at these temperatures. Hypothermia is the leading cause of cold-weather emergency room visits, and death from exposure is a tragic annual reality in Alaska. Power outages during winter storms can leave homes without heat within hours, and heating fuel delivery may be impossible during severe weather events.

Your cold-weather emergency kit must include expedition-rated sleeping bags rated to at least minus 40 degrees for each family member, layered clothing systems with moisture-wicking base layers, fleece or down mid-layers, and windproof waterproof outer shells. Pack chemical hand and toe warmers, insulated sleeping pads, and extra dry clothing sealed in waterproof stuff sacks. Battery performance drops dramatically in extreme cold, so keep spare batteries warm in inner pockets or insulated pouches. Store backup heating supplies including seasoned firewood, a hand-crank or battery-powered space heater, and extra propane if your home uses gas. Our 72-hour kit guide covers the basics, but Alaskans need to expand significantly on those recommendations.

Earthquake and Tsunami Preparedness

Alaska is the second most seismically active state in the United States, and the 1964 Good Friday earthquake remains one of the most powerful ever recorded in North America at magnitude 9.2. That event generated tsunamis that killed 131 people across Alaska and the Pacific coast, destroyed entire communities, and reshaped the coastline. The Alaska-Aleutian subduction zone continues to produce frequent earthquakes, and coastal communities from Kodiak to Dutch Harbor to Seward face ongoing tsunami risk. In 2018, a magnitude 7.1 earthquake near Anchorage caused significant damage to roads, buildings, and infrastructure across Southcentral Alaska.

Coastal Alaskans must prepare for earthquake and tsunami scenarios simultaneously. Keep a go-bag near your door containing identification, medications, warm clothing, and essential documents. Know your tsunami evacuation route and the elevation of high ground near your home. When strong shaking stops near the coast, do not wait for an official warning, move to high ground immediately. Store a gas shutoff wrench, heavy-duty work gloves, and sturdy boots in your earthquake emergency kit. For households along the coast, review NOAA tsunami hazard maps and practice evacuation drills with all family members at least twice per year. The Alaska Earthquake Center provides real-time seismic monitoring and alerts.

Volcanic Ash and Eruption Preparedness

Alaska sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire and contains over 130 volcanoes, with approximately 50 classified as active. The Alaska Volcano Observatory monitors these peaks constantly, and eruptions occur multiple times per year. Volcanic ash is not soft like snow, it is composed of jagged particles of rock and glass that can cut through engine filters, contaminate water supplies, collapse roofs under accumulated weight, and cause severe respiratory distress in humans and animals. The 2008 eruption of Kasatochi Island sent ash clouds that disrupted air travel across the entire North Pacific for days. Communities hundreds of miles downwind of an eruption can experience hazardous ashfall even without direct volcanic activity nearby.

Prepare for volcanic ashfall by keeping P100 respirator masks or gas masks with appropriate filters for every household member. Stock sealed water in closed containers since ash can infiltrate open supplies and contaminate cisterns and rainwater collection systems. Keep goggles to protect eyes from abrasive ash particles, and seal ventilation openings in your home with plastic sheeting and duct tape during ashfall events. Store tarps to protect vehicles and outdoor equipment. After an ashfall, avoid driving until roads are cleared as ash severely reduces visibility and damages engines. Our home emergency kit guide covers indoor shelter-in-place supplies that are essential during ashfall events.

Bush Village Isolation and Extended Self-Sufficiency

Approximately 80% of Alaska's communities have no road access to the outside world. Residents of bush villages in the Interior, Western Alaska, and along the Arctic coast depend on bush planes, seasonal barges, and snowmachines for transportation and supply delivery. During severe winter storms, blizzards, or periods of river ice conditions that prevent travel, these communities can be completely isolated for weeks or even months. Medical emergencies requiring hospital care may need to be evacuated by air, and even that option depends on weather conditions and available aircraft. The economic reality in many villages means that maintaining large emergency food stores is a financial challenge, yet it is also an absolute necessity.

Bush village residents must plan for at least 30 days of food and fuel independence. Build a community-level emergency plan with designated supply caches, shared generator fuel reserves, and coordinated communication protocols. Maintain satellite phones or HAM radios for emergency contact since cell service does not exist in most remote areas. Stock chainsaw fuel and spare parts, keep snowmachines in good repair with spare belts and tools, and maintain hunting and fishing equipment as a backup food source. Many Alaska Native communities incorporate traditional subsistence practices into their emergency planning, storing dried fish, smoked meat, and preserved berries alongside commercial emergency rations. Anchorage Urban Emergency Planning

Anchorage is Alaska's largest city with approximately 290,000 residents in the metropolitan area, and while it has road connections to other parts of the state, it remains vulnerable to extended isolation. The city sits in a seismically active zone, has experienced significant earthquakes within recent memory, and depends on a limited number of road and rail corridors for supply delivery. A major earthquake or severe winter storm could cut Anchorage off from the Kenai Peninsula and the rest of the road system for days or weeks. The city's aging infrastructure, including water and sewer systems and the Port of Anchorage, faces vulnerability to seismic damage that would disrupt essential services for hundreds of thousands of people.

Anchorage residents should maintain a minimum two-week supply of food, water, and essential medications stored at home. Keep vehicles fueled above half capacity during winter months and maintain an emergency kit in each car that includes a sleeping bag, fire-starting supplies, food, water, and a shovel. Know the location of your nearest warming center and emergency shelter. Register for Alert Anchorage to receive emergency notifications. Identify at least two evacuation routes from your neighborhood and practice your family communication plan. Given the possibility of extended power outages, invest in a emergency power backup system sized to run essential heating and communication equipment for at least one week.

Winter Darkness and Mental Health Preparedness

Alaska's extreme darkness during winter months is a factor that most emergency preparedness guides overlook but that Alaskans know profoundly affects daily life. In Fairbanks, the sun rises after 10:00 AM and sets before 3:00 PM in December, and in UtqiaÄ¡vik above the Arctic Circle, the sun does not rise at all for 65 days. Extended power outages during these dark months compound the psychological stress of emergency situations. Seasonal Affective Disorder affects a significant percentage of Alaskans, and the isolation of an emergency during the darkest weeks of winter can create serious mental health challenges, especially for elderly residents and those living alone.

Include full-spectrum lighting in your emergency supplies, including battery-powered light therapy lamps if any household members are sensitive to seasonal darkness. Pack decks of cards, books, puzzles, and other forms of entertainment that do not require electricity to reduce boredom and anxiety during extended indoor sheltering. Keep a battery-powered NOAA weather radio so you can monitor conditions and maintain connection to the outside world. For families with children, pack comfort items, games, and activities to reduce stress and maintain normalcy. If you or a family member takes antidepressant medications, maintain at least a 30-day supply in your emergency kit. Heating Fuel Dependence and Backup Systems

Heating is not optional in Alaska, it is a matter of life and death. Most Alaskan homes use heating oil, natural gas, or wood as their primary heat source, and the failure of any heating system during winter creates an emergency within minutes. Homes can drop to dangerously cold temperatures within two to four hours after heating failure when outdoor temperatures are below zero. Heating fuel delivery depends on functioning roads, ports, and distribution infrastructure, all of which can be disrupted by earthquakes, severe storms, or other disasters. Rural communities that depend on barge-delivered heating oil may face fuel shortages if delivery schedules are interrupted for more than a few weeks.

Maintain a backup heating source for every Alaskan home. A cord of seasoned firewood provides reliable backup heat for homes with wood stoves or fireplaces, and propane heaters rated for indoor use with battery-powered carbon monoxide detectors offer an alternative for homes without wood-burning capability. Never use outdoor propane heaters, gas grills, or charcoal burners indoors as they produce deadly carbon monoxide gas. Keep your primary fuel tank as full as possible throughout winter. Store extra heating oil in approved containers if local regulations permit. Test your backup heating system before the first cold weather arrives each fall, and ensure every family member knows how to operate it safely.

Communication and Emergency Alerts in Remote Alaska

Reliable communication is the foundation of emergency response, and Alaska presents enormous communication challenges across its vast territory. Cell phone coverage is limited to major cities and the road system, leaving most of the state without cellular service. Satellite phones provide the most reliable emergency communication for remote areas, though they require a clear view of the sky and can be expensive. HAM radio operators form a critical emergency communication network in Alaska, and the Alaska Amateur Radio Emergency Service coordinates volunteer operators who provide backup communication during disasters when commercial systems fail.

Every Alaskan household should have at least two forms of emergency communication available. A satellite communicator such as a Garmin inReach provides two-way text messaging and SOS capability independent of cell networks. Battery-powered NOAA weather radios receive alerts and forecasts from the National Weather Service across all of Alaska. Keep written contact lists and meeting plans in each family member's go-bag, since phones may be dead or unreachable when you need to coordinate. In remote communities, establish a physical message board or check-in system with neighbors so that welfare can be verified even when electronic communication fails. The Alaska Division of Homeland Security provides resources for community-level communication planning.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much emergency food should I store for an Alaskan winter?
Alaska residents should store a minimum of two weeks of emergency food, and ideally one month or more during winter months. Remote communities can lose road access for weeks due to avalanches, ice jams, or extreme storms. Focus on high-calorie, freeze-dried, and canned foods that require no refrigeration. A family of four should have at least 80,000 total calories in storage to sustain themselves through an extended winter isolation event.
What heating fuel should I stockpile for emergencies?
Most Alaskan homes use heating oil, natural gas, or wood for primary heat. Store at least 50 extra gallons of heating oil if you use oil heat, and maintain a cord of seasoned firewood as backup. Propane heaters rated for indoor use with proper ventilation and carbon monoxide detectors are a secondary option. Never use outdoor propane heaters, camp stoves, or charcoal grills indoors as they produce deadly carbon monoxide gas.
How do I prepare for an Alaska earthquake or tsunami?
Alaska is the second most seismically active state in the US. For earthquakes, secure heavy furniture, store a gas shutoff wrench, and keep sturdy boots near your bed. Coastal residents must also prepare for tsunamis. Know your tsunami evacuation route, keep a go-bag ready near your exit, and when you feel strong shaking near the coast, move to high ground immediately without waiting for an official warning. The 1964 Alaska earthquake generated tsunamis that killed 131 people.
What should a bush village emergency kit include?
Bush village kits need to account for complete isolation from supply chains. Include at least 30 days of food, a robust medical kit with prescription medications, satellite communication equipment, a hand-powered or generator-driven water purification system, chainsaw fuel, gun ammunition for hunting, and a comprehensive tool kit. Many bush villages are accessible only by air or water, so supplies must sustain residents until the next barge or plane can arrive, which may be weeks or months.
How do I protect against extreme cold in my emergency kit?
Layer your clothing system with moisture-wicking base layers, insulating mid-layers, and windproof outer shells. Pack expedition-rated sleeping bags rated to at least minus 40 degrees, insulated sleeping pads, hand and toe warmers, and backup batteries kept warm in your pockets since cold drains battery life rapidly. Keep extra dry clothing sealed in waterproof bags and store fire-starting materials including waterproof matches, ferro rods, and tinder in multiple locations.
How long can I expect to be isolated during an Alaskan emergency?
In urban areas like Anchorage or Fairbanks, isolation typically lasts 24 to 72 hours after a major event. In bush villages and remote communities, isolation can last weeks to months. The 2018 Anchorage earthquake disrupted services for days, while some Western Alaska villages have been stranded for over a month during severe winter storms. Your supply planning should match your community's accessibility level.
What volcanic hazards should Alaskans prepare for?
Alaska has over 130 volcanoes, with about 50 considered active. Volcanic ash can contaminate water supplies, collapse roofs under its weight, damage engines, and cause severe respiratory problems. Ashfall events can affect areas hundreds of miles from the eruption. Keep N95 and P100 respirator masks, sealed water supplies, goggles, and tarps to cover ventilation intakes. Monitor the Alaska Volcano Observatory for current activity alerts.
