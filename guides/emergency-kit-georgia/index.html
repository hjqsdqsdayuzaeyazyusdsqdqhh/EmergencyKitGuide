Emergency Kit Georgia: Hurricane, Tornado, and Severe Weather Preparedness

Georgia emergency kit guide for hurricanes on the coast, tornadoes inland, severe thunderstorms, flooding, extreme heat, and winter ice storms.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Georgia's Unique Multi-Hazard Environment

Georgia occupies a geographic position that exposes residents to an extraordinary range of natural hazards throughout the year. The 100-mile Atlantic coastline faces direct hurricane threats during the June through November storm season, with Savannah, Brunswick, and the barrier islands bearing the brunt of storm surge, high winds, and coastal flooding. Inland, south Georgia and the central part of the state experience a significant tornado threat, with late winter and spring severe weather outbreaks producing violent twisters that have devastated communities from Americus to Albany.

Beyond hurricanes and tornadoes, Georgia contends with severe thunderstorms that bring destructive straight-line winds, large hail, and flash flooding across every region. Extreme heat and oppressive humidity dominate the summer months, with heat index values routinely exceeding 110 degrees Fahrenheit. The combination of heat and moisture creates dangerous conditions for outdoor workers, the elderly, and anyone without reliable air conditioning. In winter, Georgia occasionally experiences ice storms that can paralyze the Atlanta metro area and surrounding regions for days, as demonstrated by the crippling ice event of 2014 that stranded thousands on highway overpasses.

Building an emergency kit for Georgia means preparing for hazards that can strike in any season. A family in Savannah needs a robust hurricane evacuation plan, while a family in Valdosta must prioritize tornado shelter and a family in Atlanta needs to plan for urban emergencies where transportation corridors can become impassable within minutes. This guide provides comprehensive, state-specific guidance to help every Georgia household prepare for the disasters most likely to affect their community.

Hurricane Preparedness for Coastal Georgia

Georgia's 100 miles of Atlantic coastline, from the South Carolina border to the Florida line, faces serious hurricane risk. The coastal cities of Savannah, Tybee Island, St. Simons Island, Jekyll Island, and Brunswick have experienced devastating storms throughout history, and development pressure continues to increase the number of people and structures in harm's way. The Georgia coast is particularly vulnerable to storm surge due to its gently sloping continental shelf, which allows storm-driven water to push far inland. Hurricane Matthew in 2016 and Hurricane Irma in 2017 both caused significant damage along the Georgia coast even though neither made a direct hit on the state.

Every coastal Georgia household needs a comprehensive hurricane kit that includes seven days of supplies, an evacuation plan, and a go-bag packed and ready to move at a moment's notice. Store at least one gallon of water per person per day for seven days, along with non-perishable food, manual can opener, and disposable utensils. Include a battery-powered or hand-crank NOAA weather radio, flashlights, extra batteries, a portable phone charger, first aid supplies, and a seven-day supply of prescription medications. Pack waterproof bags for important documents including insurance policies, identification, and medical records. Our hurricane emergency kit guide provides a complete supply checklist.

Know your evacuation zone and route before hurricane season begins. Georgia designates coastal evacuation zones using a tiered system, and mandatory evacuations are ordered for the lowest zones first. Primary evacuation routes include I-95 northbound, I-16 westbound from Savannah, and US-17 and US-301 northbound. These corridors become extremely congested during evacuations, so leave early when an evacuation is announced. Keep your vehicle fuel tank at least three-quarters full during hurricane season and carry extra fuel in approved containers. Register for Georgia's county emergency notification system to receive evacuation orders immediately.

Tornado Preparedness for South and Central Georgia

Georgia averages over 25 tornadoes per year, with the highest risk concentrated in south Georgia and the central part of the state. The peak tornado season runs from February through May, when powerful cold fronts clash with warm, moist Gulf air over the state. The 2008 Super Tuesday tornado outbreak produced multiple violent tornadoes across south Georgia, including an EF3 that struck Atlanta's southern suburbs. Unlike the Great Plains, Georgia tornadoes often occur in the cooler months and frequently strike with limited warning, making preparedness essential year-round.

Every Georgia household should designate a tornado shelter area in the lowest interior room of the building, away from windows. Pack helmets or hard hats for each family member, as head injuries are the leading cause of tornado fatalities. Include a battery-powered weather radio with alert function, sturdy shoes stored near the shelter area, a whistle for signaling rescuers, and a basic tool kit for self-rescue. Mobile home residents must identify the nearest sturdy building and practice reaching it within two minutes, since mobile homes offer zero protection in even weak tornadoes.

After a tornado passes, your kit should include work gloves, safety goggles, a dust mask, a pry bar, and a flashlight for navigating through debris. Keep a first aid kit stocked with trauma supplies including pressure bandages, tourniquet, and medical tape. Have your insurance agent's phone number and your policy number written on a card in your kit so you can begin the claims process immediately. Our tornado emergency kit guide covers shelter identification and post-storm procedures in detail.

Extreme Heat and Humidity Survival

Georgia's summers are long, hot, and dangerously humid. From June through September, daytime high temperatures routinely reach the mid-90s while humidity levels push heat index values above 105 degrees. During multi-day heat waves, overnight low temperatures may not drop below the upper 70s, preventing the body from recovering from daytime heat stress. Georgia records an average of five to eight heat-related deaths each year, with the elderly, outdoor workers, young children, and those without air conditioning facing the highest risk.

Your Georgia emergency kit should include supplies specific to heat-related emergencies. Store at least two gallons of water per person per day during summer months, significantly more than the standard recommendation. Pack electrolyte replacement packets, cooling towels, and a spray bottle for misting. Include a battery-powered fan for use during power outages. If you rely on air conditioning, plan for extended power outages by identifying cooling centers in your community, such as public libraries, community centers, and churches that open during heat emergencies.

Know the warning signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke. Heat exhaustion symptoms include heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache, and cool clammy skin. If untreated, it progresses to heat stroke, characterized by hot dry skin, confusion, loss of consciousness, and a body temperature above 103 degrees. Heat stroke is a medical emergency requiring immediate 911 response. Move the affected person to a cool area, apply ice packs to the neck, armpits, and groin, and provide cool water if the person is conscious and alert. A well-stocked home emergency kit should always include a basic first aid manual with heat illness treatment instructions.

Flash Flooding and River Flood Preparedness

Flash flooding is one of the most dangerous hazards in Georgia, capable of producing life-threatening conditions with little warning. The state's numerous rivers, creeks, and low-lying areas become overwhelmed during heavy rain events, and the combination of Georgia's red clay soil and steep terrain in the Piedmont and mountain regions accelerates runoff into narrow valleys. The Flint, Ocmulgee, Oconee, and Chattahoochee river systems all have histories of devastating floods. Urban areas like Atlanta face particular flash flood risk because impervious surfaces like roads and parking lots direct massive volumes of water into storm drains that can quickly become overwhelmed.

Build your emergency kit with flood risk in mind. Store important documents in waterproof containers and keep digital copies on an encrypted USB drive. Pack a portable weather radio so you receive flash flood warnings immediately. Keep a go-bag near your front door with a change of clothes, medications, cash, and a phone charger so you can evacuate quickly if water rises. Never drive through flooded roadways; just twelve inches of moving water can float a car and two feet can sweep away an SUV. Our flood emergency kit guide covers water-resistant gear and evacuation planning.

Georgia property owners should verify their flood risk using FEMA flood maps and consider purchasing flood insurance through the National Flood Insurance Program. Standard homeowner policies do not cover flood damage. Elevate critical utilities like HVAC systems, water heaters, and electrical panels where possible. Install sump pumps with battery backup in basements and crawl spaces. Keep sandbags, plastic sheeting, and a sump pump in your emergency supply arsenal to protect your home when flooding threatens.

Water Storage and Supply for Georgia Homes

Georgia's water supply faces vulnerabilities during multiple types of emergencies. Hurricanes can contaminate municipal water systems with saltwater intrusion and sewage overflow. Tornadoes and severe storms can damage water treatment facilities and distribution pipes. During extended power outages, water treatment plants may lose the ability to purify and pump water. Rural Georgia communities that depend on private wells lose water access entirely when electric well pumps stop functioning. The state's growing population and agricultural water demands also create long-term supply concerns during drought periods.

Store a minimum of one gallon of water per person per day for at least seven days, with two gallons recommended during Georgia's hot summer months. Use food-grade plastic containers and replace the supply every six months. Keep water purification tablets, a portable filter, and unscented household bleach for emergency disinfection. For rural households with private wells, install a hand-operated backup pump and store extra water to cover the gap during power outages. Learn more about water storage options and purification methods in our emergency water storage guide.

Georgia's hot, humid climate accelerates bacterial growth in stored water, so proper storage and rotation are critical. Keep water containers sealed and stored in cool, dark locations away from direct sunlight. After any flood event, test well water before drinking it, even if the water appears clear. Contact your county health department for free or low-cost water testing. Contaminated well water can cause serious gastrointestinal illness, and floodwaters can carry agricultural chemicals, sewage, and industrial contaminants into groundwater supplies.

Ice Storm Preparedness and Winter Weather in Georgia

While Georgia is primarily a warm-climate state, winter ice storms pose a serious and underappreciated risk, particularly in the northern half of the state and the Atlanta metro area. Georgia's infrastructure is not designed for sustained ice accumulation, and even a quarter inch of ice can topple trees, down power lines, and make roads impassable. The 2014 Atlanta ice storm stranded thousands of commuters on highway overpasses and left hundreds of thousands without power for days. The 2011 Groundhog Day ice storm caused over $100 million in damage across north Georgia. These events demonstrate that Georgia can experience winter weather emergencies as disruptive as any hurricane.

Prepare your winter emergency kit before cold weather arrives. Stock at least five days of non-perishable food, one gallon of water per person per day, and a backup heating source that is safe for indoor use. Keep extra blankets, sleeping bags, and warm clothing accessible. Store a charged portable power bank and backup batteries for flashlights and weather radio. Georgia homeowners should insulate exposed pipes to prevent bursting during freezes and know where your water shut-off valve is located in case pipes do break. Keep your vehicle's gas tank at least three-quarters full during winter months.

If you live in metro Atlanta or another Georgia city, prepare for the possibility of being stranded at work or on the road during an ice event. Keep an emergency kit in your vehicle with blankets, non-perishable food, water, a flashlight, and a small shovel. Identify alternate routes home that avoid highway overpasses and steep grades. Talk with your employer about early release policies during winter weather events so you can get home before conditions deteriorate. Georgia Emergency Management and Evacuation Planning

The Georgia Emergency Management and Homeland Security Agency (GEMA/HS) serves as the state's primary coordinating body for disaster preparedness, response, and recovery. GEMA/HS works with 159 county emergency management agencies to develop local hazard mitigation plans, conduct disaster exercises, and manage the state's emergency alert system. Each Georgia county has a local emergency management director who coordinates with local fire, law enforcement, and public health agencies during disasters. Residents should register for their county's mass notification system to receive timely emergency alerts.

Know your evacuation routes before you need them. Georgia's major interstates serve as primary evacuation corridors: I-75 and I-85 northbound for metro Atlanta, I-95 northbound for coastal areas, and I-16 westbound from Savannah to Dublin. However, these highways can become gridlocked within hours of an evacuation order. Identify alternate routes using US highways and state roads, and keep paper maps in your vehicle. Pre-identify shelter locations, fuel stops, and supply points along your evacuation route. Our hurricane emergency kit guide covers evacuation planning in detail for coastal residents.

Georgia operates a network of emergency shelters managed by the American Red Cross and local government agencies. Pet owners should identify pet-friendly evacuation shelters in advance, as not all shelters accept animals. The Georgia Department of Transportation provides real-time traffic information during evacuations through the 511 system. Keep copies of all critical documents including identification, insurance policies, medical records, and prescriptions in a waterproof container in your go-bag. Program emergency contact numbers into every family member's phone and write them on a paper card stored in each person's emergency kit.

Urban Atlanta vs Rural Georgia Preparedness

Georgia's emergency preparedness needs vary dramatically between the Atlanta metropolitan area and rural communities across the rest of the state. Atlanta's 6 million residents face unique urban challenges including dense transportation networks that can become gridlocked during emergencies, aging infrastructure vulnerable to failure, and an urban heat island effect that intensifies summer temperatures. The city's position in a Piedmont valley means flash flooding can develop rapidly in urban streams and storm drains. High-rise residents must plan for building-specific emergencies including elevator failures, stairwell evacuation, and limited water pressure at upper floors.

Rural Georgia communities face different challenges. Emergency response times are longer, medical facilities may be farther away, and rural roads may be blocked by fallen trees or washed-out bridges after severe storms. Many rural homes depend on private wells that lose function during power outages, and propane or fuel oil heating systems require electricity to operate. Agricultural communities need to plan for livestock welfare, crop protection, and equipment backup power during extended emergencies. Maintain a larger water and food reserve in rural areas, invest in a generator, and establish communication plans with neighbors who can provide mutual aid.

North Georgia mountain communities face additional winter weather risks including ice storms and occasional heavy snowfall. Mountain roads may be impassable for days during ice events, and homes at higher elevations may lose power and water for extended periods. Keep chainsaws and tree-cutting equipment accessible for clearing blocked driveways and roads. Store extra heating fuel and firewood for wood-burning stoves and fireplaces. Regardless of whether you live in urban Atlanta or rural south Georgia, develop a written emergency plan, practice it with your family twice per year, and maintain your emergency kit on an ongoing basis. Frequently Asked Questions

How often do hurricanes affect Georgia?
Georgia's coast faces direct hurricane impacts on average every six to eight years, though indirect effects from tropical systems occur more frequently. The Atlantic hurricane season runs from June through November, with peak activity in August and September. Savannah, Brunswick, and the barrier islands have experienced significant hurricane damage multiple times in recent decades. Even storms that make landfall in Florida or the Carolinas can produce destructive winds, heavy rain, and dangerous storm surge along the Georgia coast.
Is tornado risk significant in Georgia?
Yes, Georgia experiences more tornadoes than many residents realize. South Georgia and central Georgia are particularly vulnerable, especially during late winter and spring when severe weather systems move through. Georgia averages over 25 tornadoes per year, with some producing significant damage. The 2008 Super Tuesday outbreak spawned multiple violent tornadoes across south Georgia. Tornadoes can occur in any month but peak from February through May. All Georgia households should include tornado preparedness in their emergency plans.
What makes Georgia's heat uniquely dangerous?
Georgia's combination of extreme heat and high humidity creates dangerously high heat index values that can exceed 110 degrees Fahrenheit during summer months. This humid heat makes it harder for the body to cool itself through sweating, significantly increasing the risk of heat exhaustion and heat stroke. High humidity also promotes mold growth during and after flooding events. Outdoor workers, the elderly, young children, and those without air conditioning face the highest risk. Georgia heat-related deaths spike during multi-day heat waves when overnight temperatures remain in the upper 70s or higher.
Does the Atlanta metro area face different risks than rural Georgia?
Atlanta faces unique urban emergency challenges including dense population evacuation difficulties, aging infrastructure vulnerabilities, and urban heat island effects that intensify summer heat. The 2014 ice storm stranded thousands of commuters on metro Atlanta highways, highlighting the region's transportation vulnerability. Rural south Georgia faces greater tornado and hurricane wind risk with longer emergency response times. Mountain communities in north Georgia face different winter weather including ice storms and rare snow events. Each region requires tailored preparedness.
What should Georgia residents know about evacuation routes?
Georgia's primary hurricane evacuation routes are I-75 northbound, I-85 northbound, I-95 northbound, and I-16 westbound from Savannah. These corridors become severely congested during mandatory evacuations. The Georgia Department of Transportation and emergency management agencies recommend evacuating early, as delays of 12 or more hours are common. Travelers should carry a full tank of fuel, extra water, non-perishable food, and essential medications. Register for the Georgia Emergency Management Agency's mass transit evacuation assistance if you lack personal transportation.
Do I need flood insurance in Georgia?
Flood insurance is strongly recommended for any Georgia property, even outside designated floodplains. Approximately 30 percent of Georgia flood claims come from properties outside high-risk zones. Flash flooding from tropical systems and severe thunderstorms can occur in any Georgia county. Standard homeowner and renter insurance policies do not cover flood damage. The National Flood Insurance Program offers policies through participating insurers. Consider purchasing coverage before hurricane season begins, as policies typically have a 30-day waiting period before taking effect.
