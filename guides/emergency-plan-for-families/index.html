Emergency Plan for Families: Complete Guide to Household Disaster Preparedness

Create a comprehensive emergency plan for your family. Covers communication, evacuation, shelter-in-place, and reunification strategies for any disaster.

Last updated: July 25, 2025 By EmergencyKitGuide Editorial Team

Why Every Family Needs a Written Emergency Plan

A written emergency plan transforms panic into action. When a disaster strikes, the human brain struggles to make rational decisions under extreme stress. Having a pre-established plan eliminates guesswork and ensures every family member knows exactly what to do, where to go, and who to contact. FEMA reports that families with documented emergency plans recover significantly faster than those without one, and the peace of mind that comes from preparation is invaluable long before any disaster occurs.

Your plan does not need to be a complex document. It should be clear, concise, and accessible to every member of the household, including children old enough to follow instructions. The key is covering four critical areas: how your family will communicate, where you will meet, when you will evacuate versus shelter in place, and how you will reunite if separated. This guide walks you through building each component with practical steps you can implement today. Pair your plan with a complete family 72-hour emergency kit for maximum readiness.

Family Emergency Meeting Plan

Your first step is establishing a family emergency meeting location. Designate a primary meeting spot close to your home, such as a neighbor's driveway, a specific tree in the front yard, or the end of your street. This location should be far enough from your house to be safe from fire or structural collapse but close enough that everyone can reach it on foot within minutes. Every family member must know this location by heart, including young children who can recognize visual landmarks even if they cannot read written addresses.

In addition to the primary location, designate a secondary meeting point outside your immediate neighborhood. If a flood, wildfire, or gas leak prevents access to your street, family members need somewhere else to gather. Popular choices include a nearby school, a place of worship, a public library, or a trusted friend or relative's home. Share both locations with every family member and write them on a card kept in each person's wallet and in the home emergency kit. Review and practice reaching both locations at least twice per year.

Building a Family Communication Tree

A communication tree is a structured sequence of phone calls that ensures every family member's status is relayed efficiently, even when cell networks are congested. Designate an out-of-area contact person who lives at least 100 miles away. After a disaster, every family member calls this person first to report their safety and location. This out-of-area contact then relays information to other family members who may not be able to reach each other directly. The reason for choosing someone far away is that long-distance phone lines often remain functional when local networks are overwhelmed with emergency traffic.

Design the tree so that each person is responsible for contacting the next person in the chain. For example, parent A calls the out-of-area contact, parent B calls parent A, child 1 calls parent B, and the babysitter or caregiver calls child 1. Program all numbers into every phone as ICE (In Case of Emergency) contacts and write them on laminated cards. Practice activating the tree quarterly and after any significant change such as a new phone number, a change in work schedule, or a child changing schools. School and Workplace Emergency Coordination

Children spend the majority of their waking hours at school, making it essential to understand your child's school emergency plan. Meet with school administrators to learn their evacuation procedures, reunification protocols, and shelter-in-place policies. Provide the school with emergency contact information for all authorized pickup persons and ensure your child knows who is allowed to take them from school during an emergency. Many schools now use reunification systems where parents must present identification and sign out their children, so keep your ID accessible and your pickup authorization list current.

For working adults, coordinate with your employer to understand the workplace emergency plan. Know the building evacuation routes, the designated assembly area, and whether your company has a continuity plan that allows remote work during extended emergencies. Update your employer with current emergency contact information at least annually. If you work in a high-rise building, learn the location of stairwells and do not use elevators during emergencies. Keep a small personal 72-hour survival kit at your workplace in case you cannot return home immediately after a disaster.

Evacuation Routes and Transportation Planning

Every family should identify at least three evacuation routes from their neighborhood. Map these routes on paper and drive each one so every licensed family member knows the turns by heart. Primary routes may become blocked by traffic, fallen trees, flooding, or emergency vehicles, so having alternatives prevents you from becoming trapped. Practice driving each route during different times of day so you understand traffic patterns and potential bottlenecks that could slow your evacuation during a real emergency.

Keep your vehicle's gas tank at least half full at all times as a general emergency preparedness practice. Store a basic car emergency kit in every family vehicle with water, snacks, a flashlight, a first aid kit, and basic tools. If your family does not own a vehicle, pre-arrange transportation with neighbors, friends, or family members who can include you in their evacuation plans. Public transit systems may also provide emergency evacuation services, and knowing the pickup locations in advance removes a critical variable during a crisis. Shelter-in-Place vs. Evacuation Decision Framework

Knowing when to stay and when to go is one of the most important decisions your family will make during an emergency. Shelter in place during chemical spills, airborne hazardous material incidents, severe winter storms, tornadoes, or situations where authorities advise staying indoors. To shelter effectively, move to an interior room away from windows, seal gaps under doors with wet towels, and turn off HVAC systems to prevent outside air from entering. Our home evacuation checklist provides detailed shelter-in-place procedures for various scenarios.

Evacuate during wildfires, hurricanes with storm surge warnings, widespread flooding, gas leaks, or any situation where your home's structural integrity is compromised. Establish clear trigger criteria before a disaster strikes so there is no debate when the moment arrives. For example, if authorities issue a mandatory evacuation order, you leave immediately. If you see smoke on the horizon during wildfire season, you grab your go-bags and leave. If water reaches your doorstep during a flood event, you evacuate to higher ground. Write these triggers into your plan and ensure every family member understands that these conditions require immediate action without waiting for consensus.

Neighborhood Coordination and Community Preparedness

Your neighbors are your first responders in many emergencies. Before first responders can arrive, it is often the people living next door who pull you from rubble, extinguish a small fire, or call for help when you are injured. Get to know your neighbors and discuss emergency preparedness openly. Share your evacuation plan and ask about theirs. Identify neighbors with specialized skills such as medical training, military experience, or construction knowledge who could provide critical assistance during a crisis.

Consider forming a neighborhood emergency response team. This does not require formal training or organization. Simply create a shared contact list, agree on a neighborhood meeting point, and designate someone to check on elderly or disabled residents during an emergency. Community emergency response team (CERT) training offered by FEMA is free and equips volunteers with basic disaster response skills. Strong neighborhood coordination multiplies your family's safety net far beyond what any single household can achieve alone. Pair neighborhood efforts with a comprehensive disaster preparedness plan for a layered approach to safety.

Reunification Procedures When the Family Is Separated

Disasters do not wait for families to be together. Parents may be at work, children at school, and teens out with friends when an emergency strikes. A reunification plan specifies exactly where each person goes and how they contact the family once they are safe. Start by assigning each family member their own responsibility: the youngest children report to the nearest fire station or school office, teenagers call the out-of-area contact, and adults make their way to the primary or secondary meeting point.

Practice reunification scenarios with your family so that children understand what to do even when a parent is not present. Teach young children how to identify safe adults such as police officers, firefighters, or teachers and how to state their name, address, and parent's phone number. Keep a recent family photo in each child's emergency kit so authorities can help identify them if needed. Update reunification procedures every six months and share them with babysitters, nannies, grandparents, and anyone else who regularly cares for your children. Children's Emergency Education and Practice Drills

Teaching children about emergencies does not have to be frightening. Use age-appropriate language and frame emergency preparedness as an empowering skill rather than a source of fear. For toddlers and preschoolers, practice fire escape routes as a game and teach them to stop, drop, and roll. For elementary-age children, conduct earthquake drills, practice calling 911, and review the family meeting locations. For teenagers, involve them in planning discussions, teach basic first aid, and assign them responsibilities such as grabbing the pet emergency kit or helping younger siblings.

Conduct full practice drills at least twice per year. Time your family's evacuation from the house to the primary meeting point and work to improve your speed. Practice communication tree activation by having each member call the designated contact in sequence. Role-play scenarios such as a fire at night, an earthquake during dinner, or being separated at a shopping mall. Children who have practiced emergency responses are far less likely to freeze or panic during a real event. Document lessons learned from each drill and update your plan accordingly. Special Needs Family Members and Pet Emergency Planning

Families with members who have disabilities, chronic medical conditions, or mobility limitations require tailored emergency plans. Register with your county's special needs registry so emergency managers know to prioritize your household. Pre-arrange accessible transportation if standard vehicles cannot accommodate wheelchairs, walkers, or other mobility devices. Ensure that all caregivers, including babysitters and after-school program staff, understand the individual's specific needs and have copies of necessary medical information. Pack a dedicated emergency kit for the individual with extra medication, backup batteries for medical devices, and written care instructions.

Pets are family members who also need emergency planning. Identify pet-friendly evacuation shelters and hotels along your evacuation routes before an emergency occurs. Pack a pet emergency kit with at least three days of food and water, medications, vaccination records, a leash, a carrier, and a comfort item. Ensure your pet has current ID tags and is microchipped with up-to-date contact information. Some disasters move too fast for pet retrieval, so practice loading your pet into their carrier quickly and keep carriers accessible rather than stored in hard-to-reach locations. Our wildfire preparedness guide includes detailed pet evacuation procedures for families in fire-prone areas.

Updating and Maintaining Your Family Emergency Plan

An emergency plan is a living document that must evolve with your family's changing circumstances. Schedule a formal review every six months, coinciding with daylight saving time changes as a convenient reminder. During each review, verify that all contact numbers are current, confirm that meeting locations are still accessible, and assess whether evacuation routes need adjustment due to new construction or road changes. Update medical information for any family member whose prescriptions or health conditions have changed.

After any drill or real emergency, debrief as a family. Discuss what went well, what caused confusion, and what needs improvement. Incorporate lessons learned into the updated plan. As children grow older, increase their responsibilities and involve them more deeply in planning discussions. Store your updated plan in multiple locations: a printed copy in the home emergency kit, a digital copy in cloud storage, a copy on each parent's phone, and a copy in each child's school backpack. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the first step in creating a family emergency plan?
The first step is conducting a family meeting where every member discusses potential hazards in your area, such as floods, tornadoes, earthquakes, or wildfires. Identify which scenarios are most likely based on your geographic location and create specific response protocols for each one. Establish a primary meeting location near your home and a secondary location outside your neighborhood in case the primary site is inaccessible.
How do I design a family communication tree?
Start by designating an out-of-area contact person who lives at least 100 miles away. Every family member calls this person first after a disaster to relay their status. Next, create a tiered contact list where person A calls person B, person B calls person C, and so on. Write the tree on a laminated card and program all numbers into every family member's phone as ICE contacts. Practice the tree quarterly to ensure everyone knows their role.
How do I prepare my children for emergencies?
Teach children how to call 911 and recite their full name, address, and parent phone numbers. Practice fire escape routes twice a year and conduct earthquake drop-cover-hold drills monthly. Use age-appropriate language to explain disasters without causing unnecessary fear. Create a comfort kit for each child with a small toy, a family photo, and a handwritten note from parents. Role-play scenarios so children build muscle memory for emergency responses.
How often should our family practice the emergency plan?
Conduct full evacuation drills at least twice per year, once during daytime and once at night. Practice communication tree activation quarterly and review contact information monthly. Update your plan every six months or whenever there is a significant change such as a new family member, a move to a new home, or children changing schools. Seasonal reviews before hurricane or wildfire season are especially important.
How do I create an emergency plan for a family member with special needs?
Identify all medical equipment, medications, and caregiver requirements for the individual. Register with your local emergency management agency's special needs registry. Pre-arrange transportation if the person cannot use standard evacuation routes. Pack a dedicated supply kit with extra medication, backup power for medical devices, and copies of medical records. Establish relationships with neighbors who can assist during an evacuation and ensure they understand the person's specific needs.
Should I have both a shelter-in-place and evacuation plan?
Yes, every family needs both plans and a clear decision framework for choosing between them. Shelter in place during chemical spills, severe winter storms, or situations where roads are blocked. Evacuate during wildfires, floods, hurricanes, or structural damage. Establish specific trigger criteria such as mandatory evacuation orders, rising water levels, or visible smoke that prompt immediate evacuation without debate.
What is a family reunification plan?
A reunification plan specifies where and how family members will reconnect if separated during a disaster. Choose a primary reunification point near home and a secondary point outside the neighborhood. Teach children to report to the nearest fire station or school if they cannot reach either point. Keep copies of the reunification plan in every family member's emergency kit and share it with schools, workplaces, and caregivers.
